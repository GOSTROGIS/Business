Teaching Adaptability: preserve the purpose while revising the approach

Adaptability becomes visible when a learner notices a change, considers its consequences and chooses a workable response. It may involve continuing, changing a method, asking for support or revising a commitment. Teach these decisions explicitly. A calm appearance does not prove that a learner has understood a changed task, and visible frustration does not show an inability to adapt.

The learner chapter uses a fictional learning folder, reduced device access and feedback on a project note. It provides changes that can be discussed without asking learners to recount distress, employment conflict or family responsibilities. In an ACCESS IS4S classroom, acknowledge that people already navigate changing circumstances. Do not turn that recognition into an expectation that individuals must overcome inaccessible systems without support.

Keep the six components distinct

The source names responsibility; persistence and perseverance; regulating emotions when appropriate; setting or adjusting goals; planning and prioritizing; and seeking improvement (SkillPlan, 2024, p. 99). Teach responsibility as an honest account of commitments and evidence. Teach persistence as purposeful continued effort that can include changing strategy. Emotional regulation here concerns a learner-selected action that supports the task; it is not an assessment of feelings or personality.

AD-01–AD-03 establish purpose and a plan. AD-04–AD-06 offer optional response strategies and boundaries. AD-07–AD-09 examine persistence. AD-10–AD-12 revise a goal and communicate the arrangement. AD-13–AD-15 use feedback and prepare for another change. AD-16–AD-18 test a strategy and assemble evidence across all components. Numeracy supports time allocation, Reading supports interpreting updates, and Communication makes changed commitments clear.

Pair a growth orientation with useful support

The first source strategy promotes a growth mindset through feedback and opportunities to improve (p. 100). Make the pathway to improvement concrete: identify a feature in the current work, demonstrate a strategy and allow another attempt. Avoid telling a learner that effort alone will remove every barrier. If device time disappears, the learning arrangement needs to change; the learner cannot solve that shortage simply by believing more strongly.

Use before-and-after work samples. Ask which action made a difference and what support was used. Praise a useful decision rather than a fixed trait: “You checked the owner against the brief and corrected the note” gives more guidance than “You are adaptable.” Offer accessible formats and different ways to show the target skill. A choice that enables a learner to act is part of good teaching, not a reduction in the learning goal.

Offer strategies for attention and response by choice

The second strategy concerns focus and emotional regulation (p. 101). Establish a short menu: reread the written change, ask for one point at a time, use a checklist, request a brief pause or seek private clarification. Model a task-focused message such as, “I need five minutes to compare the versions; then I will confirm the next step.” Learners can practise with an invented change in AD-05.

Do not require closing eyes, breathing exercises, personal disclosures or a cultural practice. These may be meaningful choices for some people, but they are not universal prerequisites for adaptability. Keep instructional support within your role and follow the program's established support arrangements when a learner asks for help beyond teaching. The observation is the chosen action and its usefulness, not whether someone appeared sufficiently relaxed.

AD-06 adds an essential boundary: a request to skip a source check does not authorize a false completion claim. Practise a response that states what can be done, what remains pending and a reasonable next step. Adaptability includes protecting the integrity of the work.

A worked teaching example: revise the folder plan

Begin with Case document 2. Thursday shrinks from 90 minutes to 60, and device access is limited to the final 25 minutes. The original digital allocation was 35 minutes. Model three columns: changed conditions, unchanged outcomes and decisions to confirm. The 30-minute reduction affects the work period; the 10-minute shortfall concerns device time. Those are related but different quantities.

The note can be drafted from checked paper information before the device is available. A workable plan uses the first 35 minutes for a 25-minute draft and a 10-minute source review, then the final 25 minutes for the agreed digital actions. The offered Monday appointment supplies another 10 minutes. The instructor has agreed a Monday completion arrangement for the digital evidence; the Friday review can remain partial.

Ask learners what this plan does and does not demonstrate. It shows planning, but actual device actions still require observation. A paper plan cannot establish saving and reopening a file. Have pairs use AD-11 to propose a schedule, then AD-12 to communicate the commitment and check the listener's understanding. Accept different workable sequences when they respect the stated conditions.

Vary situations and make reflection actionable

The third strategy develops flexibility through varied scenarios, practice and adaptive leadership (p. 102). Introduce one manageable change at a time: a different file layout, revised information or an unavailable resource. Give enough information to reason and a route to clarify what remains unknown. Avoid surprise pressure as a proxy for authentic learning.

In AD-13–AD-14, feedback identifies specific errors: the owner and contractor are confused, funding is called profit, requirements are overgeneralized and unfinished digital work is described as complete. Learners convert the feedback into actions, revise the note and ask a reader to check the new meaning. The useful lesson is that revision can preserve the concise format while changing unsupported claims. AD-15 then transfers the approach to a different computer arrangement.

Observe development over time

Assessment needs several sources of evidence and repeated opportunities (p. 103). Keep an original goal, changed condition, chosen response, support, result and next step. Self-reflection is useful alongside observation, not as a substitute for it. Increase complexity through multiple changes, competing priorities or a less familiar context, while keeping access and expectations clear. Do not grade optimism, emotional display or willingness to tolerate unfair conditions.

The three source grab-and-go patterns are goal-setting with reflection, optional refocusing practice, and adapting instruction to learners' needs (p. 105). AD-02/AD-17, AD-05 and the choices throughout this chapter support these patterns. Offer specific, checkable goals with meaningful timeframes, but revise them when circumstances warrant. Adapt teaching from individual evidence rather than demographic assumptions. Preview the resource leads on p. 104 before using them; the source's developmental levels are not an official classroom rating scale.

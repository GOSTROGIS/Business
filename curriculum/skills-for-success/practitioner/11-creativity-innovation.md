# Teaching Creativity and Innovation: develop an idea through use and feedback

Creativity can begin with an ordinary difficulty: a label is unclear, materials are hard to find or a schedule does not answer a reader's question. Innovation involves putting an idea into use and learning from the result. Teach learners to notice an opportunity, generate alternatives, develop a small version, test it and revise. A polished object alone cannot show that process.

The learner chapter uses a fictional practice-card shelf. Learners can change labels, organization and classroom checking steps using simple materials. The setting allows useful experimentation without changing equipment procedures or drawing on protected knowledge. In an ACCESS IS4S classroom, welcome varied ways of making and explaining while leaving ownership with the learner. No one needs to produce a cultural design or disclose personal knowledge to demonstrate creativity.

## Make all six components visible

The source identifies imagination and curiosity; identifying opportunities; generating ideas novel to oneself or others; developing ideas; applying ideas; and facilitating a creative environment (SkillPlan, 2024, p. 106). Creativity does not require a world-first invention. An idea can be new to a learner or useful in a particular setting. Teach its value through the task it helps someone perform.

CI-01–CI-04 investigate and frame the opportunity. CI-05–CI-07 generate alternatives and challenge assumptions. CI-08–CI-10 compare features and develop a prototype. CI-11–CI-15 test, revise and make limited claims. CI-16–CI-18 facilitate, transfer and collect evidence. Document Use appears in labels, contents lists and version records. Problem Solving structures inquiry, Collaboration supports feedback, and Digital can provide another making tool when it suits the task.

## Connect creativity with a real purpose

The first source strategy uses practical applications and visible products (pp. 107–108). Begin by asking whose task could become easier. The shelf case supplies waiting-time observations but does not establish their cause. Have learners separate what they know from what they would need to ask or observe. A design question should leave room for alternatives rather than hide a preferred solution inside its wording.

CI-02 asks, “How could we help a learner…” A useful completion might focus on finding and checking the Number Check kit without remembering its contents. “Make everyone use a better folder” selects a tool too early. Compare these questions and ask what each allows the learner to investigate. Use fictional viewpoints in CI-03 so that nobody must represent a disability, identity or entire community.

Keep a visible brief with requirements: accurate contents, plain language, black-and-white usability, no personal data and manageable updating. A striking design that ignores those conditions is not ready to use. Constraints can help learners make choices, provided the limits are explicit and leave meaningful room to think.

## Separate idea generation from selection

The second strategy teaches a structured creative problem-solving process (p. 109). Allow a period of divergent thinking before comparing options. Learners can sketch, speak, write, arrange materials or use an accessible tool. Ask for genuinely different approaches, such as a contents-list folder, matching board and return tray, rather than three colours of the same design.

During generation, invite clarifying questions and possible extensions. During selection, compare ideas against the brief and investigate uncertainties. “No criticism” is a temporary protection for early possibilities, not a ban on checking facts or feasibility. Teach the change between phases explicitly. A table can help organize criteria, but guessed scores do not make a decision objective. Label predictions, and revisit them after a test.

Use CI-06 to practise supporting another person's unfinished idea. The listener asks a question and offers an extension; the author decides what to use. Credit the contribution with permission. This routine helps prevent a confident partner from taking over the work while believing they are helping.

## A worked teaching example: a testable contents system

Use the Number Check kit. Version 1 contains one data table, one worked example and two blank record sheets: four items. Model a large folder label, a contents list and a “check needed” slip. State the intended user action: find the kit, check its contents and report a missing item. This creates a small prototype that can be tested before investing in a finished design.

The fictional test record says a user finds the folder and counts the items, but misses the slip instruction because it is on the reverse. Another user asks what to do with used sheets. Model three columns: observation, possible explanation and next check. Do not write “the user did not pay attention.” The design has hidden information and an unanswered process question.

The instructor clarifies that used sheets stay with the learner and two blanks are restocked. The kit also gains a second worked example, bringing it to five items. In CI-14, revise the visible instruction, mark the slip location, update the contents and identify Version 2. Ask why each change is needed. In CI-15, retest with a willing user and record support. A successful small test supports a limited claim about that trial, not a promise that the system works for everyone.

## Build an environment where ideas can develop

The third strategy concerns a supportive and inclusive creative environment (p. 110). Provide time, materials, accessible participation routes and permission to offer an unfinished thought. Make feedback specific to the purpose. Ask what the author intended before recommending a change. Keep accurate source information fixed while allowing different ways to present it.

Discuss ownership before photographing, displaying, publishing or combining work. Learners may choose to keep an idea private. Nation-specific knowledge, imagery and teachings require guidance from the appropriate rights holders; a general invitation to be creative does not supply permission. CI-17 makes these boundaries part of transfer to a fictional community-room or workplace task. The instructor should model asking whose approval a real change would require.

## Assess process, product and contribution

The source recommends varied evidence and reflection (p. 111). Keep the design question, alternatives, comparison, prototype, test notes and revision. Assess whether the learner explored possibilities, developed details, used evidence and supported others. Fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration can prompt discussion, but classroom observations are not standardized creativity-test scores. Avoid grading drawing polish, cultural expression or the number of ideas alone.

Increase complexity through more user needs, changing requirements, less obvious opportunities or a wider set of constraints. Offer a partially completed prototype or one-kit starting point when needed. Record the support and use another task to see what transfers. A plan for future use is valuable but remains a plan until an actual application is observed.

The source's three grab-and-go patterns are brainstorming in varied forms, working through an open problem with a creative-process diagram, and building a portfolio (p. 113). CI-05/CI-16, the full shelf sequence and CI-18 respectively retain those purposes. A portfolio should be shared by agreement. Preview the resource leads on p. 112 before using them; they are not independently verified recommendations in this chapter.

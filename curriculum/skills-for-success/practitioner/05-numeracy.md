# Teaching Numeracy: connect the question, the quantities and the decision

Numeracy develops through useful questions and visible reasoning. A learner may calculate accurately but choose the wrong quantity; another may understand the relationship and need help recording it. Start by finding out what the learner already does with numbers, then distinguish reading the problem, choosing a method, calculating and interpreting. The teaching response should address the point of difficulty rather than repeat the whole task unchanged.

The learner chapter offers fictional supply orders, journey planning and an information-board layout. These connect with training, employment and community tasks in British Columbia while keeping personal finances and private circumstances outside the exercise. Learners may bring another non-sensitive context if they wish. Recognize experience in estimating, sharing quantities, planning time and checking materials without assuming that every learner has the same experience.

## Use the complete numeracy cycle

The six components are identifying the task; identifying mathematical information; connecting information; applying operations and tools; interpreting and evaluating; and sharing results and implications (SkillPlan, 2024, p. 62). Keep all six visible. A worksheet that ends at a calculator answer leaves interpretation and communication unobserved. Ask what the answer means, whether it is plausible and what another person needs to know before using it.

NU-01–NU-06 develop this cycle through quantities, stock, packs and budget conditions. NU-07–NU-10 use time and fares. NU-11–NU-16 distinguish units, area, perimeter, fit and data displays. NU-17–NU-18 require transfer and a numerical handover. Tables, graphs and diagrams integrate Document Use throughout (p. 63). Correct a source labelling error when planning: component 6 concerns sharing results; it is not another instance of choosing operations.

## Attend to confidence and the learning conditions

The first source strategy addresses attitudes and emotions around mathematics (pp. 64–65). Invite a private statement of what would make practice easier. Do not ask for public accounts of school failure or turn confidence into a score. Predictable routines, time to think, worked examples, useful tools and specific feedback can make a task more approachable. Offer counters, sketches, enlarged tables or spoken explanation while preserving the adult purpose.

Avoid praising speed as evidence of understanding. Ask a learner who answers quickly to explain the units and check a condition. Give another learner time to draw the relationship. Both may demonstrate strong reasoning. If a learner says “I cannot do maths,” respond with an observable starting point: “You identified what the cupboard already contains. Let us connect that to the amount needed.” This locates something teachable without making an unsupported promise about progress.

## Make context support the mathematics

The second strategy uses meaningful contexts and explicitly teaches missing foundations (pp. 65–66). Before introducing a formula, establish what is being counted or measured. Write units beside quantities. Ask for an estimate before exact calculation and a second check afterwards. A calculator is appropriate when it supports the intended task; show how to detect a mistaken input or an implausible result.

Context can also overload a learner. Separate a long paragraph into a quantity table while retaining its conditions. Practise one foundational relationship, then return to the full decision. Do not replace every task with isolated arithmetic: the return to context shows whether the learner can choose and use the method. When changing a scenario, distinguish invented assumptions from real workplace specifications. These lessons do not establish material tolerances, equipment capacities or operating instructions.

## A worked teaching example: whole packs

Use the pen order in NU-03. Eighteen learners need two pens each, and the group wants four spares. The target is `18 × 2 + 4 = 40 pens`. Nine usable pens are already available, so the additional requirement is `40 − 9 = 31 pens`. Each pack contains 12 pens. Two packs provide only 24, while three provide 36; therefore the order needs three whole packs.

Continue beyond the purchase count. At $6.60 per pack, the pens cost `3 × $6.60 = $19.80`. Existing stock plus the purchase gives `9 + 36 = 45 pens`, leaving five beyond the target. Check by estimation: three packs at about $7 should cost about $21, close to $19.80. Ask why rounding 31 ÷ 12 down would fail. The answer is tied to complete packs and sufficient supply, not a universal instruction to round every result upward.

Model the calculation once, then give pairs the folder or label row. One person explains the relationship; the other checks quantities and units. Swap roles before using NU-04 to combine the order and delivery condition. A correct line item does not yet answer whether the whole purchase fits the budget.

## Structure collaboration so everyone reasons

The third strategy uses collaborative learning (pp. 66–67). Give partners a purposeful difference in responsibility: proposer and checker, estimator and calculator, or diagram-maker and explainer. Rotate roles. Ask for two methods where they illuminate the same relationship, such as a timeline and subtraction for elapsed time. Invite disagreement about a method using the quantities and assumptions, not a judgement about the person.

A group answer alone cannot show each learner's numeracy. After discussion, ask everyone to solve a small variation or explain one decision. Offer a private instructor conversation when public explanation is a barrier. Link the handover in NU-18 to Communication and Writing, but assess those demands separately if they obscure the mathematical evidence.

## Assessment, complexity and next practice

Use diagnosis, observation, work samples and a retry after feedback (pp. 67–68). Record whether difficulty arose in locating data, translating the situation, choosing operations, calculating or interpreting. Increase complexity through more steps, less obvious information, different representations or additional conditions. Avoid increasing all of these at once. Digital navigation and reading demands can conceal numerical understanding; provide another route when the aim is numeracy.

The source's three grab-and-go patterns are measuring with an imperial tape and fractions, calculating volume or mass from supplied data, and working with percentages and graphs (p. 70). NU-14 supports unit-system reading and NU-15 combines charting with a fraction and percentage. For physical measurement, use a ruler or instructor-handled tape on a safe classroom object. Locate zero, identify the smallest marked fraction and compare two readings; never measure a resized illustration as if it were full scale.

For the volume-to-mass pattern, provide this additional fictional calculation: a rectangular box has inside dimensions `0.5 m × 0.4 m × 0.2 m`. Its volume is `0.04 m³`. Assume an invented practice material has a mass of `500 kg per m³`; the stated relationship gives `0.04 m³ × 500 kg/m³ = 20 kg`. Check: the box holds one twenty-fifth of a cubic metre, so its contents have one twenty-fifth of 500 kg. Ask learners to explain why adding the three lengths would not find volume, then halve the depth: volume becomes `0.02 m³` and mass `10 kg`. These are calculation assumptions, not asphalt properties, handling advice or equipment limits. The resource page, p. 69, supplies further leads to preview.

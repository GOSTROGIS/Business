13.1 HMA Overlays over

Asphalt Pavements

The most popular method of pavement rehabilitation for both hot mix
asphalt (HMA) pavements and Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements
is the asphalt overlay. This chapter discusses the rehabilitation of HMA
pavements, and Chapter 14, “Rehabilitation of Concrete Pavements with
HMA” covers the use of HMA overlays for PCC pavements. Other options
for resurfacing, such as surface treatments, are discussed in Chapter 12,
“Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance”. Properly designed and constructed,
an asphalt overlay is a cost-effective method of correcting surface deficien-
cies, restoring user satisfaction, and increasing the load-carrying capacity
of the roadway.

The first step in developing an asphalt pavement rehabilitation project
is to conduct a detailed project-level pavement evaluation using the proce-
dures detailed in Chapter 11, “Pavement Management.” This information is
needed for identifying available rehabilitation alternatives and for design-
ing the project. In addition, the designer needs data to assess the structural
adequacy of the pavement. This data is collected by sampling and testing
the materials in the pavement, by conducting in situ testing, or by conduct-
ing deflection testing. In some situations, the project’s physical constraints
may require pavement reconstruction rather than overlay.

The three purposes for overlaying an asphalt pavement are the following:

° Heavy structural overlay. A heavy structural overlay is more than
5 inches (125 millimeters) thick. These overlays considerably improve
the load-bearing capacity of the pavement and usually have the same
design period as a newly constructed pavement.

¢ Structural overlay. A structural overlay is 3-5 inch (75-125 millime-
ters) thick. These overlays add strength to an old pavement and last
longer than functional or maintenance overlays.

° Functional or maintenance overlay. A functional overlay is 0.75-3
inches (20-75 millimeters) thick. Functional overlays are intended
to address ride quality, surface friction or other minor problems for
pavements that are structurally sound. Thin HMA overlays are also
a popular choice for preventative maintenance. A functional overlay
may be placed after the surface has been cold milled to replace the
material that was removed. This technique is commonly called “mill
and fill,” or referred to as an inlay.

The pavement designer has a number of options available for rehabili-
tating an existing HMA pavement. There is a point at which the extent of

full-depth recycle the pavement rather than to place a thick overlay. Practi-

For example, poor drainage coupled with a poor base and/or subbase

or full-depth recycling may be the desired alternative when serious dura-

being overlaid. The problem layer must be corrected where stripping, or
moisture damage, is apparent. In a majority of cases, the most realistic
approach to this is removal and replacement.

In selecting the proper rehabilitation alternative, a number of items need
to be considered. The goal is to choose the most cost-effective solution for
a particular problem. A discussion of these items follows. Table 13.1 gives
some general guidelines related to the causes and solutions for pavement

Pavement Condition

Pavement condition is the most important consideration when deciding
which rehabilitation process to use. A discussion of the different pave-
ment distress types and mechanisms is provided in Chapter 11, “Pave-
ment Management,” and MS-17, Asphalt Overlays for Highway and Street
Rehabilitation. A pavement in good condition—little surface distress and
no evidence of any structural failure problems—is a good candidate for
a functional rehabilitation, which might include a surface treatment, mill
and fill overlay or hot-in-place recycling. A pavement in poor to failed
condition requires an extensive rehabilitation, which might include cold-
in-place recycling or full-depth reclamation prior to an overlay.

Construction Feasibility

An HMA overlay requires less traffic control than a reconstruction
project because in most cases the overlay can be placed one lane at a time.
But some pre-overlay techniques, such as full-depth reclamation, require
that the roadway be closed or that its usage be significantly restricted.
Therefore, some techniques in urban areas or on mountainous roadways
with limited width may not be appropriate. In some situations, the lack
of specific equipment in the project locality may limit the feasibility of a
particular rehabilitation method. For example, it may not be economically
feasible to mobilize specific equipment for a small project, such as a hot-
in-place or cold-in-place recycling train. Overlay placement that increases
the thickness of the existing pavement has the disadvantage of decreasing
overhead clearances under bridges or utilities. Also, the overlay placement
in an urban area may cause loss of curb reveal or problems at adjoining
driveways and intersections. Any possible clearance problems must be
considered in the initial design, and steps must be taken to solve them.
Possible solutions are to reconstruct the section under the bridge or utility
or to mill the existing roadway below the level of the curb line and cross
streets. These steps must be taken before placing the overlay.

Performance
A number of factors can lead to diminished asphalt overlay performance,
including the following:
¢ Insufficient overlay thickness
° The use of the wrong type of HMA surface for the overlay
° Insufficient preparation of the existing pavement before the overlay
° Insufficient consideration of the potential for reflection cracking

Overlay Thickness

The overlay thickness must be designed for the anticipated traffic
over the design period. It should be designed using the principles and
procedures discussed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design.” The selection of
the proper material for the overlay is discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix
Asphalt CHMA) Design.”

Pre-overlay Preparation

Overlay performance is influenced by the amount, type, and severity of
distress in the existing pavement. Extensive repair of the existing pavement
will maximize the performance of the overlay. For example, replacing all
areas that have medium-to-severe alligator cracking and improving all
areas with poor drainage on a project will significantly increase the life
of a pavement rehabilitation project. This is one approach to pre-overlay
preparation. The other approach is to place an overlay of sufficient thick-
ness to protect all the weak areas in the existing pavement structure. If
an existing base or subbase layer has deteriorated, the designer increases
the thickness of the overlay to account for the decreased strength of the
underlying layers, instead of removing that layer. As the overlay thickness
is increased, the applied loads are distributed over a larger area in the
lower layers, thereby decreasing the stresses and deflections on the weak
layer. The thickness required to adequately protect weak layers usually
makes this approach economically unfeasible. Figure 13.1 shows the total
repair cost and the total overlay cost plotted against the percentage of
area repaired. When the percentage of the total area repaired increases,
the cost of the overlay will decrease. But when these two costs are added
together, the total cost of the project reaches a point at which it is mini-
mized. When the total cost begins to increase relative to the percentage
of the area repaired, the designer should consider reconstructing or using
full-depth reclamation as more cost-effective design alternatives.

Reflection Cracking Control

Reflection cracking is the appearance of the pattern of joints or cracks
in the old pavement at the surface of an overlay, and can be a serious
problem to consider when choosing an approach for asphalt pavement
rehabilitation. A complete discussion of the mechanism of reflection crack-
ing in overlays of PCC pavement is provided in Chapter 14, “Rehabilitation

13.2 Preparing Pavements
for Overlays

13.2.1 Local Weak Areas (Patching)

Any structurally inadequate areas should be removed and replaced using
proper patching techniques before placing the new overlay. Detailed visual
inspection should be used to identify any load-associated distress such as
alligator cracking. “Proofrolling,” or observing the amount and the extent of
deformation in the existing pavement while driving a loaded truck over the
pavement surface, is a very quick and simple method of defining the structur-
ally inadequate areas. These areas should be removed and patched following
the procedures detailed in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance.”

13.2.2 Leveling Courses

If milling is not used to correct surface distresses, the placement of
one or more leveling courses may be required to restore proper line and
cross-section. A leveling course can be either a scratch course or a wedge
course. Both are designed to fill in low spots on the existing pavement
surface and to restore cross-slope.

Scratch courses can be placed with either a paving machine or a motor
grader (Figure 13.2). They are thin courses of HMA used to fill in small
undulations in the pavement surface. The HMA mix used for a scratch
course generally has a small nominal maximum particle size (many times
a sand mix or a 98 inch [4.75 millimeters] mix). Care should be taken that
the aggregate in the mix used in a scratch coat not be so large that drag-
ging of the mix with the motor grader or the paving machine results.

Leveling wedges are courses of HMA used to level depressions or correct
cross-slope issues (for example, adding superelevation to an otherwise flat
horizon curve) in an old pavement before additional layers of HMA are
placed. Leveling wedges should be placed in lifts of not more than 3 inches
(75 millmeters). Figure 13.3 illustrates several design considerations for
constructing leveling wedges. The HMA can be placed with either a motor
grader or a paving machine. Care should be taken in choosing the mix for
a leveling course. The thickness of the leveling course should be at least
three times the nominal maximum particle size for the mix being used.

weak pavement structure or inadequate materials, should be repaired or

are usually wider at the surface and milling off 1-2 inches of the surface
can result in having much narrower cracks below the new overlay. These
cracks are sealed with the tack coat during the construction process. A
detailed discussion of crack sealing/filling is in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in
Pavement Maintenance.”

13.2.4 Widening

Pavement widening includes adding paved shoulders to an existing
pavement or adding additional lanes. The thickness for the widened section
should be designed and constructed to match the existing pavement plus the
overlay pavement. The structural number (see Chapter 6 “Thickness Design”)
should be equal or exceed the structural number of the mainline pavement.
The materials and the construction procedures used for widening are similar
to those used for new HMA pavement construction. A geotechnical engineer
should be consulted to evaluate the need for a subsurface longitudinal drain-
age system, and, if necessary, design one.

When widening a pavement, it is important to ensure that the additional
pavement is tied to the existing pavement. This can be done by making
a longitudinal saw cut along the existing pavement and then applying a

13.2.5 Drainage

If drainage problems are a contributing factor to the need for the overlay,
they must be addressed. A frequent cause of failure is inadequate pavement
and subgrade drainage. An assessment of the drainage-related pavement
damage is critical in the development of the overlay design. Correcting a
drainage problem can be as simple as cleaning or deepening the side ditches
to permit rapid runoff of surface water, or the correction may be as complex
as installing a longitudinal drainage system. Chapter 5, “Soils and Drainage
for Asphalt Pavements,” contains a discussion of the design and construction
of a longitudinal drainage system.

13.2.6 Interlayer Techniques

Function of an interlayer
An interlayer is a layer of material placed between an existing pavement
(either HMA or PCC) and the overlay. The purpose of the interlayer is to
minimize the reflection cracking in an overlay. Reflection cracking is the
result of horizontal and vertical movements in the cracks in the underly-
ing asphalt pavement. Reflection cracking results in increased roughness
and infiltration of surface water into the underlying layers. This increase
in moisture results in a weakening of the support for the pavement. The
use of an interlayer will not always prevent reflection cracking, but it has
been found that the proper use of an interlayer will:
° Retard the occurrence of the reflection cracking (delay the onset of
the cracking)
° Reduce the severity of the crack when it does occur
¢ Provide a waterproofing membrane for the underlying layers
° Reinforce the asphalt overlay to reduce horizontal stresses in the
asphalt layer

Geotextiles

Woven or non-woven synthetic geotextiles made of polypropylene, poly-
ester, fiberglass, nylon, and combinations of these are used as interlayers for
HMA pavements. There are many of these products on the market, and the
engineer is encouraged to contract the local DOT and various suppliers for
information on a specific product. They are placed directly on the existing
pavement surface after a heavy tack coat is applied. The geotextile is rolled
or brushed into the tack coat and an overlay is placed on top of the geotex-
tile. Many agencies place the geotextile within the overlay. This requires two
lifts of HMA and a minimum overlay thickness of 3 inches (75 millimeters).

After surface preparation is complete, an asphalt membrane is applied
using a calibrated distributor truck. See Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement
Maintenance” for a discussion of the proper operation of the distributor.
Selection of the proper type and application rate, along with a uniform
distribution of the membrane, is essential to the performance of the inter-
layer and thus the overlay. The manufacturer’s recommendations should be
carefully followed regarding the type and amount of asphalt that should
be applied with a specific geotextile.

The quantity of asphalt binder applied varies with the type and supplier
of the geotextile. The amount of tack coat applied also depends on the
condition of the underlying surface. An aged, weathered surface requires
a higher application rate than a new HMA leveling course. It is essential
to apply the membrane uniformly across the surface. A common range

should be placed over a sufficient length such that the distributor can place
a uniform quantity. Care should be taken to avoid placing the membrane
too far ahead of the paving process. Small, variable-width areas should
be applied with the hand spray boom on the back of the distributor to
ensure proper coverage. Before placing the membrane, the surface should
be swept to remove debris. If weather or equipment breakdown cause
paving operations to be suspended, the surface should not be opened to
traffic because of safety concerns. Uncovered geotextile can have very low
skid resistance.

Paving Nembranes

Several manufacturers produce a composite membrane that consists
of a paving geotextile saturated with asphalt and coated with a rubber-
ized asphalt adhesive. The top surface is saturated with asphalt cement.
Beneath the geotextile is a second layer—rubberized asphalt adhesive.
The adhesive is protected by a release sheet that is removed just before
placement. The paving membranes are basically a peel-and-stick type of
system. It may be necessary to apply a primer to improve adhesion before
placement of the membrane. This step is needed whenever problems with
adhesion may develop, such as on a surface that is wet or dusty, where
temperatures are cool, or on an irregular surface (for example, a milled
surface). The prime coat should always be used before installing a pav-
ing membrane on a bridge deck or when the paving membrane will be
exposed to moisture combined with traffic before placement of the over-
lay. Before a paving membrane is installed, the manufacturer should be
consulted for proper installation procedures.

Strip interlayers

Strip interlayers (Figure 13.5) are membrane materials designed to repair
localized pavement cracking and pavement joints. As with surface-wide
interlayers, these materials absorb and dissipate movement (strain) in the
pavement, reduce reflection cracking, and minimize water infiltration.
Strip interlayers usually consist of modified asphalt sandwiched between
non-woven paving geotextile and high-modulus woven geotextile. The
non-woven geotextile layer ensures a strong bond with the pavement sur-
face. The center modified-asphalt layer provides stress relief and resistance
to water infiltration. The top woven geotextile adds modulus and strength
to resist cracking in the overlay. Strip interlayers come in 12- to 20-inch
rolls that are 65 feet long.

Chip Seals

Chips seals are used as interlayers to help seal the underlying pavement
and help bond the overlay to it, as well as a means to delay reflection
cracking. These applications range from “conventional” single chip seals
to multiple course treatments that have a similar effect as an open-graded
crack relief layer in delaying reflection cracking. A chip seal may be placed
on an existing pavement or on a milled surface. Placement procedures are
described in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance”.

Stress-Absorbing Membrane

An asphalt-rubber stress-absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) is a
chip seal that consists of a heavy, hot-applied [G25-425°F) (162-218°C)]
crumb rubber (Figure 13.6) modified asphalt binder membrane, into which
a relatively large cover aggregate is imbedded. The SAMI is very flexible
and has a low modulus, allowing it to dampen movement at underlying
cracks, reducing tensile stress in the HMA overlay. The membrane will
also provide a waterproofing layer to reduce moisture intrusion into the
underlying layers.

Asphalt-rubber is defined by the (ASTM) D8 as: “a blend of asphalt
cement, reclaimed tire rubber, and certain additives in which the rubber
component is at least 15 percent by weight of the total blend and has
reacted in the hot asphalt cement sufficiently to cause swelling of the
rubber particles.” Information about the production and qualities of
asphalt-rubber can be found in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt,” and from
the Rubber Pavements Association (www.rubberpavements.org).

Asphalt-rubber is viscous and includes a dispersion of particulates that
require application at higher rates and temperatures than other asphalt
binders. Specially modified distributors that have larger and more pow-
erful circulating pumps, larger heating systems, and larger spray nozzle
openings are used.

13.3 Mill and Inlay

Cold milling a pavement consists of removing the surface to a specified
depth with specially designed equipment. Cold-milling equipment is avail-
able in many sizes. The equipment will range from large high-capacity
machines (see Figure 13.7) capable of milling up to a width of 16 feet.
(4.9 meters) and a depth of 12 inches (300 millimeters) to smaller machines
that can mill around manholes, valves, etc. (see Figure 13.8).

Cold milling is used to:

e Remove a deteriorated pavement to a desired depth, eliminating failed
or faulty materials;

Restore the pavement surface profile;

e Restore or maintain the drainage flow and curb reveal along street

curbs;

° Provide a textured surface for skid resistance and improved bonding

of an asphalt overlay; and/or

¢ Remove material under overhead structures to maintain clearances for

bridge structures, traffic signals, and overhead utilities.

The byproduct of the milling operation, Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement
(RAP), can be readily recycled into future asphalt pavements, saving both
asphalt cement and aggregates. Processes for recycling RAP are described
elsewhere.

Milling is conducted using a cutting drum, which is available in multiple
widths. The cutting drum uses replaceable tungsten carbide teeth to cut
the pavement surface (see Figure 13.9). These drums generally operate in
the “upcutting” direction to facilitate breaking and removal of the existing
material. The teeth are oriented in a helical or a spiral pattern to move the
milled material to the center of the cutting drum, where it is forced onto a
loadout conveyor for removal. The service life of the tungsten teeth depends
on the nature and the depth of the material being milled. A small amount
of water is used to control the amount of dust created by the milling process
and to cool the cutting teeth to extend their life. The size of the reclaimed
material can be adjusted by changing the rotational speed and/or reducing
the forward speed of the milling machine.

Micromilling (also called fine milling) is a variation of the standard
milling procedure. The micromilling process uses more cutting teeth to pro-
duce a much finer textured surface (see Figure 13.10). A conventional milling
machine has teeth spaced % inch (15 millimeters) - 3% inch (20 millimeters)
apart. The teeth on a micromilling drum are typically spaced about % inch
(8 millimeters) apart. This results in almost double the number of teeth for
micromilling versus conventional milling. It is also possible to have the
teeth spaced even closer at about 4 inch for micromilling, but this is not
very common. Micromilling can provide a very fine, uniform texture that
can serve as the final riding surface with or without an overlay. It can
also be used to correct some minor pavement distress or to remove pave-
ment markings because of changing traffic patterns. With micromilling,
the depth of cut is limited to about 1.5 inches (40 millimeters) because

13.3.1 Specifications

The end-result specification for cold milling is generally simple and

includes provisions that address the following details:

° A general requirement about the cold-milling machine. A typical
specification requires the contractor to use a machine with automatic
grade and slope controls, capable of cold milling an existing pave-
ment surface to the depth or profile specified.

¢ The milling width and depth. Whether to specify a depth of cut or
a nominal depth depends on whether or not the milling operation is
considered to be an opportunity to improve the profile, or ride qual-
ity, of the pavement. The smoothness, or ride quality, of the milled
surface can only be obtained if a nominal depth of cut is specified

¢ The maximum particle size of the reclaimed material. This will
generally be a maximum particle size of 14-2 inches (37.5-50
millimeters).

° The grade and slope requirements. This will generally be included
in the project plans or special provisions rather than in a standard
specification.

° The texture of the milled surface. This can be a problem if the
surface is open to traffic for an extended period before the overlay is
placed.

° Method of measure of quantities. This can be either “volume (cubic
yards [cubic meters]) or mass (tons [tonnes]) of reclaimed material
removed,” or “square yard (square meter) of surface milled” to a pre-
scribed depth.

* The ownership of the reclaimed material. Some agencies retain
ownership of some or all of the RAP generated from milling or
other forms of reclamation, while others make it the property of

13.3.2 Inspection

During the cold-milling process, the specifying agency should monitor
the following areas:

¢ The depth of the milling and the grade and cross-slope. This should
include checking both the edge of the surface being milled and the
crown. (Many times, the milling is used to correct the cross-slope of
the roadway.)

* The moisture content of the reclaimed material, if the cold-milling is
being paid for by mass.

° The smoothness of the surface, if a nominal depth of cut is specified.
This is usually checked with a 10-foot. straightedge, a profilograph, or
an inertial profiler as a part of a ride quality specification.

10.6.5 Pavement Smoothness

Pavement smoothness for quality-control purposes is generally measured
with a straightedge. Typical acceptance specifications require hand mea-
surements with a 10-foot (3 meter) straightedge, a rolling straightedge
(also called the California profilograph) or, inertial profilometers which
measure the International Roughness Index (IRD.

Typical requirements with a 10-foot straightedge are that there be no
bumps or dips greater than ¥% inch when compared with the two outer edges
of the straightedge. Measurements are generally taken in both the longitu-
dinal and the transverse direction. The straightedge is laid on the pavement
surface, and the maximum deviation of the surface from this straightedge is
measured and recorded. These measurements can be taken while compac-
tion is ongoing, and feedback can be provided to the paver and the roller
operators for making adjustments.

The rolling straightedge, or profilograph, is used by a large number of
states to measure the initial roughness of new construction and overlay proj-
ects. These profilographs use wheels at each end of the straightedge as the
contact point on pavement surface, and deviations in the pavement profile
are measured at the center point between the wheels. The devices can gener-
ally be used in lengths of 10-30 feet 3-9 meters). The measured deviations
are accumulated and reported as roughness in inches per mile. Profilograph
measurements and readings can be taken while compaction is ongoing .or
immediately afterward. Because profilograph testing can provide quick feed-
back to the paving crews, such testing can be useful as a quality-control test.
Some contractors will also use profilograph testing to evaluate the smooth-
ness of a pavement before placement of the surface course. The cost of pro-
filograph testing and subsequent grinding to improve smoothness could be
inexpensive when compared with potential incentive payments.

10.7 Acceptance Point
and Test Results

Acceptance testing is usually provided for the following properties in
HMA project specifications:

° Asphalt content

° Aggregate gradation

* Compaction

® Volumetric properties (air voids, VMA, VFA)

° Aggregate characteristics (CAA, FAA, F&E, SE, etc.)

° Ride quality

° Pavement thickness or spread rate

The criterion for acceptance is generally the same as the parameters
tested for quality-control. This makes sense because the purpose of quality-
control is to control production so the final product meets the acceptance
criteria. Because of differences in the use of quality-control and acceptance
tests, however, there are different sampling points and differences in test
procedures in some cases. The following sections discuss the sampling
and testing methods commonly used for acceptance, giving emphasis to
those that may be different from the quality-control methods discussed in
Section 10.3.

10.7.1 Asphalt Content and Gradation

The point of acceptance for asphalt content and aggregate grada-
tion is normally derived from the finished HMA product. HMA samples
are typically obtained from the roadway or the delivery truck. Refer to
Sections 10.2.3 and 10.2.4 for more detailed descriptions of these sam-
pling methods.

Asphalt binder content may be determined by solvent extraction, nuclear,
or ignition oven testing. The use of the ignition binder content method is
increasingly becoming the method of choice for acceptance testing. This
test provides reasonably accurate test results if it is properly calibrated for
the specific mix design materials being used, and it allows the use of the
recovered aggregate materials to be tested for gradation.

10.7.2 Aggregate Angularity (Fractured
Faces)

The point of acceptance for aggregate angularity is normally in the road-
way or after blending of the aggregate stockpiles. HMA samples from the

The methods used for acceptance of aggregate angularity are the same
as those used in quality-control testing.

10.7.3 Volumetric Properties (Voids, VMA,
VFA)

The sampling point for acceptance of volumetric properties is normally
at the point of delivery on-site or in delivery trucks at the plant. HMA
samples are generally taken from the roadway behind the paving machine,
from plant sampling devices, or from the delivery trucks prior to leaving
the plant. Refer to Sections 10.2.3 and 10.2.4 for more detailed descrip-
tions of theses sampling methods.

Volumetric properties generally refer to one or more of the following
three test parameters: 1) Laboratory air voids; 2) voids in the mineral
aggregate (VMA); or 3) voids filled with asphalt (VFA). These properties
are calculated using values derived from several different tests. Labora-
tory air voids require bulk specific gravity results from the laboratory
compacted specimen and the maximum theoretical specific gravity. Calcu-
lations of VMA require the following data: laboratory compacted bulk spe-
cific gravity of the mix; the bulk specific gravity of the blended aggregate;
and the asphalt content as a percentage of total mix. Calculations for VFA
use the air voids and the VMA values.

The acceptance point for compaction is the finished pavement. Sam-
pling is usually accomplished by cutting cores with a wet cut high-speed
core drill and diamond-studded core barrels. These methods remove the
cored specimens with little distortion or damage. The cored test speci-
mens are easily damaged, and the damage is not always visible. They need

10.7.5 Thickness

The acceptance point for thickness is the finished pavement. It is almost
exclusively measured by cutting cores from the completed pavement sur-
face. The cored specimens taken for the compaction testing described
above are measured for thickness before testing for unit weight.

Some agencies specify spread rates in lieu of thickness. Spread rates can
be checked as either an acceptance criteria or as a quality-control mea-
sure by calculating the weight of material placed over a measured area.
For example, if the paver is placing a 14-foot (4.3-meter) wide mat and it
spreads a 22.0-ton load over a length of 100 feet (30.5 meters), the spread
rate would be 31.4 pounds per square foot. Assume the target-spread rate
was 30.2 lb/ft? (147.5 kg/m?) to achieve an average design thickness of
2% inches (63.5 millimeters). The actual spread rate in this example is
4 percent over the target-spread rate.

Agencies do not like to have thinner pavement sections than are speci-
fied in the design, so a typical specification would require that spread
rates fall between the target and 10 percent over the target.

Spread-rate specifications are especially useful for overlay projects that
are seeking smoothness improvements on the roadway. Paving equipment,
by its nature, provides significant leveling characteristics as it places the
asphalt thicker in low areas and thinner in high areas. This improves ride
quality but creates variations in pavement thickness. The more rough the
existing pavement, the more variation in pavement thickness.

10.7.6 Ride Quality

Pavement smoothness is the leading factor in the traveling public’s per-
ception of road quality. In addition, rough or uneven pavements can have
an adverse affect on fuel usage, roadway safety, and vehicle maintenance
costs. Smoother pavements can also improve pavement durability, thereby
extending the life of the pavement.

Many agencies use pavement smoothness as an acceptance criteria, and
many offer incentives to contractors for building smoother pavements.
Ride quality is generally specified and tested with one of the following
four methods: Straightedge measurements, Profilograph, Response Type
Road Roughness Measuring Systems (RTRRMS), or Inertial profilometers.
All four methods have been used by agencies for acceptance testing of
roadway smoothness. Straightedge and Profilograph measurements have
also been used for quality-control testing. Each of the four pavement
smoothness measuring methods are discussed briefly below.

Straightedge

Straightedge measurements are perhaps the oldest form of measuring
pavement smoothness, but are generally included in even the most mod-
ern specifications, and they may be required for both quality-control and
acceptance testing. Quality-control checks of the pavement surface can
be made with a straightedge during the compaction operation, provid-
ing immediate feedback to the paving-machine and compactor operators.
Acceptance criteria using straightedge measurements generally include
requirements for joints, pavement edges, and miscellaneous paved areas
that are not accurately measured by other smoothness equipment.

Handheld straightedges range in length from 8 to 15 feet (2.4 to 4.6 meters),
and rolling straightedges can be 30 feet (9.2 meters) in length or more.

Profilograph

Profilographs measure the longitudinal profile of pavements. They are
used extensively for both quality-control and acceptance testing of HMA
pavements. Profilographs measure smoothness with a profile index that is
reported in inches per mile. In a rough sense, the inches per mile are the
number of inches of bumps or depressions that are accumulated in a mile.
The units are generally hand operated, and can be used in short lengths.
They can be used for quality-control or acceptance, but not for network
level pavement management.

Two types of profilometers are commonly used. They are referred to as
the “California type profilograph” and the “Rainhart type profilograph.”
The two types of equipment do not give the same results, so it is important
to use the type that is specified for the project.

Response Type Road Roughness Measuring System

Response Type Road Roughness Measuring Systems (RTRRMS) gauge
smoothness by the up and down movement of a standard car in response
to changes in road profile. This approach is intended to be what a traveler
would feel if driving the roadway. As such it is susceptible to variations
based on items such as speed, acceleration, and vehicle suspension. Read-
ings are taken at standard speeds to simulate actual driving conditions.

The Mays Meter is the most common form of RTRRMS. The units of
roughness are inches per mile. This equipment would not give the same
values of inches per mile as the profilographs. Mays Meter values are used
by agencies for acceptance and in pavement management systems to mea-
sure changes in pavement condition over time.

inertial Profilometers

Inertial profilometers measure the actual profile in a longitudinal
direction with readings typically taken along every inch of the roadway.
They measure profile changes with laser or acoustic devices that are
typically mounted on vehicle bumpers. They utilize inertial systems (accel-
erometers) that remove the up and down movement of the vehicle suspen-
sion from the data. Testing can be done at highway speeds (between 10 and
70 mph (6.2 kph and 43.4 kph) and the roadway roughness is presented in

of inertial profilometers, and AASHTO has developed provisional stan-

Inertial profilometers can be sensitive to the location and the number

but manufacturers have now developed lightweight devices mounted on
small four-wheeled vehicles that can be used almost immediately after
construction at slower speeds and for shorter distances.

10.8 Acceptance Criteria

The criteria for acceptance of HMA construction projects are established in
the project specifications. Various types of specifications are used—and oth-
ers are being proposed—that emphasize different levels of accountability and
risk to construction contractors. The four specifications listed below can be
viewed as a spectrum representing the progression of development for HMA
specifications in the United States. They are the following:

¢ Method, or recipe, specifications

® End-result specifications

° Quality-assurance specifications

° Performance-type specifications

Method, or recipe, specifications detail the products and procedures
used in the construction process. Equipment types and quantity, material cri-
teria such as aggregate gradations, methods of construction such as number
of roller passes, and other items closely control the construction process.

End-result specifications move away from controlling the process of con-
struction and concentrate on the testing of the mixture and pavement, after
completion, for specification conformance. The intent of this type of specifica-
tion is that the quality criteria used to judge final acceptance be selected based
on their impacts on maintenance costs and long-term pavement performance.

Quality-assurance specifications, while similar to end-result specifica-
tions, are based on statistical sampling and testing performed by the con-
tractor and the purchasing agency. Quality-control procedures conducted
by the contractor are used to assure that the specifications are being met
throughout the process. Acceptance sampling and testing, performed by
the agency, check the contractor’s QC process and determine whether the
materials should be accepted. The acceptance tests are based on appropriate
quality criteria, as are end-result specifications. In addition, Independent-
assurance (IA) sampling and testing are performed by a third party to
validate the acceptance and/or quality-control results.

Performance-type specifications emphasize performance of the fin-
ished product over time and do not concentrate on construction activities
or materials properties. Specific performance criteria are established in the
specifications, which are based on what the finished roadway must meet after
a specified length of time. Warranty specifications would fall in this category.

In addition, specifications that use performance-related tests to evaluate the
ability of a mix to perform as intended over a period of time are considered
to be performance-based specifications. Mathematical models are used to cal-
culate the future performance of the pavement based on the test results from
construction. Further discussion of performance specifications can be found
in Section 10.9.

In evaluating acceptance test data, it is important to realize that the results
of testing contain recognized statistical variation. In order to understand
what the data means, the engineer cannot ignore the statistical nature of
the results. The variations occur because of recognized limitations in the
precision and the consistency of test equipment and procedures. Statistical
acceptance methods are described in Section 10.8.4, “Statistical Acceptance,”
but even for traditional acceptance criteria, an understanding of the statisti-
cal nature of test results cannot be overlooked. It is necessary to have an
understanding of the normal variation in specific test parameters to make
reasonable decisions about acceptance and/or rejection of materials.

10.8.1 Historical Acceptance

Acceptance criteria are usually based on a number of specific properties
of the mixture and the finished pavement. Historically, the following set of
items was used to evaluate HMA construction projects:

° Materials—aggregate gradation, asphalt binder viscosity properties,

and asphalt content

° Thickness tolerances—cores, yield checks, and string lines

° Pavement density—cores and nuclear gauge readings

° Pavement smoothness—straightedge

For many years, the responsibility for testing and controlling materials
was held by the specifying agency. Contractors often submitted proposed
aggregate and asphalt materials to the agency. The agency then performed
a mix design and dictated the mixture proportions. At the start of produc-
tion, the agency conducted asphalt content tests and aggregate gradations
to make sure the job-mix formula was being duplicated within allowable
tolerances. As long as the job-mix formula was adhered to, the assumption
was made that the mixture volumetric criteria were also being met. This was
often a mistaken assumption.

This type of combined process control and acceptance also required the
agency to be responsible for adjusting the aggregate proportions and/or
the asphalt content of the mixture. By changing the mixture proportions
at the plant, the agency actions potentially had a direct effect on the con-
tractor’s ability to achieve density in the finished pavement. In some cases,
penalties were assessed to the contractor for low densities even though the
contractor was not in a position to make changes to the mixture to remedy
the situation.

10.8.2 Modern Acceptance (with
Volumetrics)

There have been recent trends toward quality-assurance specifications.
Current trends also are emphasizing mixture volumetric properties over the

volumetric properties in acceptance criteria will lead to better perfor-

Volumetric properties must be established in the mix design and con-

Process control has also been incorporated into the quality-assurance

the contractor more control over the finished product. Changes made at
the asphalt plant, which affect the volumetric properties, placement, and
compaction operations, are made by the contractor.

The quality of the finished pavement, however, is not entirely based on
mixture properties. The density of the roadway after construction plays a
major role in the overall performance of the roadway.

10.8.3 General Compaction Requirements

In most cases, pavement-density specifications are used to judge the
acceptability of the compaction process during construction. The goal of
compacting an HMA pavement is to achieve an optimum air-void content
and provide a smooth, uniform surface. The resultant in-place air-void
content of the HMA is a very important factor that affects performance of
the mixture throughout the life of the pavement.

There are a variety of factors that are not part of the compaction
operation that can have a significant impact on compaction. Many of these
factors are listed below:

° Properly designed plant-produced mixtures

° Proper sampling and handling procedures of samples from the mixing

facility or roadway

° Proper field laboratory testing procedures, especially correct compac-

tion techniques and maximum theoretical density as appropriate

° Proper sampling, handling, and testing of the pavement core samples

e Adequate underlying support for the mixture during the compaction

process

Most pavement compaction specifications fall into two categories. The
first and most common specifies that compaction shall achieve a certain
percentage of a reference density. This is called a reference density speci-
fication, and the reference density can come from the laboratory or from
test strips in the field. The second category is called a method specification.
This type requires that specified means and methods be accomplished.
Each of these types of specifications will be discussed in more detail on
the following pages.

be determined and corrected. This problem may also require that the mix
be adjusted or redesigned. Pavement with an initial air void content below
3 percent is susceptible to permanent deformation and flushing after
further consolidation due to traffic. Conversely, high initial air void levels,
above 8 percent, are likely to yield pavements that age prematurely. These
low-density pavements are more permeable to the damaging effects of
air and water migrating into the interior of the mat. The result is a brittle
pavement, which is more likely to crack due to age hardening of the
asphalt binder and the effects of thermal expansion and contraction of the
aggregate and moisture, resulting in a reduction in pavement strength.
For these reasons, it is recommended that compaction specifications
require the resulting in-place air voids, immediately after construction,
to be between 4 and 8 percent (96-92 percent of maximum theoretical
density). Studies have shown this range in pavement air voids offers
adequate resistance to air and water intrusion for most dense-graded

Control Strip Density Specifications

Some agencies specify compaction requirements to meet a percentage
of a control strip density. Under these specifications, a test strip of HMA is
placed and compacted at the beginning of normal production paving. The
control strip is placed using specified or otherwise selected rolling proce-
dures and equipment. The intent is that the test strip be compacted to the
maximum achievable density, which then becomes the specified reference
density. Once the density of this control strip is established, the rest of
the paving is required to achieve a percentage of this density. These types
of specifications usually require the control strip to achieve a minimum
percentage of the laboratory density as well, to avoid mixtures that are
not compactable. This type of specification allows the relative difficulty
(or ease) of compacting a given mix to become a factor in establishing the
compaction requirements. New control strips are required for a different
mix design or pavement application.

Quality-control testing for this type of project includes in-place density
testing as described in 10.6.2, except that the compaction is calculated
differently.

98 percent of control strip density, and the control strip density measured
for this particular application is 140.6 Ib/ft®? (2251 kg/m/%). If the quality-

Method Density Specifications

Some agencies specify detailed means and methods to be followed for
compacting HMA. These methods include requirements such as size and
type of compaction equipment, and they usually include a specific num-

10.8.4 Statistical Acceptance—Percent
Within Limits (PWL) Process

Acceptance test results on many projects are evaluated using statistical
acceptance procedures. Pay factors may be calculated on specified criteria
to award incentives or disincentives on the criteria that are evaluated. Some-
times pay factors may be calculated with a statistical process called Percent
Within Limits (PWL), which is calculated for a given lot of material produced.
The materials produced and placed for each lot would have pay factors that
would be applied to the quantities produced in that lot.

Pavement owners wish to get their most important quality parameters
as close as possible to the target values. The asphalt content of a mixture,
for example, will be detrimental to the longevity of a pavement if it is
either too high or too low. So the owners want plants producing HMA for
their projects to be focused on achieving the actual target value of the job-
mix formula, as opposed to achieving some minimum value. Statistically
speaking, this means the owner wants the sample mean to match the tar-
get values. The sample mean is the average value of all test results. If four
asphalt content tests were run for a given lot, for example, the average of
those four tests would be the mean asphalt content for that lot.

The owners also want consistency of the materials produced. They want
all the material produced to be close enough to the target values so that the
long-term performance of the entire pavement will be good. Roadways often
have to be replaced when the worst portion of the material begins to fail.
Statistically speaking, the owner wants to have low variability or low variance
in the test results. Variance is the difference between a tested value and the
average tested value. So, specifying low variance is the same thing as saying
all of the test results should be close to the average. Statisticians calculate a
“standard deviation” to represent the variability in test results.

The third thing that owners want is to have a higher level of confi-
dence that they know the level of quality actually achieved on the project.
The test results from one compaction test for 5,000 feet of roadway, for
example, would not give an engineer a high degree of confidence that he
knows what compaction level was achieved throughout the pavement.
A higher degree of confidence would be achieved if the appropriate num-
ber of samples were taken and the best (most accurate) sampling and test-
ing methods available were used.

Statistical Basis
It is important to realize that the test results do not always represent
the exact values that exist within the mixture. Assume, for example,

that an asphalt plant produces hot mix asphalt during a given day and
the binder content starts out at 5.1 percent, increasing to 5.5 percent
at the end of the day, but the overall average of the binder content is
exactly 5.3 percent. Assume the laboratory gets two samples from the
plant, one in the morning, and one at midday, and their test results are
5.1 percent and 5.3 percent. In this example, the average of the test results is
(5.1 + 5.3)/2 = 5.2. The tested average was 5.2 percent and the actual
average was 5.3 percent. This example also shows that if the laboratory
had taken more samples, and had the samples been spaced more evenly to
better represent the day’s production, it could have come closer to achiev-
ing the actual average value.

This concept, that the test results do not exactly represent all of the
material produced, is important for understanding the statistics. It is the
reason that a statistical parameter called percent within limits (PWL) is cal-
culated. If the asphalt content is measured on four samples evenly spaced
throughout the day and all four of the tested asphalt contents fall within
the upper and lower limits of the specification, intuitively that means we
had 100 percent within the limits. Statistically speaking, however, that
means there were four test values that can describe something about the
mean asphalt content and the standard deviation of the asphalt content.
Based on those numbers (mean and standard deviation), the percentage
that fell within the upper and lower limits can be calculated. That percent-
age is the PWL, and it is calculated for each set of test results to determine
what percentage, of all of the mix produced, likely fell within the specifi-
cations limits. The more test results there are, the more accurately the PWL
represents the materials produced. If an insufficient number of samples
are tested, the PWL will be inaccurate. If an agency desires to reduce the
number of samples taken, the number of samples per lot should not-be
reduced. Increasing the lot size would be more appropriate.

Statistical acceptance procedures assume reasonably uniform produc-
tion. The statistical models will not adequately represent materials’ proper-
ties if a significant change occurs in the production process. This can be
remedied by varying a lot size or truncating a lot when a known change
is made to the production process.

Quality Characteristics for Pay Factors

Projects that are poorly controlled will have high variability in test
results. The more consistent the materials and methods are, the lower the
variability will be. Specifications establish acceptance parameters that will
be used in pay-factor calculations. Attributes often used for acceptance
include asphalt content, aggregate gradation, mixture volumetric proper-
ties, compaction, and ride quality. Agencies do not use statistical methods
for all their acceptance criteria. Only parameters that can be tested and
produced within reasonable statistical variation can be used. Segregation,
for example, is not a parameter that can be effectively incorporated into
a statistics-based acceptance program. Test procedures that generate
repeatable numbers are needed for application of statistical methods.

10.9 Performance-Based

Specifications

Public agencies and other owners of asphalt pavements generally desire
to construct smooth, long-lasting pavements that are cost effective when
considering maintenance and all pavement costs. These needs have driven
many innovative developments and are leading to different ways of pur-
chasing HMA products.

Traditionally, HMA specifications have required measurement of basic
quality characteristics that are believed to relate to long-term pavement per-
formance. Pavement quality is defined by how well the measured properties
of the constructed pavement match contract requirements. The specifications
are focused on compliance with empirically derived quality parameters.

New “performance-based” specifications are being used that define
quality based on predicted performance of the completed pavement. Pay-
ment is determined by comparing the life-cycle costs of the constructed
pavement with those used in the pavement design. Two types of models
are needed to calculate the life-cycle costs. The first are performance pre-
diction models that estimate the severity, extent, and timing of distresses
that will occur in the pavement. The second are maintenance cost models.
They estimate life-cycle costs, including those for maintenance, repairs,
and rehabilitation based on the distresses predicted.

10.9.1 Specification Definitions

Agencies and other owners of HMA pavements have used various types
of specifications throughout the years. Specifications that use perfor-
mance measures can be explained in the context of some of the other
specifications that have been used. The first four definitions are from the
AASHTO Highway Subcommittee on Construction, Quality Construction
Task Force.

Method specifications are specifications that require the contractor to
produce and place a product using specified materials in definite propor-
tions and specific types of equipment and methods under the direction of
the agency.

End-result specifications are specifications that require the contractor
to take the entire responsibility for producing and placing a product. The
agency’s responsibility is to either accept or reject the final product or to
apply a price adjustment commensurate with the degree of compliance
with the specifications.

Performance-related specifications are specifications that use quantified
quality characteristics and life-cycle cost relationships that are correlated to
product performance.

10.10 Performance

Specifications—Warranties

Warranties are not new to construction contracts. In recent years, however,
there has been a growing interest by many owners of asphalt pavements to
get long-term warranties for road construction. Warranties, or performance
specifications, require a contractor to warrant that an item will perform in a
manner that has been agreed to in advance.

Successful performance specifications require the contractor to be respon-
sible for those specific pavement characteristics that the contractor can con-
trol. Specifications that require contractors to warrant everything, including
things well outside of the contractor’s control or ability to estimate, are both
unfair and unwise. For example, contractors generally have no control over
traffic-loading, future development, etc. It is in the owner’s interest to have
clear definitions of the contractor’s responsibilities under the warranty. The
warranties are normally secured by bonds. For long warranty periods, the
bonds can be very expensive, and become a significant part of the cost of
the project. Because of this, warranty paving projects in the United States
typically have been limited to about 5 years. Requiring a 20-year bond for
full reconstruction costs may be unreasonable and/or very expensive.

Warranty contracts can require the contractor to provide project accep-
tance and quality-control criteria to the owner. Typically a quality-control plan
including documentation of certifications for the materials used on the proj-
ect will be included. The plan could provide certifications of the contractor's
testing laboratory and personnel, mix design methods, sampling methods,
plant operations, lay-down operations, and density control methods. Some
warranty contracts also require a documentation process that will provide the
agency with acceptance-type test reports and other pavement data that they
would normally collect on their own projects, use with pavement manage-
ment or other asset management databases.

14.4 Rubblization

Rubblization is the process of fracturing the existing PCC pavement
in-place into small, interconnected pieces that serve as a base course for
a new hot mix asphalt (HMA) overlay. The rubblization process destroys
slab action, eliminating the possibility of reflection cracking.

14.4.1 Project Selection and Overview

Whether a pavement is a good candidate for rubblization is a judgment
to be made by a qualified engineer. Generally, PCC pavements that require
reconstruction or unbonded PCC overlays may also be candidates for rub-
blization. The Wisconsin Department of Transportation considers the use
of rubblization when one or more of the following conditions are met:

¢ More than 20 percent of the concrete pavement joints needs repair.

* More than 20 percent of the concrete pavement surface has been

patched.

° More than 20 percent of the concrete slabs exhibits slab breakup

distress.

* More than 20 percent of the project exhibits longitudinal joint distress

greater than 4 inches wide.

Unlike other fractured slab techniques, rubblization is suitable for any type
of PCC pavement. It is the only method that can be used on continuously
reinforced concrete (CRCP). CRCP exhibiting more than five punch-outs per
mile should be considered as candidates for rubblization and overlay.

The equipment and processes that have evolved for rubblization were
developed for use on relatively thick PCCP slabs constructed over an
improved subbase. Thin (less than 6 inches, 150 milimeters) slabs placed
directly over poorly drained fine-grained subgrade soils are not good can-
didates for rubblization.

14.4.2 Design Considerations

The thickness of the HMA overlay should be determined according to the
principles outlined in Chapter 6. Falling-weight deflectometer (FWD) testing
on rubblized pavements and subsequent back-calculation analysis has consis-
tently shown that the layer coefficient of a rubblized concrete pavement layer
is greater than that of a crushed-aggregate base course. Most airfield agencies
have structurally characterized a rubblized layer conservatively by treating
it as equivalent to a crushed-aggregate base having a CBR of 100. Highway
agencies using the 1993 AASHTO design guide have typically characterized

14.4.3 Construction

The procedural steps in the rubblization technique are essentially the
same for airfields and highways. These steps are the following:

° Prepare the surface and remove any existing overlays.

¢ Install a subdrainage system (if necessary).

° Rubblize the concrete pavement.

° Cut off and remove any exposed steel reinforcement.

* Remove exposed joint-sealing material.

° Roll the rubblized concrete pavement.

° Remove and patch any unstable areas.

° Place asphalt HMA leveling course/HMA overlays.

Prepare the Surface

For the rubblization equipment to work properly, any material from a previ-
ous overlay must be removed before rubblizing the underlying concrete.

Adjacent pavements that are not to be rubblized must be isolated from the
rubblized pavements. This is best accomplished by a full-depth sawcut or by
cutting a relief trench with a wheel saw. Either method allows the rubblizing
equipment to work up to the project limits without damaging the structural
integrity of the pavement that is to remain in place.

Underground structures, utilities, and in-pavement fixtures must be
identified as needing protection during the rubblization process. Isola-
tion or removal and replacement may be required during the rubblization
process. Provisions must also be made to raise all in-pavement features to
match the grade of the new HMA pavement.

install a Subdrainage System

A key element to a successful rubblization project is to assure that no free
water exists immediately under the PCC pavement. This often requires the
installation of a properly designed underdrain system. Underdrain systems
serve two purposes: before rubblization, a subsurface drainage system dries
and stabilizes the subbase or subgrade, and during the service life of the
new pavement it prevents water from becoming trapped within the pave-
ment structure.

Any free water trapped in the subbase or subgrade will weaken the
subgrade and cushion the impacts from the pavement breakers. This loss
of subgrade support reduces the ability of the equipment to properly frac-
ture the concrete. To ensure proper subgrade drainage, many rubblization
specifications require drainage to be installed a set period of time (often
two weeks) before commencing rubblization.

During the service life of the pavement, the underdrain system must
have sufficient capacity and depth to collect and carry the water being
discharged from the subgrade, the old permeable base, and the top or
“permeable zone” of the rubblized layer (see Figure 14.10). The upper
2 inches of the rubblized concrete is porous, but the remainder of the layer
does not appear to be highly permeable. The edge drainage system should
be designed to collect and dispose of moisture directly below the interface
between the rubblized PCCP and the new HMA layer. The trench depth
must also be deep enough to accomplish the intended drainage function.
It is recommended that the trench be deep enough to allow the top of the
pipe to be located a minimum of 2 inches (5 centimeters) below the bot-
tom of the permeable base material. The filter fabric and trench backfill
material must also be designed with sufficient capacity to handle the out-
flow from the new pavement structure. Erosion of fines into the side-drain
system should not pose problems because the base, or rubblized concrete,
should not contain erodable fines that might clog the system.

Rubblize the Concrete Pavement

There are two basic types of rubblization equipment: the Resonant Pave-
ment Breaker (RPB) and the Multihead Breaker (MHB). These two machine
types are described below.

Various models of the resonant pavement breaker exist. All are
self-contained, self-propelled units that vary in size and weight. The larg-
est model is the RB-500 (see Figure 14.11), which is a sixth-generation
machine developed to rubblize heavy-load PCCP—thicker than 13 inches

(330 millimeters). The RB-500 is powered by a 600 horsepower (447

The concept for resonant rubblization was developed by Ray Gurries as
a means to facilitate the removal of reinforced PCC slabs. High-frequency
(42-46 Hz), low-amplitude (less than 1 inch; 25 millimeters) blows are
generated by an oscillating beam and applied through a steel-breaking shoe.
Diagonal cracks are induced through the entire thickness of the slab, while
the PCC near the surface is crushed, resulting in smaller particles near the
surface. The broken concrete is debonded from any reinforcing steel and
remains tightly interlocked.

The RPB rubblizes the PCC pavement in narrow strips as the machine
moves forward adjacent to the edge of a previously fractured pavement.
It continues with successive passes until it has moved transversely across
the width of the pavement. The forward speed of the machine depends
on the size of the machine, slab thickness, stiffness of the PCC, and the
support provided by the subgrade. The machine is operated at a forward
speed of less than 5 miles per hour (8 kilometers per hour). One machine
can be expected to rubblize 6,000-8,000 square yards (5000-7000 square
meters) per workday shift, with thick airfield pavement work being on the
low end of this range. It is not unusual for multiple machines to be employed
to expedite the process on major interstate projects.

As an RPB fractures an existing PCC pavement, a small amount of dila-
tion takes place. For extremely wide pavements, such as airport runways
and taxiways, relief trenches sometimes have to be cut full depth through
the pavement at fixed intervals to allow for horizontal expansion and to
reduce the confining pressure at interior locations on these pavements. This
is usually not necessary for highway pavements. To remain intact, any exist-
ing PCC pavement must be isolated by using a full-depth sawcut to avoid
damage. No damage has been observed from operating a resonant breaker
adjacent to new HMA placed during shoulder or edge-drain installation.

Multiple-Head Pavement Breaker

The multiple-head pavement breaker is a rubber-tire, self-contained, self-
propelled unit (see Figure 14.12) that breaks the PCC using a series of drop
hammers. It consists of six pairs of hammers—each weighing 1,200 pounds
(544 kilograms) measuring 8 inches (20 centimeters) in width—mounted
laterally in pairs, with half the hammers in a forward row and the remainder
diagonally offset in a rear row so the breakage is continuous from side to
side. Wing units, carrying two 1,500-pound (680 kilograms) hammers, can
be added to each side for a total breaking width of up to 13 feet (4 meters).
In the 16-hammer configuration, the MHB weighs 57,000 pounds (25,855
kilograms). Each pair of hammers is attached to a separate hydraulic lift
cylinder with an adjustable drop height from 60 inches (0-1.5 meters). Each
pair operates as an independent unit, and develops 1,000-8,000 foot-pounds
of energy, depending on the drop height selected, and cycles at a rate of
30-35 impacts per minute.

By controlling the individual lift cylinder for each pair of hammers,
the MHB can rubblize in widths of 2-13 feet (0.6-4 meters) at a produc-
tion rate of 500-1500 square yards (418-1254 square meters) per hour.

(0.11-0.32 kilometers per hour). Production rates are comparable to the
RPB and depend on the strength and thickness of the slab and the underly-
ing subgrade.

Rubblization using an MHB requires a single pass over an area to be
rubblized. The tractor or power unit travels on the unbroken slab as it
moves forward and tows the breaking unit behind. Because it is a single-
pass technique, the MHB does not have to travel on the rubblized surface.
Fracturing of the slab is controlled by a set combination of drop height,
frequency of the impacts, and the forward speed of the machine, as deter-
mined by a test strip/pit.

Part of the process of using an MHB is to roll the broken pavement
using a smooth steel-wheel roller onto which a Z-grid pattern has been
attached. This breaks any elongated surface particles into a more desir-
able, cubical shape. Its operation is described later in this section.

Cut Off and Remove Any Exposed Steel Reinforcement

Reinforcing steel or wire mesh that emerges above the surface during
rubblization must be removed by cutting with a torch that is flush with the sur-
rounding surface. Otherwise, reinforcing steel, wire mesh, and load-transfer
devices should be left in place.

Remove Exposed Joint-Sealing Material

Joint sealant materials such as rubberized asphalt and low modulus silicone
are usually loosened during rubblization but often left in place. Any wood
joint fillers should be removed before rubblizing the PCC pavement.

Roll the Rubblized Concrete Pavement

The PCC pavement must be rolled after rubblizing. Rolling tightens the
surface by seating loose particles as well as by smoothing the surface in
preparation for paving. This rolling is not intended to achieve additional
densification of the rubblized material or underlying layers. Excessive roll-
ing could reduce particle interlock and cause a weakening of the rubblized
layer.

When the rubblization is done with an RPB, the rubblized concrete
should be rolled with two to four passes of a smooth tandem vibrating
roller Gninimum of 10 tons; 9,000 kilograms) for surface smoothing and
further alignment of the fractured concrete pieces. The roller should be
operated at the highest-frequency setting available at a speed that will
produce at least 10 impacts per foot (33 impacts per meter).

When the rubblization is done with a multiple-head breaker, the rubblized
concrete should be rolled with two passes of a vibratory steel-drum roller fit-
ted with a special “Z” pattern grid on the drum face (see Figure 14.13). The
grid roller should weigh a minimum of 14 tons (12,700 kilograms). This pro-
vides a uniform surface and reduces the size of the surface pieces. The grid
roller is followed by a 25-ton (22,680 kilograms) pneumatic-tire roller and
often a tandem vibratory roller for final seating.

Remove and Patch Any Unstable Areas

During rubblization and rolling, any area found to be unstable must be
removed and replaced. It is recommended that the section be replaced
with a high-quality aggregate base or with HMA.

Place Asphalt HMA Leveling Course/HMA Overlays

Rubblized surfaces cannot be trimmed or fine graded like conventional
granular surfaces. Larger pieces will be disturbed by fine-grading equip-
ment, resulting in the loss of particle interlock and an overall reduction in
the strength of the rubblized layer. Leveling courses may be necessary to
accommodate grade and profile corrections.

Placement of the HMA overlay on a rubblized surface is accomplished
according to the procedures discussed in Chapter 8, “Hot Mix Asphalt
Paving.” Tack coats are not used because of the pickup of the rubblized
surface by the construction equipment. Belly-dump trailers and windrow
elevators for transferring the HMA into the hopper of the paving machine
should not be used to place the first lift of HMA over rubblized PCC. It is
suggested that the first lift be placed with a tracked paving machine. It is
also recommended that traffic be kept off the surface until the entire HMA
overlay has been placed.

14.4.4 Specification Criteria and Inspection

The rubblization process will result in smaller particles near the surface of
the pavement; these particles will range from sand size to 3 inches (75 milli-
meters) to 12-15 inches (300-380 millimeters) at the bottom of the rubblized
layer on very thick PCC pavements. The actual size will vary depending on
the slab thickness, the type of reinforcement, the subgrade strength, and the
equipment used to rubblize. The specifications usually require that the con-
tactor dig a test pit on the first day of production to verify the efficiency of
the rubblization process. The specification requirements vary. Airfield proj-
ects funded by the Federal Aviation Administration require at least 75 per-
cent of the particles smaller than 3 inches (75 millimeters) at the surface and
12 inches (300 millimeters) in the bottom half. Other highway projects require
all the particles to be less than 10 inches (250 millimeters) on the surface.

15.1 Cold Mix Asphalt

15.1.1 Introduction

Cold mix asphalt preparation is not as complicated as that described
for hot mixes in Chapter 7. Generally, placing and compacting does not
usually call for equipment as sophisticated as that required for hot mix
asphalt. However, this type of pavement construction is no less important,
because a large percentage of the nation’s roadways have cold mix asphalt
bases and even surfaces.

A less-controlled, simplified process is an advantage during in situ cold mix
preparation with a distributor and a motor-grader operation. But the same
quality control as that found in hot mix asphalt preparation is possible when
a cold mix is prepared in a plant-mix operation. However, many plants used
for producing cold mixes do not use, or have, all the means for maintaining
close uniformity of control. Wherever suitable materials are available, such
plant-mixing procedures are employed for high production and low cost.

Cold mix recycling or reworking of existing asphalt pavement or aggre-
gate structures is another important pavement construction alternative.
Cold mix recycling is the process by which reclaimed asphalt pavement
(RAP), reclaimed aggregate materials (RAM), or both are combined with
new asphalt in place or at a central mixing facility to produce cold mix
base mixtures. For more complete information on cold mix recycling, see
Chapter 13, “Rehabilitation Methods for Asphalt Pavements.”

15.1.2 Description

Cold-placed asphalt pavement mixtures (cold mixes) are generally made
with emulsified or cutback asphalt. Emulsified asphalt may be anionic
or cationic MS and SS grades. Cutback asphalt may be MC or SC grades.
Special additives to the emulsions add a High Float (HF) designation to
the grading system and will slow the breaking of the emulsion when
the reclaimed material has a high percentage of fine material or minus
No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. The Asphalt Institute’s MS-19, Asphalt Emulsion
Manual should be consulted for more information.

The aggregate material may be anything from a dense-graded crushed
aggregate to a granular soil having a relatively high percentage of dust.
The aggregate at the time of mixing might be damp, air-dry, or artificially
heated and dried.

Mixing occurs either in the roadway, along the roadway, or in a stationary
mixing facility. The resulting mixtures are usually spread and compacted at
ambient temperature; however, there are exceptions. Some asphalt mixes

15.1.3 Suitability for Pavement
Construction

Cold-laid asphalt mixes may be used for surface, base, or subbase courses if
the pavement structure is properly designed. Cold-laid surface courses are suit-
able for light and medium traffic. When used in the base or subbase, they may
be suitable for all types of traffic. Also when used on large jobs, cold-laid paving
mixtures reduce construction costs, but not necessarily the strength or quality
of the pavement structure. High quality cold mixes, if properly designed and
constructed, can provide a structural equivalency equal to that of hot mixes.

Asphalt pavements made by cold-laid methods can be designed and built
to meet current traffic requirements. A thicker pavement can be achieved by
adding additional aggregate material if required, during in-place mixing or
by adding additional pavement courses.

15.1.4 Mix Composition

A wide variety of aggregates and soil-aggregate combinations can be
used in cold-mixes. In many cases, material from road cuts, pits, or nearby
quarry sites may be economically crushed or used. Often the entire output
of a rock crusher less than a certain maximum size is used.

Suitable local materials also include granular soils having up to 20 percent
passing the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve, as well as clean sands and gravel.

Granular materials that are mostly fine aggregate and have a relatively
high percentage of mineral dust may cause mixing, aeration, and compac-
tion problems.

Well-graded aggregates are always desirable for any course of the asphalt
pavement structure. However, poorly graded and gap-graded aggregates
can be adequate for base courses when they are properly prepared by
road mixing methods and procedures.

Dense-graded aggregate mixtures are graded from the maximum size
down to and including material passing the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve.

larly, can be used for the full range of base and surface pavement types,
depending on aggregate quality and equipment. Substantial savings can
be realized when locally available, unprocessed aggregates are used on
lightly traveled roads and in bases for heavy-duty pavements.

Sand Mixes

Except for aggregate gradation, the same basic principles apply for
production of sand-emulsion plant mixes as for dense-graded, coarse-
aggregate emulsion mixes. Sand mixes may be used for either base or
surface courses.

The addition of 1 to 2 percent Portland cement will aid in the develop-
ment of early initial strength. Thorough mixing is essential to uniform
distribution of the cement throughout the mixture.

A wide variety of fine aggregates throughout the country have produced
satisfactory results. Blending of two or more aggregates may be necessary
to produce the desirable mix characteristics. Typical gradations are listed
in Table 15.4.

The emulsified-asphalt content usually varies from 6 to 10 percent. Emul-
sion grades SS-1, SS-1h, CSS-1, CSS-Ih, or HFMS-2s may be used, along
with a laboratory-determined amount of mixing water added to the sand.

15.1.8 Construction Methods

Roadbed Preparations

The roadbed on which cold-mixed material is placed must be shaped and
compacted. The surface must be swept with a power broom to remove dirt
and other foreign matter. Depending upon the condition or type of base
or subgrade, a prime coat of MC-30 or MC-70 may be necessary. In some
regions, environmental regulations have restricted the use of MC and RC cut-
back asphalts. After application, the prime coat is allowed to cure, and after
24 hours, any excess asphalt remaining on the surface is blotted with sand.

Windrows

In several types of road-mix construction, it is necessary to place the
aggregate into windrows. Windrow sizers are used to ensure that the cor-
rect volume for the desired depth of pavement is placed. Usually, loose
material on the unpaved road surface is of too small a quantity and not
uniform enough in size for use in the road mix. In such cases, the mate-
rial is bladed onto the shoulders. If the material from the roadway can
be practically used, it is bladed into a windrow and measured. Additional
aggregate may be added as necessary to improve the gradation or to pro-
vide the required volume.

If two or more materials are combined on the area being surfaced, each
is placed in its own sized windrow. These windrows are then bladed back
and forth to thoroughly mix them and combine them into a single wind-
row before asphalt is added.

Proper viscosity is essential for the asphalt to flow easily through the
spray nozzles and adequately coat the aggregate particles. Cutback asphalt,
although already fluid, needs some heating to bring it to the proper vis-
cosity for spraying, which is 20-120 centistokes. Proper safety precautions
should always be taken when heating cutback asphalt. Mixing should
begin immediately, because the viscosity will quickly rise above that rec-
ommended for mixing, which is 150-300 centistokes. The volatiles in cut-
back asphalt keep them fluid long enough for the completion of road
mixing. The aggregate temperature should be at least 50°F (10°C) in the
shade at mixing time.

15.1.9 Methods of Preparing Mixes

The four techniques for preparing paving mixtures for cold-laid pave-
ments are the following:

° Blade mixing

° Rotary mixing

° Travel-plant mixing

¢ Stationary mixing facilities

All but stationary plant mixing are considered mixed-in-place tech-
niques. The principal advantage of using mixed-in-place techniques for
construction is that they use aggregate already on the site or available
from nearby sources. The aggregate and asphalt can be mixed in place
with minimal equipment.

The in-place mixing systems provide low-cost techniques for production
of cold mixes. It is not possible to achieve 100 percent asphalt coating
of the aggregate. Sufficient coating can be achieved to produce a tightly
bonded mixture. The mixing procedure selected should be one that mini-
mizes the amount of uncoated aggregate particles.

Stationary mixing facilities provide opportunities for better control of propor-
tioning and mixing. In some cases, such as those mixes made with emulsified
asphalt, no heating or drying of the aggregate before mixing is necessary.

Whenever asphalt emulsions are used, the aggregate should be damp-
ened before introducing the emulsion. Dampening slows the breaking
of the emulsion. Otherwise, the dry aggregate absorbs the water in the
emulsion, which makes coating the aggregate particles very difficult. A
good practice is to determine the optimum fluid content (a combination of
moisture and emulsion, equal to the optimum moisture of the aggregate)
for the mix. That amount of moisture (optimum moisture content minus
the emulsion to be added) plus about 2 percent should be applied to the
aggregate before introducing the emulsion. This process yields good uni-
form mixing, with well-coated aggregate particles, and provides the added
moisture (which will evaporate during handling and placing of the mix)
needed to ensure proper fluid content at the time of compaction.

Blade Mixing

Using motor graders or some type of multiple-blade drag to blend the
asphalt and the aggregate usually accomplishes blade mixing (Figure 15.2).
The aggregate is first air dried by manipulating it across the roadway with
the grader to expose it to the sun and air. After the moisture content has
been reduced to 3 percent or less, the windrow is spread uniformly over
about half the roadway.

Asphalt is then applied from an asphalt distributor in two or three
spraying passes. Immediately following each application, the asphalt and
aggregate are partially mixed to leave as little free asphalt as possible on
the surface and to prevent pools of asphalt from forming.

After receiving the full predetermined amount of asphalt, the partially
mixed material is formed back into a windrow. It is then worked back and

self-propelled machine (see Figure 15.3). The chamber is open at the bot-

carbide-cutting teeth. This type of equipment is particularly useful for

be incorporated into the mix. The blades or cutting teeth serve a twofold

material to a predetermined level. The asphalt can be introduced in one

3.1.4 Aggregate Production

The overall objective in aggregate processing is to economically pro-
duce aggregates that are appropriate for the end use. Particularly for the
structural and functional needs of the more demanding asphalt paving
mixtures, aggregates must have particular particle shape, surface texture,
and grading or particle-size distribution characteristics in addition to the
inherent strength and durability characteristics.

Extraction

The first stage in the production of aggregates is the extraction or min-
ing of the source material from its deposited condition. The material must
be accessed through an open or “working” face of the deposit. Many times,
overburden (unwanted or unusable material) that lies above the aggre-
gate deposit must be removed or stripped before the working face can
be established. In cases where overburden is primarily soil, removal is
typically accomplished with common earth-moving equipment such as
scrapers. Removal of overburden containing more highly consolidated soil
and rock fragments may require bulldozers, front-end loaders, and haul
trucks.

In their natural state in a dry pit, gravel and sand are typically in a rela-
tively unconsolidated condition. For this condition, mining of the working
face is usually accomplished with front-end loaders or power shovels. For
pits with larger and taller working faces, a bulldozer is often employed to
push the material down the face to then allow for the use of loaders or
shovels. The extracted material is then loaded into haul trucks or placed
on a conveyor for transportation to the processing plant for screening,
crushing, and washing. ;

Some deposits of sand and gravel lie beneath bodies of water, such as
rivers and lakes. Extraction of these materials usually requires dredges,
draglines, or large suction pumps.

Quarry stone is commonly extracted by drilling and blasting the rock
formation. Mechanical extraction tools, such as rock hammers for further
rock fragmentation after blasting, are sometimes necessary. Equipment
and methodology for handling and transporting the rock fragments or
shot rock is essentially the same as that for gravel and sand.

Crushing

Most HMA mixtures use a maximum aggregate size of 1% inches (37.5
millimeters) in diameter or less. In addition, they require some minimum
level of aggregate surface roughness and shape for developing the neces-
sary aggregate structure to resist pavement loads and deformation. Hence,
crushing of the aggregate is usually necessary to further reduce gravel and
rock fragments to proper particle sizes and to create appropriate shape
and surface characteristics.

The two main types of crushers are called compression and impact—so
named according to the manner in which force is applied to the fracture

as jaw, cone, gyratory, and roll crushers, essentially squeeze or compress
rock between their crushing surfaces and fracture it. In impact crush-

surfaces inside the crusher at high speeds. The rock particles shatter in
impact crushers.

The amount of reduction in particle size, the shape of the aggregate par-
ticles, and the amount of fines or minus No. 4 (4.75 millimeters) material
vary with the different sizes and kinds of crushers, as well as with the rock
type and rate at which the rock is fed into the crusher. In order to yield the
most appropriate end products, multiple crushing stages with optimized
crushing equipment for each particular stage coupled with appropriate
screening sequences are common.

Screening

The screening operation is the part of the processing procedure in
which aggregates are separated according to size. Material may be passed
through single-, double-, or even triple-screening applications in order to
make the various needed sizes of aggregate. Before or during screening
applications, material can be routed to crushing operations. Additionally,
material can be conveyed to washing operations before, during, or after
screening applications.

Several types of equipment are used for screening, but the most com-
mon is the vibrating inclined screen. This equipment allows the slope,
vibration speed, stroke, and direction of rotation of the screen surface
to be adjusted as needed. Typically, units with multiple-deck screens are
used in production facilities. Screen surfaces are available in various types
and materials and with a variety of sizes and shapes of screen openings.

Washing

Aggregates may need to be washed during production to remove dust
generated during crushing. Depending on the nature of sand and gravel
deposits, washing may also be needed to remove silts, clays, and other
deleterious substances such as coal and soft or lightweight fragments.

The various types of washing equipment include high-pressure spray
nozzles, screw washers, log washers, and rotary scrubbers. Sprayers
mounted above the screens might adequately rinse some unwanted mate-
rials, such as dust, from aggregates. Other materials, such as more highly
plastic clays and cemented aggregates, may need the rigorous agitation of
a log washer.

Handling and Stockpiling

The handling and stockpiling of aggregates is an integral component of
aggregate processing for producing a quality product. The methods used
for the pickup or loading of material by shovels and loader buckets should
avoid segregation. Feed bins, chutes, gates, and conveyors should be sized
and operated in such a way that a proper and consistent rate of feed is
maintained to crushers, screens, and washers.

Each stage of conveying and storing aggregates must minimize
contamination, degradation, and segregation. Degradation can mean not
only the breakdown of aggregates into smaller pieces but also the creation
of dust. Excessive dropping of aggregates from conveyors, pushing of aggre-
gates with bulldozers, handling of aggregates by front-end loaders, and
buildup of stockpiles to excessive heights all contribute to degradation.

Segregation is the separation of larger particles from smaller particles,
leaving a nonhomogenous and unrepresentative blend of particle sizes.
Because movement and vibration cause segregation, every stage of han-
dling and processing can contribute to it. On conveyor belts and in haul
trucks, smaller particles migrate downward. More segregation takes place
at the conveyor end and as the material is dumped from the truckbed. In
these cases, larger particles flow away from the belt or the truckbed and
the finer particles drop more vertically and under the belt or truckbed
(see Figure 3.3). The worst segregation occurs in single-cone stockpiles
where material is dropped from a tall, stationary conveyor belt to build
the stockpile. Segregation can be controlled with better stockpiling tech-
niques, such as using radial stackers. This equipment allows the formation
of a continuous layered stockpile. Each layer will have segregation, but
it occurs in a more finite volume because load-out of the multiple layers
negates the individual-layer segregation. Another way to control or reduce
segregation is to screen aggregates into a greater number of fractions with
fewer particle sizes in each fraction.

Sampling

Material sampling is a key component of aggregate quality-control, yet
so often it is overlooked or taken for granted. It is imperative that samples
be obtained in such a manner that they are representative of the aggregate
product. Too often, samples are manually taken from the faces of coarse-
aggregate stockpiles where, due to the common segregation that occurs in
the building of stockpiles, the samples are not representative of the whole
pile but are assumed to be. In actuality, they represent only a small volume of
aggregate in the particular sample locations.

The methodologies of AASHTO T 2 (ASTM D 75) should be followed when
sampling aggregates. Typically, the best sampling locations are wherever the
full material flow-stream can be sampled, such as from discharge streams,
from bins or conveyor belt ends, or from the full cross section of conveyor-belt
surfaces. If sampling must be done from stockpiles, the procedures detailed
in the appendix of AASHTO T 2 should be followed.

Maximum Particle Size and Grading

Two key properties of an aggregate that go hand in hand are maximum
particle size and grading. ASTM D 8 defines the maximum size of an
aggregate as “the smallest sieve opening through which the entire amount
of aggregate is required to pass.” AASHTO MP 323 refers to the maximum
size of an aggregate for Superpave mixes only as “one size larger than the
nominal maximum aggregate size.”

AASHTO MP 323 defines it as “one sieve size larger than the first sieve to
retain more than 10 percent of the material.” Care must be exercised when
using and referring to the terms. For good paving results, the lift thickness
of HMA should be at least 2 to 2% times the maximum aggregate size in
the mix or 3 to 4 times the nominal maximum aggregate size in the mix.

The grading or gradation of an aggregate is the distribution of the vari-
ous particle sizes. Gradation is typically determined by both a washed
sieve analysis and a dry sieve analysis. ASTM C 136 (AASHTO T 27) details
the procedure for dry sieving.

In this procedure, the mass of a sample of dry aggregate is determined
and then the sample is passed through a series of sieves with progressively
smaller openings (see Figure 3.4). Once separated, the mass of material
retained on each sieve is measured and compared with the total sample
weight. The aggregate gradation is ultimately expressed as the cumulative
percentage passing each sieve size. Figure 3.5 gives an example of sieve
analysis data for an aggregate that contains both fine and coarse particles. A
graphical plot of the particle distribution is also shown.

Dry sieving alone may be satisfactory for general checks during aggregate
production but does not appropriately determine the amount of fine particles
smaller than the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. Therefore, before performing
the dry sieve analysis, a washed sieve analysis should be made as detailed
in AASHTO T 11 (ASTM C 117). They describe the procedure in this way:
“A sample of the aggregate is washed in a prescribed manner, using either
plain water or water containing a wetting agent, as specified. The decanted
wash water, containing suspended and dissolved material, is passed through
a 75 microns (No. 200) sieve. The loss in mass resulting from the wash treat-
ment is calculated as mass percent of the original sample. . . .”

Particle Shape and Surface Texture

Particle shape refers to the form and contour of the individual aggregate
particles (angular versus round, cubic versus flat or elongated). Surface texture
refers to the extent of roughness of the surface of the aggregate particles.

Although two separate characteristics—particle shape and surface texture
of aggregates—are often considered collectively in their roles in HMA
workability, compaction, and deformation resistance (see Figure 3.6), both
fine- and coarse-size fractions of crushed stone and gravel have irregular
or angular shapes as well as rougher surface textures. Natural Cuncrushed)
gravels and sands have generally rounded shapes and smoother textures.

Like particle shape, the surface texture influences the workability, com-
pactability, and strength of asphalt paving mixtures. A rough, sandpaper-
like surface texture (as opposed to a smooth glass-like surface) tends to
increase the strength of the paving mix and requires additional asphalt to
overcome the loss of workability. Voids in the compacted mineral aggre-
gate are also typically greater, a characteristic that provides additional
space for the required increase of asphalt.

Asphalt paving mixes that carry heavy traffic loads and are highly resistant
to rutting and deformation usually contain rough-textured, crushed, and
angular aggregates, both coarse and fine. These mixes require greater care
and effort to place and compact.

Many asphalt mixes in pavements for medium to low traffic contain
both angular, rough-textured aggregate particles and smoother, rounded
aggregate particles. The coarse aggregate fraction is usually crushed
stone or crushed gravel, and the fine aggregate is usually all natural sand
(rounded particles) or a combination with stone screenings. Such mixes
rely principally on crushed aggregate for strength and rounded sand par-
ticles for workability and ease of compaction.

Rounded particles, such as most natural gravels Cuncrushed) and sands
from streambeds, have been used successfully in light duty asphalt pave-
ments, such as parking lots and walking trails. However, because a higher
density is possible with round aggregate particles, the amount of asphalt
in the mix is usually a more critical factor in such dense-graded mixes.

Toughness

An aggregate’s toughness is measured by its ability to resist wear and
degradation. Aggregates are subject to additional crushing and abrasive
wear during manufacture, placing, and compaction of asphalt paving
mixes and so must exhibit a certain degree of toughness. Aggregates in
HMA layers at or near the pavement surface are also subject to abrasion
under traffic loads and require more toughness than aggregates in the
lower layers where loads have dissipated or are not as concentrated. Rela-
tively high resistance to wear, as indicated by a low percentage of abra-
sion loss, is a desirable characteristic of aggregates to be used in asphalt
pavement surface layers. Aggregates having higher abrasion losses, within

Pore Structure, Porosity, and Absorption

The pore structure of an aggregate refers to the characteristics and con-
figuration of voids in an aggregate particle. Pores or voids can be isolated
or encapsulated within aggregate particles and they are thus imperme-
able, or they can be interconnected and open to the surface, making them
permeable.

Porosity is the total volume of voids in the aggregate particle. Higher
porosity can relate to lesser aggregate strength and lower density and spe-
cific gravity. Absorption is the intake or permeation of liquid into the pores
of an aggregate particle. Higher porosity may not mean higher absorption
if most of the pores are impermeable. However, high absorption does
indicate a large volume of permeable voids.

An aggregate with permeable voids will absorb asphalt. Additional
asphalt must be incorporated into the paving mix to compensate for the
absorption of asphalt by the aggregate. This is noneffective asphalt. Such
aggregates usually are not used unless less-absorptive aggregates are not
available or the aggregates possess other qualities or properties that make
them desirable in spite of their high-absorption rate. Blast furnace slag and
many synthetic or manufactured aggregates are lightweight materials that
are also highly absorptive. Their lightness and wear-resistant properties
frequently outweigh the high-absorption rate consideration for pavement
construction use.

AASHTO T 85 (ASTM C 127) is commonly used to determine absorption
for coarse aggregates. For fine aggregates, AASHTO T 84 (ASTM C 128)
is the common test standard. In these tests, absorption is expressed. as
the amount of water as a percentage of the total dry aggregate mass that
penetrates into the pores only and does not include water that adheres
to the surface of the aggregate. The following section on specific gravity
provides a description of the two test procedures.

Specific Gravity

The specific gravity of an aggregate is the ratio of the weight of a unit
volume of material to the weight of the same volume of water at 73°F
(23°C). This property is primarily used in mix volumetric calculations for
voids determination. Also, bulk specific gravities (defined in the following
paragraphs) are used in the computations for adjusting quantities of the
aggregate components that are to be used in an HMA mix because of the
differing specific gravities of various aggregates.

The three generally accepted types of specific gravities for aggregate,
depending on the defined volume of the particle, are the following:

* Apparent specific gravity

° Bulk specific gravity

° Effective specific gravity

As illustrated in Figure 3.8, apparent specific gravity considers the volume
as being the volume of the aggregate itself. It does not include the volume
of any pores or capillaries that become filled with water after a 24-hour
soaking. Bulk specific gravity considers the overall volume of the aggregate
particle, including the pores that become filled with water after a 24-hour
soaking. The effective specific gravity considers the overall volume of the
aggregate exclusive of the volume of pores that absorbs asphalt. Whereas
bulk and apparent specific gravities can relate to individual aggregates or
combined aggregates, effective specific gravity relates exclusively to the
total combined aggregate structure in a mix of HMA.

Air-voids in the compacted asphalt pavement course appear throughout
the mix as small pockets of air between the asphalt-coated aggregate
particles. The air voids content cannot be directly measured but is
back-calculated from other test results. The choice of specific gravity for the
aggregate used in asphalt mix calculations can have a substantial effect on
the calculated amount of air-voids in the compacted mix. The actual specific
gravity of the aggregate in the mixture depends on the degree to which the
aggregate absorbs asphalt. When apparent specific gravity is used, asphalt
is assumed to be absorbed by all the water-permeable pores. If bulk spe-
cific gravity is used, it is assumed that the asphalt is not absorbed by the
water permeable pores. Except in rare cases, neither is true. The concept of
effective specific gravity more nearly approaches the correct value for deter-
mining the voids in a compacted asphalt paving mix. Bulk specific gravity
may be used, however, only if an allowance for the asphalt absorbed by the
aggregate is taken into account.

Equipment and procedures for determining the bulk and apparent specific
gravities of coarse aggregates are outlined in AASHTO T 85 (ASTM C 127).
Equipment and procedures for determining the apparent and bulk specific
gravities of fine aggregates are detailed in AASHTO T 84 (ASTM C 128).

Freeze-Thaw/Weathering Resistance

Freeze-thaw resistance is an aggregate’s ability to resist degradation
caused by cycles of freezing and thawing when the aggregate is in a satu-
rated condition. Internal expansive forces develop in the pores of the
aggregate each time the absorbed water transforms into ice. Weathering
resistance can include the freeze-thaw resistance factor, but it also refers to
the aggregates’ ability to withstand the volume changes and degradation
that occur with cyclic wetting and drying. An aggregate’s ability to resist
the effects of wetting and drying cycles and freezing and thawing cycles
can also be called the aggregate’s durability or soundness property.

In moist and freezing climates, aggregates must exhibit a certain level
of durability or soundness to resist deterioration in stockpiles in the
interim stage (after aggregate processing and before HMA production).
However, once the aggregates are introduced into HMA production, the
need for weathering durability is debatable. Moisture within the aggre-
gates is driven out in the dryer, and the aggregates receive a coating of
impermeable asphalt binder.

The test most commonly used to determine an aggregate’s durability
is the sulfate soundness test—AASHTO T 104 (ASTM C 88). In this test,
an aggregate sample is sieved into specified size fractions. The separated
fractions are then immersed for 16 to 18 hours in a saturated solution
of either sodium or magnesium sulfate for absorption of the solution
into the aggregate pores. The samples are then removed from the solu-
tion, allowed to drain for a short period, and then dried in an oven until
they reach constant mass. The samples are then cooled to room tempera-
ture, and the cycle of immersion and drying is repeated. Typically, five
cycles are specified. At the end of the required number of cycles, each
size fraction sample is washed to remove the salts, and then dried and
sieved over the specified screen. The mass loss of each fraction sample is
determined and expressed as a percentage loss. A weighted loss for the
composite aggregate is calculated based on the original aggregate grada-
tion. The applicability of this test to durability is given in ASTM C 88: “The
internal expansive force, derived from the rehydration of the salt upon
re-immersion, simulates the expansion of water on freezing.”

Typical standards and agency specifications set maximum allowable loss
values in the 10-20 percent range for test durations of five cycles.

Aggregate cleanliness is one way of describing the level of surface coatings
on an aggregate. Aggregate coatings can affect the bond or adhesion of binder
to the aggregate. Aggregate cleanliness of coarse aggregate is often determined
by visual inspection, but a washed sieve analysis generally provides positive
proof. The sand-equivalent test, developed by the California Division of High-
ways and described in AASHTO T 176 (ASTM D 2419), is the most common
method of assessing the level of cleanliness of fine aggregates by determining
the relative proportion of detrimental fine dust or claylike materials. The test
method summary describes the procedure: “A measured volume of soil or
fine aggregate and a small quantity of flocculating solution are poured into a
graduated plastic cylinder and are agitated to loosen the claylike coatings from
the sand particles in the test specimen. The specimen is then ‘irrigated’ using
additional flocculating solution forcing the claylike material into suspension
above the sand. After a prescribed sedimentation period, the height of floc-
culated clay is read and the height of sand in the cylinder is determined. The
sand equivalent is the ratio of the height of sand to the height of clay times
100.” Cleaner aggregates will have higher sand equivalent values. Common
specification minimum limits for this property range from 40 to 50.

Determination of liquid limit and plasticity index properties is another
method sometimes required by specifications for assessing the clay content in
fine aggregate. Procedures for determining these properties are contained in
AASHTO T 89 and T 90 (ASTM D 4318). These procedures define liquid limit
as “the water content, in percent, of a soil at the arbitrarily defined boundary
between the semi-liquid and plastic states.” Plasticity index is defined as “the
range of water content over which a soil behaves plastically.”

Samples for both the liquid limit and the plastic limit tests use the portion
of the material that passes the No. 40 (0.425 millimeter) sieve mixed with
varying amounts of water. In the liquid limit procedure, a prepared sample is
spread in a brass cup and cut with a grooving tool, forming a notch through
the sample. The brass cup is then repetitively raised and dropped until the
divided sample sections flow together. The liquid limit is the corresponding
water content at which the sample flowed together at 25 drops of the cup.

In order to determine the plasticity index, the plastic limit of the material
must be determined. According to the test method summary of D 4318, “the
plastic limit is determined by alternately pressing together and rolling into a
¥%-inch (3.2 millimeters) diameter thread a small portion of plastic soil until
its water content is reduced to a point at which the thread crumbles and can
no longer be pressed together and re-rolled. The water content of the soil at
this point is reported as the plastic limit. . .. The plasticity index is calculated
as the difference between the liquid limit and the plastic limit.”

3.2 Composite Aggregate

Analysis

3.2.1 Introduction

HMA is normally composed of a blend of individual aggregates that make
up the composite aggregate structure, or matrix, of the mix. These individual
aggregates are stockpiled separately, are typically different in size and grada-
tion, and might even come from different sources. Particularly during the
processing stages, it is important that the individual aggregate components
possess certain qualities. It is also important that the composite of the blended
aggregates possess key characteristics critical to the HMA. These qualities and
characteristics are reflected in most specifications that stipulate requirements
for both the composite aggregates and the individual aggregate components.
One of the key characteristics of the composite aggregate is gradation.

A uniformly graded aggregate structure consists of particles that are
primarily of one size or their range of sizes is very narrow. The amount
of particle-to-particle contact is low, and the interlock of the aggregates is
low. The amount of voids between the particles is high; thus, permeability of
the aggregate structure is high. Uniformly graded aggregates are not usually
desired in asphalt mixtures.

Some asphalt mixes have an aggregate structure that is called open-graded.
This is actually a uniformly graded aggregate structure with a very small
amount of fine-aggregate particles. Open-graded mixes are most often used
for their characteristics of high permeability and high surface friction. Surface
courses made with these mixes are also called “porous friction courses.”

A well-graded aggregate structure has an extensive distribution of particle
sizes ranging from fine to coarse. The smaller aggregate particles fill in gaps
and voids between larger particles. The grading is considered dense in that
the concentration of aggregate particles is high and the amount of voids
between aggregate particles is small. Permeability of the aggregate structure
is low, and interlock between aggregate particles is high. Paving mixtures
with such aggregate structures may be easily compacted but tend to tightly
pack and have air-void contents that are too low.

Gap-graded aggregates refer to the gradation characteristic in which there
is a “gap” in the particle-size distribution. There is a range of particle size
fractions (typically the mid-size range) that is either devoid of particles or
the amount of particles in that range is low. Gap-graded aggregates are
not as dense in particle concentration as well-graded aggregates. In turn,
the amount of voids between aggregate particles and the permeability are
higher than that of well-graded aggregates. Many times, asphalt mixtures
must necessarily have the greater voids content of a gap-graded aggregates
so that the mix can contain an adequate amount and coating thickness of
asphalt binder. Gap-graded aggregate structures typically have good inter-
lock between particles.

Two other dense-gradation types that are often mentioned are “coarse-
graded” or “fine-graded.” Fine-graded aggregate structures usually contain a
greater amount of fine aggregate, generally resulting in HMA mixtures with
higher asphalt content, a lesser compactive effort needed during placement,
a more closed-surface texture, and an increased potential for surface defor-
mation. Coarse-graded aggregate structures, on the other hand, contain a
lesser amount of fine aggregate. HMA mixtures with a coarse grading tend
to have a lower asphalt content, require more compactive effort, have a more
open-surface texture, and have a lower potential for surface deformation.

As discussed in the previous section, the various types of aggregate grada-
tions each create different characteristics in asphalt mixtures and each can
serve a different purpose. In addition to grading type, the maximum size
of the aggregate is critical in paving mixtures because it also affects key

The combined percentages (P, in equation 1) for each sieve size should

grading. None of the percentages should fall outside the specified grad-

optimum combination is achieved when the blended or composite per-
centages match as closely as possible to the original desired percentages.
If the blended gradation falls outside the specifications, the responsible
proportion in the blend should be modified, with the other proportions
also modified to total 100 percent. Sometimes, it is necessary to delete
a proposed aggregate and obtain replacement material if a satisfactory
blend cannot be developed with the aggregate initially suggested.

Mathematical methods have been developed that will directly determine
an “optimum” combination of aggregates. To accomplish this calculation,
various optimization techniques have been used, such as the closest to the
middle of the specification range. The speed of these methods varies with
the number of stockpiles being blended. Many designers use computer
spreadsheet programs to quickly evaluate a number of alternative blends.
The Asphalt Institute’s SW-2, Mix Design Program computer program can
also be used to visually evaluate the gradation plot of numerous blends
very rapidly. Regardless of which method is used, a trial-and-error approach
guided by reasoning, is usually the easiest and best procedure to determine
and refine a satisfactory combination of aggregates. A computer-generated
blend should not be used without further evaluation and checking.

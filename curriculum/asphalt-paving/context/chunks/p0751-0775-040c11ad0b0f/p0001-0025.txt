Travel plants are self-propelled pugmill plants that mix the aggregates
with asphalt, which is applied at a controlled rate, as they move along the

road. One type of travel plant has an aggregate receiving hopper, an asphalt
storage tank, pugmill mixer, and floating screed. Recycled material, virgin
aggregate, or a blend of the two can be fed (by truck or low-lift loader from
the windrow) into the plant for mixing with asphalt (Figure 15.4). The com-

Travel plants should be capable of proportioning and thoroughly mixing

The asphalt application rate for the windrow pickup plant must be accu-

essary with a motor grader. This ensures that all the windrowed material
is incorporated into the mix. It also aerates the mixture. The number of
passes with the motor grader varies with job conditions.

Travel-plant mixing offers the advantage of achieving closer control of
the mixing operation than is possible with blade mixing. The proportion-
ing of the asphalt with the aggregate, as well as the uniformity with which
the two can be incorporated, is of great importance.

Stationary Mixing Facilities

Mixes using emulsified and cutback asphalt prepared at a stationary mix-
ing facility offer certain advantages over road-mixing methods. Weather
conditions are not as much a factor in a stationary mixing facility. Mixing
that would otherwise require hours of windrow manipulation is accom-
plished in a few minutes.

The stationary mixing facility provides good control over both the pro-
portions of materials in the mix and the mixing time. Uniformity of mixes
is practically assured. Processing in a central plant allows a more viscous
asphalt to be used. Often, enough aeration has been accomplished in the
mixing operation—dumping and spreading mix in the roadway—to permit
immediate compaction with rolling equipment.

Often, these advantages make facility-mixing methods more economical
for producing and placing mixes in the roadway than road-mixing methods.

Cold mix facility setups may vary depending on the quality and the
type of mix being produced. At the very least, the setup should consist of
a mixer, an asphalt storage tank, an asphalt metering pump, piping and
spray bar equipment, a spray bar for feeding water and additives, con-
trols for adjusting and monitoring the various components, a conveyor
for feeding the aggregate, and a power source. It may also include one or
more aggregate bins, proportioning aggregate feeders, a scalping screen,
an aggregate load-sensing device, and a surge bin or storage silo. Batch-
type pugmills can be used. However, this mix production is ideally suited
for continuous mixers, which are used almost exclusively.

The production of high quality cold mixes for heavy duty pavements
requires a well controlled facility setup to ensure success. In addition
to carefully monitored and controlled blending of aggregate, asphalt,
and in some cases water, the mixer should be a type that permits
variation in mixing time. Surge hoppers or storage silos are highly desir-
able because they minimize facility shutdowns and improve mix unifor-
mity. Good stockpiling and loader techniques can also be used in lieu of
surge bins or silos. A typical cold mix facility is shown in Figure 15.5. The
steps in the cold mix design procedure are shown in Figure 15.6.

Aeration/Spreading/Compaction

Aeration of open-graded aggregate mixtures is usually not critical.
However, as the presence of fine aggregate increases and the mix becomes
dense-graded, ample aeration is necessary. Proper. aeration for cutback
asphalt is usually reached when the volatiles have been reduced to about
50 percent of that contained in the original asphalt and the moisture content
does not exceed 2 percent by weight of the total mixture. For emulsified
asphalt mixtures, the moisture content should be reduced to not more than
2-5 percent by weight of the total mix. In the case of sands and sandy soils
used as a base course and surfaced within 30 days, volatiles in the mix
should be reduced by at least two-thirds.

The point at which adequate aeration is achieved is often based on
experience and judgment. Experience with the particular mixture being
prepared and trial rolling have practical value for determining the pres-
ence of moisture or volatiles when test data are not immediately avail-
able. (AASHTO T 110) Standard Method of Test for Moisture or Volatile
Distillates in Bituminous Mixtures can also be used. This test uses a metal
still (similar to the one used for the distillation of emulsified asphalt),
condenser, and a glass trap. A light solvent is added to a sample of the
uncompacted mix in the still and then heated.

off the end of the plow board. If the material displays sheen, the mix has
not had adequate aeration. Once the material displays a dull or flat color,

Mixtures that have been windrowed by a travel plant or by blade mix-
ing are spread and shaped to the desired cross section by successive
passes with a motor grader. Mixtures blended by rotary cross-shaft mixers,
although in place on the roadway, generally require additional shaping.

Mixtures that do not require aeration may be spread to the required
thickness immediately after mixing. Mixtures that require aeration are
generally deposited upon the roadbed in a windrow. Because of a ten-
dency to leave a hump in the road when blade spreading from a centerline
windrow, it is better to move the windrow to the side before spreading.
Blade spreading should be done in successive layers, with no layer thinner
than three times the maximum particle size.

Construction should not continue during rainfall, nor should it begin when

the ambient temperature is below 50°F (10°C). Emulsified-asphalt mixes
gain stability as the fluids (mainly water) evaporate. It is important not to
retard this process. Therefore, lift thickness must be limited by the rate at
which the mixture loses its fluids. The type of emulsified asphalt, the water
content of the mix, the gradation and temperature of the aggregate, wind
velocity, ambient temperature, and humidity all affect evaporation.

Although each job has its own combination of these factors, experience
has shown that under the best conditions, a dense-graded mix should be
placed in compacted thicknesses no greater than 3 inches (75 millimeters).
Open-graded mix may be placed in layers up to 6 inches (150 millime-
ters). When multiple lifts are required, some curing time must be allowed
between successive lifts. Curing time varies with the rate of evaporation.
However, a second lift can usually be placed after two to five days under
good curing conditions.

A delay in initial rolling may be necessary, especially with dense-graded
mixes. Rolling seals the pavement as it reduces the voids in the mix. Pre-
mature rolling retards evaporation of the water in the mix and greatly
extends the time required for the mix to reach required density. The mix
must be allowed to develop sufficient cohesion to support the rollers.
If rolling is delayed too long, however, compaction will be difficult. In
some cases, the developing asphalt-aggregate bond will be irreversibly
broken, therefore, timing is important. Rollers may be selected as indi-
cated in Table 15.6.

Because the tires of a motor grader compact the freshly spread mix,
the tracks will appear as ruts in the finished mat unless there is adequate
rolling between each successive lift. Therefore, the roller should follow
directly behind the motor grader in order to eliminate wheel tracks.

If the asphalt mixture exhibits undue rutting or shoving at any time
during compaction, rolling should be stopped. Compaction should not be
attempted until there is a reduction in moisture content, either naturally
or by mechanical aeration.

After one lift is thoroughly compacted and cured, another lift may be
placed on it. The operation should be repeated as necessary to bring the
road to required grade and cross section. For a smooth riding surface,
the motor grader should be used to trim and level as the rollers complete
compaction of the lift.

After the mat is shaped to the final cross section, it is rolled with a steel-
wheel roller until the roller marks are eliminated.

15.2 Hydraulic Structures

Asphalt has been used in hydraulic applications for several thousand
years. Archaeological excavations show that asphalt was used as water-
proofing material for temple baths, aqueducts, and water tanks as long as
5,000 years ago.

The use of asphalt for the control, conservation, and storage of water
has expanded in past decades.

Areas of growing importance include the following:

e Water conservation, to meet the increasing demand for water supplies

° Irrigation, to open up new lands for cultivation

¢ Flood control, to protect lives and investments in personal property

and industry

° Beach erosion, to preserve beaches and protect waterfront property

15.2.1 Application/Uses for Hydraulic
Structures

The following list is a compilation of some applications and uses of
asphalt in hydraulic structures.

° Breakwater—a structure protecting a shore area, harbor, anchorage,
or basin from waves

° Canal—a watercourse cut through a land area and used for either
irrigation or navigation

° Catch basin, pond, or reservoir—a large, shallow holding area
designed to collect rainwater. It usually discharges the collected water
into a storage tank (see Figure 15.7)

¢ Dam—an artificial barrier of earth, rock, masonry, or other material
across a channel to impound water or to obstruct its flow

¢ Dike—a wall or mound built around an area to impound water or to
prevent flooding. If used to protect from flooding, it is usually called
a levee

° Embankmeni—an artificial bank, mound, or dike built for a specific
purpose, such as to hold backwater, or to carry a roadway

° Groin—a shore-protective structure (such as a pier or structure of
stone, rubble) extending out from and perpendicular to the shoreline
to trap lateral drift sand and retard shore erosion, or to protect a har-
bor or to influence water currents or tides

° Levee—a dike or embankment to protect a land area from flooding

e Sanitary landfill—an area of land designed for the disposal of refuse
in accordance with sound engineering methods whereby the refuse

Asphalt Materials and their Functions

Many types of asphalt materials are used in hydraulic applications. Each
type can be classified in one of the following distinct categories:

° Impermeable asphalt mixes

° Porous asphalt mixes

° Asphalt mastics

° Asphalt cement

These materials can be used in various forms to perform one or more of
the following functions:

° To waterproof a structure

° To protect a structure

° To reinforce a structure

Table 15.7 shows how each type of asphalt material may be used to
perform these functions.

Porous Asphalt Mixes

There are two types of porous mixes: permeable and open-graded. Per-
meable asphalt mixes are used to line reservoirs that are made watertight
by compacted layers of earth or clay. The asphalt linings protect the earth
embankment from erosion and at the same time permit the free flow of
water through the lining, thereby eliminating the danger of hydrostatic
back pressures developing under the lining. Open-graded mixes are used
to provide drainage layers beneath watertight linings. Drainage layers are
designed to dispose of seepage or leakage that may occur in the water-
tight lining. They are also used to drain away groundwater seepage that
would otherwise build up hydrostatic pressures underneath the watertight
lining. Open-graded mixes, in addition to providing a subsurface drainage
layer, add to the stability and overall strength of the lining.

Asphalt Seals

An asphalt membrane lining consists of a continuous layer of asphalt
cement sprayed over the surface being waterproofed. It is usually covered
with a layer of soil, gravel, or other material to hold the lining in place,
to prevent weathering (oxidation) of the asphalt, and to protect it from
mechanical damage. The asphalt is usually sprayed in two applications at a
total rate of about 1% gallons per square yard (6.8 liter per square meter) to

15.2.2 Hydraulic Mix Design Requirements

There are certain basic requirements for HMA mixes to be used for
waterproofing hydraulic structures. They must be workable while placing,
and impermeable, flexible, and stable in service. To ensure impermeability
(ess than 1 X 107 centimeters per second), the void content of an asphalt
concrete lining should not exceed 4 percent. A compaction level of 96
percent of the maximum specific gravity is required.

The lining will usually be placed on a sloped surface. Therefore, the mix-
ture should be quite workable to allow efficient placement by both mechani-
cal and hand methods. Usually, the desired compaction must be achieved
using lighter compaction equipment. While operating on the slope, cables are
used to winch the rollers up and down.

The lining should be flexible and able to withstand, without cracking,
long-term loading stresses caused by settlement in the supporting sub-
grade or foundation. On the other hand, it must also be resilient under
short-term loading, such as wave impact.

Increased asphalt content helps satisfy the foregoing design require-
ments. However, after a certain point, increased asphalt content results
in decreased stability. Although this mix does not need the high stability
usually associated with highway paving mixes, it should be stable enough
to support its own weight on relatively steep slopes while hot. In addition,
it should not creep after the lining has been compacted.

The design procedure consists of selecting the grade of asphalt cement,

mixes should be one grade higher on the high-temperature side, when
compared with a normal highway binder grade in that climate. For exam-

reservoirs. This relatively more viscous asphalt cement produces linings
with added resistance to the destructive action of water, the growth of
vegetation, and the extremes of weather. Linings made with this grade
of asphalt cement are more stable on side slopes than linings made with
the softer grades, yet they retain sufficient flexibility to conform to slight
deformations of the subgrade.

The asphalt content will usually range from 6 to 9 percent, which is
somewhat higher than the asphalt content of average roadway paving
mixes. The asphalt content is limited, however, by the ability of the mix to
resist flow down the slope at placement temperatures.

The aggregate used should be sound and have good adhesion
characteristics in the presence of water. The maximum aggregate particle
size should be limited to one-third to two-fifths of the finished thick-
ness of the paving course to ensure uniform density throughout the layer.
For mineral filler, crushed limestone dust is generally preferred. However,
other inert mineral dusts having satisfactory performance records may be
considered. Suggested mix compositions for asphalt concrete for imper-

As a final check, a test or trial strip is recommended. It should be placed
under the same conditions as will be experienced in the field. Careful
observation of the behavior of the designed mix during the spreading and
compacting operation might indicate slight adjustments in the job-mix
formula that could not be detected in the laboratory.

Permeable Surface Mix Composition

Permeable asphalt mixes for hydraulic structures are characterized by the
absence or reduced amount of fine aggregate or sand in the mix. As a conse-
quence, the asphalt content is also reduced. The mixes have interconnected
voids that permit passage of water. The void content of these mixes must be
a minimum of 18 percent to assure easy passage of water through the mix.

A harder, or more viscous, grade of asphalt cement is desirable in these
mixes to allow sufficient film thickness and to prevent drainage from the
aggregate during construction. This choice of asphalt also provides addi-
tional cohesion in the mix between the aggregate particles.

The selected grade should be two PG grades higher on the high-temperature
side compared with a normal highway binder grade in that climate. For instance,
instead of using a PG 64-22, a PG 76-22 should be considered.

Sub-liner Drainage Layers

Porous drainage layers, with higher void content than a permeable lin-
ing, serve as drainage layers under an impermeable lining while at the
same time contributing to the structural strength of the lining. In either
case, the purpose is to provide free drainage to prevent hydrostatic pres-
sures from building up in the embankment or within the lining itself. As
a surface lining, water is allowed to flow to and from the embankment
through the lining. As a drainage layer, the subsurface water should be
collected and carried off by drains.

To be effective, porous drainage layers must have 20-22 percent void
contents to assure proper drainage of water through the layer.

Asphalt binder selection and content is accomplished with the same
procedures used for permeable mixes. The suggested mix composition for
the porous layers is listed in column E of Table 15.8.

15.2.3 Construction Considerations

impermeable Surface/Lining

Hydraulic linings of asphalt concrete should be prepared and placed in
accordance with established construction practices. However, some spe-
cial aspects of construction are mentioned here.

Asphalt concrete linings are placed on side slopes, on curved sections, in
angles and corners, and on level surfaces. Conventional highway spread-
ers, modified pavers or spreaders, spreader boxes, special slip-form pav-
ers, hand methods, or some combination of these methods may do this.
In most instances, conventional highway spreaders are used. They are
often modified as necessary for slope operations. When side-slope paving
and floor paving are separate operations, experience has shown that best
results are obtained when the slopes of the reservoir or channel are paved
before paving the floor.

Conventional highway spreaders, (sometimes winched up the slope), usu-
ally perform spreading on side slopes; often they are modified to facilitate
the work. In some cases, especially with large shallow reservoirs, asphalt
mixes are spread with specially designed finishers operating on rails within
a steel-frame structure up and down the slope. Also, side slopes of shallow
reservoirs have been paved with pavers moving along the slope longitudi-
nally. During the operation, the paver is held with cables attached to equip-
ment operating on the crest. This method presents compaction problems,
however, because rollers operating this way apply nonuniform rolling
pressures and the drum tends to cut into the mix on the downslope side.

When a spreader operates up and down the slope, a sloped ramp at the
crest may be used to permit the paver to pave all the way to the top. Cable
winches attached to the ramp framework raise and lower the paver on the
slope. Upon completion of a paved strip with the paver on the ramp, the
entire assembly is then moved over for the next paving pass.

When the paving operation proceeds down the slope, the ramp is not nec-
essary. Recently, this has become the preferred method of paving the slope.

Several methods can be used to supply the paver with hot mix for slope
paving. Probably the most common is to load the paver at the bottom of
the slope with enough mix for one complete trip up the slope. Another is
to load the paver at the top of the slope and then lower it to the bottom
for the paving pass. When slope distances are too great for paving a strip
without recharging, a front-end loader with a side-dump bucket has been
used to supply the paver partway up the slope. Cranes have also been suc-
cessfully used to supply the paver on the slope. Material transfer vehicles at
the bottom of the slope, feeding conveyors equipped with belting material
approved for handling hot materials, have also been used successfully.

Light steel-wheel rollers and vibratory steel-wheel rollers are often used
for compacting the freshly spread mix on the slope. Power winches and
cables are usually required for maneuvering the roller up and down the
slope. The rolling is done independently of the paving, but it should begin
as soon as possible without interfering with the paver. The initial rolling
pass is usually made up the slope. Depending on the stability of the mix, the
vibrator is not desired on the initial pass but on the following coverages.

When the lining is to be placed in two or more courses, the paving
joints should be staggered or offset. Emphasis should be on a tight bond
for impermeability.

A tack coat is usually required whenever an asphalt mix is placed over
another paved surface. For bonding impermeable asphalt layers together, a
tack coat of SS-1, SS-Ih, CSS-1, or CSS-1h emulsified-asphalt diluted 1:1 with
water and applied at a rate of 0.05-0.15 gallons per square yard (0.2-0.7 liter
per square meter) over the surface to be paved will usually be adequate. Tack
coats of emulsified asphalt should be allowed to cure fully before paving;
otherwise moisture will be trapped between the two layers at construction.

Tack coats can be critical when used to bond two impermeable layers
together. An excessive tack coat application combined with warm tem-
peratures can cause a slippage plane between two layers on a sloped
surface. On the other hand, unless the two layers are bonded together
so that there are no voids or pores in the interface, seepage water from
a crack or opened joint may get between the layers. If this happens,
hydrostatic pressures can develop and eventually cause layer separation.

Permeable Surface/Lining

Essentially, the procedures for placing permeable surface lining mixes
are the same as those for impermeable asphalt mixes. However, they are
generally placed in one layer 3-6 inches (7.5-15 centimeters) thick, rather
than in two or more courses.

There is no real effort to obtain density in open-graded drainage layer
courses; the intent is only to firmly seat the aggregate particles. Exces-
sive rolling can be harmful because of aggregate degradation or by the
breaking of the asphalt bond between particles, particularly if the mix
temperature is below 150°F (66°C). For this reason, generally, only a static
steel-wheel roller is used.

Porous Drainage Layer

The same construction for permeable linings is used for porous drain-
age layers, with the expectation that the mixing temperature at the hot mix
facility should not exceed 250°F (121°C). Higher temperatures may cause
the asphalt to drain from the aggregate particles and settle in the bottom of
the truck bed as it is hauled to the paving site. This problem is not usually
encountered with mixes such as mix composition D.

Asphait seals

An asphalt seal may be applied to an asphalt surface for a number of
reasons. It may be designed to make the surface more watertight, or to
protect it from abrasion by waves, water currents, or even by ice. It may
also be used to protect the surface from mud curl or the curling of drying
algae along the waterline, or to reduce the rate of oxidation of the exposed
asphalt surface. These seals are generally of two types.

° Sprayed asphalt sealers/asphalt cement. (It should be noted that
emulsified asphalt or cutback asphalt cannot be used as a seal for
reservoirs that are used for drinking water, to avoid the residual
emulsifying agent or cutter stock from contaminating the water.) The
asphalt should be sprayed over the surface of an asphalt lining at the
rate of 0.25 gallons per square yard (1 liter per square meter). This
will provide a film coating as much as 0.04 inch (1 millimeter) thick.
A continuous film coating will fill and seal any exposed pores and
increase the water-tightness of the asphalt lining. It will also tend to
fill and seal small cracks in the surface that may have been caused by
improper rolling procedures in compacting the lining.

The surface should be clean, dry, and free from loose material.
A sloped surface usually necessitates hand spraying. This should be
done in a back and forth sweeping motion to build up the film and to
keep the asphalt from flowing down the slope.

° Asphalt mastic seals. Asphalt mastics applied to the surfaces of
asphalt linings provide water proofing and extra surface protection
from mechanical abuse. Asphalt mastics permit a heavier coating than
sprayed asphalt applications, and well-designed mixtures can make
the surface resistant to abrasion by waves, or scouring by waterborne
sands.

15.3 HMA Utilization in
Railway Track Beds

15.3.1 Introduction

Since deregulation in 1980, the U.S. freight railroad industry has expe-
rienced unprecedented growth in traffic volumes, revenue ton-miles, and
wheel loadings. This growth has necessitated the application of new
technologies to provide stronger and longer-lasting track and support
structures.

The railroad companies and the asphalt paving industry have developed
designs and applications for utilizing a support layer of HMA to strengthen
the track structure. HMA is being used in new mainline tracks, yards, and
terminals. It is also being used as a solution to specific trackbed (mainte-
nance) instability problems in existing trackbeds. These include the recon-
struction of turnouts, railroad crossings, bridge approaches, hump tracks,
tunnel floors and approaches, highway crossings, and loading facilities
where conventional trackbed designs have not performed satisfactorily.
The use of HMA in railway track beds results in an optimal track modulus,
which significantly reduces the rail and rolling stock wear.

Two designs are used to incorporate HMA in the track structure as
depicted in Figures 15.8 and 15.9. The most common design used on
U.S. railroads is known as HMA underlayment in which the HMA layer is
placed directly on select subgrade or old roadbed and the typical layer of
ballast is subsequently placed between the HMA layer and the ties. This
design requires little change from normal trackbed designs because the
HMA layer merely replaces the typical granular subballast layer.

The HMA layer is typically 5-8 inches (125-200 millimeters) thick and
12-14 feet (3.7—4.3 meters) wide. It is similar to a paved lane of a highway,
but is contained within the track structure to form a structural layer or
hardpan between the ballast and subgrade or existing roadbed.

The HMA overlayment design involves placing the HMA layers in a similar

15.3.2 Desirable Benefits and Applications
of HMA Underlayment

An HMA underlayment has the following benefits:

e A strengthened track-support layer below the ballast to uniformly
distribute reduced pressures to the roadbed (subgrade)

e A waterproofing layer and confinement to the underlying roadbed to
provide consistent load-carrying capability for track structures even
on roadbeds of marginal quality

° An impermeable layer to divert water to side ditches, essentially
eliminating roadbed (subgrade) moisture fluctuations, to consistently
maintain adequate underlying support

° A consistently high level of confinement for the ballast to provide
high shear strength and uniform pressure distribution

° A resilient layer between the ballast and roadbed to reduce the like-
lihood of subgrade pumping without substantially increasing track
stiffness

e An all-weather, uniformly stable surface to place the ballast and track
superstructure

Track sites deemed most applicable for realizing the benefits of an HMA

underlayment are those having heavy freight traffic and/or high-speed
passenger traffic, where one or more of the following conditions exist or
are expected:

15.3.3 Prevailing Design Practices

Recommended ideal HMA mix specifications, trackbed section designs,
and application practices have steadily evolved over the years, reflecting
refinements from initial recommendations. Slight variations from the rec-
ommended mix designs and construction techniques are typical and do
not appear to adversely affect HMA trackbed performance.

The HMA mix, which is considered to impart the ideal properties to the
track structure, is a low modulus (plastic) mix, having design air voids of
1-3 percent. This mix will easily compact to less than 5 percent air voids.
This has been accomplished by specifying the local dense-graded highway
base mix having a maximum aggregate size of 1.0-1.5 inches (25-37 mil-
limeters). Ideally, the asphalt binder content can be increased by about
0.5 percent above optimum for highway applications because rutting and
bleeding are not concerns in the insulated trackbed environment.

Long-term monitoring and testing of in-service trackbeds indicate that this
low-voids, impermeable mix undergoes minimal oxidation from the effects
of air and water. It is also isolated from extreme temperature fluctuation
within the insulated trackbed environment. This isolation provides a layer
with reasonably consistent stiffness in hot weather but slightly resilient in
cold weather, thus eliminating any chance for the mix to rut and bleed in hot
weather or crack in cold weather. A long life for the mix is thus ensured.

Tests on subgrade/roadbed samples, obtained directly from under the
HMA layer, indicate that the in situ moisture contents are very close to opti-
mum values for maximum density of the respective materials. For struc-
tural design analyses, it is reasonable to assume base support strength or
bearing capacity values at optimum conditions for the material under the
HMA layer.

The predominant HMA layer width is 12-14 feet (3.7—4.3 meters) for
open track, but the width is wider under special trackwork, such as turn-
outs, to provide support under the longer ties.

HMA layer thickness varies depending on the quality of the roadbed
(subgrade) support and traffic loadings. A layer 5 to 6 inches (125-150
millimeters) thick is normally used for average conditions. For unusu-
ally poor roadbed support conditions and high-impact areas, a minimum
thickness of 8 inches (200 millimeters) is used. Ballast thickness usually is
6-12 inches (150-300 millimeters).

The HMA layer should extend several feet (meters) beyond the ends
of special trackwork so that subsequent track surfacing operations and
any impact from track stiffness changes will not infringe on the area. The
roadbed should be reasonably well-compacted, well-drained, and capable
of accommodating the hauling and spreading equipment without exces-
sive rutting or deformation. A slight crown or side slope is desirable. The
need for sub-surface drainage or roadbed support improvements, before
placing the HMA, will depend on site-specific conditions.

The structural design of railway trackbeds containing HMA underlay-
ment can be performed using KENTRACK, a finite element computer
program available through the University of Kentucky Civil Engineering
website. Asphalt Institute’s Research Report RR 84-1 covers the use of this
program and the design of railway systems utilizing HMA underlayments
as an alternative to the traditional ballast systems. The primary governing
factor is limiting the vertical compressive stresses, or permanent deforma-
tion, on the subgrade. For the HMA layer, the tensile strains at the bottom
of the HMA layer control its service life. Damage analyses are conducted to
predict the service life of the trackbed components for various combina-
tions of traffic, tonnages, subgrade support, and component layer compo-
sitions and thicknesses.

15.3.4 Typical Installation Practices

The construction of new rail lines and extensions, classification yards,
and intermodal terminals represent ideal conditions for HMA underlayment.
These are usually large projects with exposed subgrades that are readily
accessible to conventional asphalt paving equipment for placing the HMA
layer before placing the ballast and track. Typical highway paving technol-
ogy can be used. A typical paving view on the Burlington Northern-Santa
Fe Railway's double-tracking of its transcontinental mainline in Southern
Kansas is shown in Figure 15.10. This project involves approximately 200
miles (320 kilometers) of asphalt underlayment. The HMA is being placed in
two lifts, with a total compacted thickness of 6 inches (150 millimeters).

New intermodal yards use HMA for all or portions of new track con-
struction. A particular advantage is the waterproofing characteristics of the
HMA layer and the positive drainage systems that can be utilized in the
design of the loading area. Also, better construction accessibility reduces
the construction time required.

15.4.1 Types of Facilities

Almost every conceivable type of athletic or recreational facility can use
asphalt in some way—from running tracks (see Figure 15.12) to walk-
ing paths to tennis courts (see Figure 15.13) to playgrounds to football
fields. A wide selection of all-weather asphalt construction is economi-

constructed, maintenance costs are negligible. These surfaces are designed
and constructed differently than a typical roadway or parking lot; when
developing these types of facilities, a qualified consultant with experi-
ence in the particular type of facility (walking or running track, tennis
court, etc.) should be engaged. Also, it is suggested that the contractor
have experience with the construction of the type of facility being built.

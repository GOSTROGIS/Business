The measured surface deflections are used as input to a back calculation

5.5 Drainage of Asphalt

Pavement Structures

5.3.1 Introduction

Adequate drainage of the pavement structure is considered the most
important element in ensuring long-term pavement performance. The
proper design of the drainage system for a particular pavement is of para-
mount importance. This section of the manual is a brief overview of the
issues to be considered in designing pavement drainage. For additional
details, consult MS-15, Drainage of Asphalt Pavements or the resources
listed at the end of the chapter.

Reason for Drainage

Highway engineers recognize the critical need for good drainage in
designing and constructing pavements. Probably no other feature is as
important in determining the ability of a pavement to withstand the effects
of weather and traffic, and in providing trouble-free service over long
periods of time. Reducing the moisture content of the granular base and
subgrade through the use of effective drainage directly affects fatigue
cracking of the HMA surfaces, and thus the life of the pavement.

Water in a subgrade is usually slow to evaporate or drain. Even in dry
weather, the subgrade soils may remain damp or wet indefinitely. Often, voids
in porous materials become subterranean reservoirs unless they are drained.

With a few exceptions, most subgrade soils contain silt and clay, which
weaken when wet. This weakening may be hastened by vibrations from traf-
fic. If the subgrade is not properly shaped and sloped for drainage, water
may collect at the interface of the granular base or subbase and the subgrade
soils. As this water saturates the subgrade, the subgrade soils tend to lose sta-
bility and thus the strength required to support the loads on the pavement.
Under traffic, wet soil may be worked into a fine-grained slurry that pushes
up into voids in the granular base or subbase course, thus destroying the
grain-to-grain interlock and weakening the granular materials.

Design
In developing the various features of a drainage system, consideration
should be given to the following principal purposes:
1. To collect and drain water from the pavement surface
2. To collect subsurface water in the granular layers and keep the
subgrade moisture content as low as possible
3. To prevent or retard embankment erosion
4. To intercept water from surrounding areas and carry it from
the area
5. To lower the groundwater table

Drainage problems are caused by either surface flow or subsurface flow.
Each requires separate analysis and treatment.

Surface drainage includes the disposal of all water present on the pave-
ment surface and nearby ground. If possible, surface water should be
prevented from entering the subdrainage system. Inevitably, some water
enters the pavement structure, but may be kept to a minimum by surface
maintenance procedures (e.g., crack sealing, etc.).

Subsurface drainage deals with water in the granular layers and in the
subgrade soils beneath the pavement. Such water may enter the pavement
through open joints, cracks, and other openings in the surface, through
adjacent porous material, or from the ground below.

Subsurface water may be present in several forms, including these:

° Water that is free to flow under the force of gravity, referred to as “free

water”

° Water that moves by capillary forces, referred to as “capillary water”

Important differences exist between surface and subsurface drainage.
Surface runoff is usually a direct result of rainfall or melted snow. Water
flow to a subdrain depends entirely on the permeability of the soil and
the pavement courses through which the water must pass. The maximum
flow of surface water into a subdrain usually occurs within hours of a
rainfall event. In contrast, the maximum flow into a subdrain from ground-
water sources may occur several hours or even days after rainfall events.
Design requirements in the first case are based on the amount of rain or
melted snow and, in the second case, on the permeability of the soil or
aggregate.

5.3.2 Surface Drainage

Design for Roadways

Protecting pavement from the action of surface water is most easily
accomplished with full-width asphalt paving. This means that the traffic
lanes and the shoulders are paved with HMA to provide an impervious
cover that prevents surface water from directly entering the pavement struc-
ture during rainfall. The pavement surface should be free from holes and
cracks, have a permanently tight joint with the shoulder or gutter, and be
shaped and sloped (crowned) for adequate surface runoff. Surface water
usually is collected at the edge of a paved surface in ditches or gutters,
which discharge the runoff by gravity flow to a location remote from the
pavement. To prevent erosion on embankments, water should be directed
to spillways by way of curbs or dikes constructed on the extreme edge of
the shoulder. In addition, if either the volume or the velocity of the flowing
water is expected to be excessive, these features can be constructed using
asphalt lining, or other impermeable materials, to reduce the potential for
erosion.

For urban sections, the paved surface, including shoulders, is crowned
and typically drained by a curb and gutter system leading to catch basins,
which carry the surface flow to a system of storm drains. If asphalt gut-
ters are used, it is usually possible to tack the surfaces to ensure that no
leakage occurs at the joint between the shoulder and the gutter/curb.
Where concrete curb and gutter is used, it is critical to ensure that the joint
between the paved surface and the concrete curb is maintained over the
life of the pavement because the two materials respond to temperature in
different manners and thus the joint tends to open.

In addition, the sidewalk or sodded area beside the curb must be graded
to drain into the gutter to prevent water ponding in this location. Ponded
water on the backside of the curb will seep into and saturate the sub-
grade at the curbline and may cause increased localized stress to the HMA
layers.

Highway pavements are also built with a crown sloped to facilitate the
flow of water from their surfaces into ditches at each side. The most com-
mon design practice allows the surface water to flow across the shoulder
into a ditch or gutter at the side. On a pavement uniformly sloped to either
the outside or the inside (such as a superelevated curve), the upper and
lower shoulder crossfall is generally the same as that of the driving lanes
to address safety concerns.

Paved shoulders provide the most effective surface drainage solution. From
a drainage point of view, a less-effective practice (but still acceptable) is to
provide gravel shoulders with increased crossfall to promote surface flow and
minimize seepage into the subgrade. Sodded shoulders tend to prevent ero-
sion, but they may also prevent water from draining freely from the road
surface, thereby increasing the amount of moisture entering the ditch and
saturating the subgrade soils at the edges of the pavement. Because sodded
surfaces are typically weaker than granular surfaces, they may create a traffic
hazard if they are used adjacent to higher-speed traffic lanes.

In some cases—where profile grade is sufficient—a raised lip or curb
4 inches (100 millimeters) or more in height may be constructed at the out-
side shoulder edge to intercept water and guide it to some convenient outlet.
In this design, the edge of the pavement is sometimes shaped into a shallow
channel which leads into storm sewer openings or other paved channels.
Curbs and dikes should be located so that they do not interfere with the
removal of snow from the pavement surface.

Drainage ditches receive water from pavement surfaces and subsur-
face drains. When the surrounding area slopes toward the roadway, these
ditches also intercept water that would otherwise reach the roadbed. It
is usually preferable to widen rather than deepen a drainage ditch to
increase its capacity. Side slopes usually vary from about 4 (horizontal):
1 (vertical) on the roadway side to about 8:1 or flatter for the outside
slope. The ditch should be large enough to accommodate the estimated
surface runoff. Generally, the ditch should be constructed on a continuous
smooth grade without depressions or pockets that will collect water.

Water collected in ditches may discharge directly into natural channels
or into storm sewer openings. In other cases, it may be directed from the
roadway by special ditches constructed for that purpose.

The ideal velocity of flow in a ditch is one that is fast enough to pre-
vent settling of suspended material but not so fast as to cause erosion or
scouring. Generally, a figure of 2 feet per second (0.6 meters per second)
for newly seeded loam or fine sand, to 6 feet per second (1.8 meter per
second) for well sodded or coarse gravel linings is an acceptable range of
safe velocities. Higher velocities require measures such as rock dams, rock
lining, or paving to prevent erosion. Hot mix asphalt is a desirable material
for ditch paving.

5.3.3 Design for Large Paved Areas

Surface drainage design procedures for parking lots are the same as
those for airports. A recommended guide is the Federal Aviation Adminis-
tration’s Advisory Circular 150/5320-5B, Airport Drainage.

After the parking area plan has been established and an efficient parking
pattern worked out, drainage grades that obviate patrons stepping from
cars into gutters are designed.

To prevent puddles from forming in rainy weather, ideally a minimum
of 2 percent, or 4 inch per foot (20 millimeters/meter), grade is necessary.
On sloping terrain, constructing a grade presents no problem, but in flat
areas, built-in drainage valleys give rise to a gently undulating flow on
the paved area. Flows to proposed inlets should be calculated for existing
runoff conditions, and if necessary, spacing should be adjusted to give
satisfactory results. For average conditions, inlet spacing of 200-400 feet
(60-120 meters) is satisfactory.

Because only a fixed contour can be laid in one pass of a paving machine,
designs requiring a meandering gutter or valley should be avoided. Such
gutters require hand placing, the quality of which is inferior to machine
laying. A layout should provide for drains that follow straight lines. When
the mix is being placed by the paving machine, the direction of the paver
should parallel these lines. For the most efficient paver use, these lines
should parallel the long dimensions of the lot. Watertight longitudinal
joints are essential to prohibit the intrusion of surface water and to mini-
mize the possibility of erosion under traffic.

In some instances, local bylaws restrict the flow of water into the storm sew-
ers during rainfall events. In these situations, the pavement surface is some-
times used for short-term storage (tens of minutes) of precipitation. In these
conditions, the pavement surface should be adequately maintained to prevent
saturation of the pavement structure during the temporary storage period.

In order to drain the granular materials and subgrade, the use of stub
drains 15-20 feet (5-7 meters) in length installed in a cruciform pattern
at each catch basin is recommended. The stub drains should be installed
in trenches with an invert below the underside of the granular layers and

5.3.4 Subsurface Drainage

Introduction

Subsurface water results from rainfall or melting snow soaking into
the soil and percolating downward until an impervious layer is reached.
At this point, the water saturates the void spaces of the soil. The boundary
between the saturated and unsaturated soil is referred to as the ground-
water table. Subsurface water tends to be in motion and will percolate
long distances through a porous layer if confined by impervious layers
or strata.

A porous or cracked pavement may permit rainwater or melted snow
to enter the granular base or subbase and saturate the various layers in
the pavement structure. This water may then flow downgrade through the
granular layers and create problems in other areas. These problems may
be avoided most effectively with proper attention to pavement mix design
and construction. A dense asphalt-treated base is not as subject to weaken-
ing and may be particularly effective. However, water infiltration into the
subgrade soils is still a concern. The installation of subgrade drainage is
fundamental to long-term pavement performance. If the structural section
contains open-graded layers, these should be drained by pipes or other
positive means to prevent water ponding.

Subsurface seepage, under certain conditions, may develop a hydro-
static head sufficient to lift the pavement completely off the base, causing
cracking, and in extreme cases, complete disintegration of the pavement
structure. The problem is more acute when steep grades in high rainfall
areas are involved. With steep gradients, the water may travel longitudi-
nally in an untreated base course along the direction of the gradient and
cause excessive hydrostatic pressures at sag vertical curves or on the low
side of superelevated horizontal curves.

Most urban pavements would benefit from the installation of subdrains
along both curblines. The subdrains should be installed in trenches with
an invert below the underside of the granular layers and should be con-
nected to permanent year-round frost-free drainage such as the catch
basin system. When full-length subdrains cannot be installed, the use of
stub drains 15-20 feet (5-7 meters) in length on the upstream side of the
catch basins is recommended.

In many cut sections, longitudinal subdrains are needed to prevent water
from higher ground accumulating under the pavement. If not intercepted,
subsurface water from a cut may flow onto an adjacent fill section and
cause slumping of the fill slope and cracking of the pavement. The choice
of filter material and the design of the drainage system must be given care-
ful attention, considering both the type of material to be drained and the
quantity of water to be expected.

Filter Material and Drain Rock

The purpose of pavement subdrainage is to enhance the long term (10
to 15 years) performance of the pavement. Thus, it is necessary to design
the drainage system for long-term performance. Such a system requires
the use of filters around drainage pipes to ensure performance. The filter
allows water to flow to the drain and, at the same time, to prevent the loss
of the fine particles from the soil. The design of a granular filter for any
particular soil was defined by Terzaghi and Peck as follows:

D15, filter < 5 * D85, soil (for soil retention)
D15, filter > 4 * D15, soil (for adequate permeability)

where D,, refers to the diameter of the soil or filter particle at the stated
percent passing.

The material that meets these criteria for most subgrade soils is washed
mortar sand, which is a clean, essentially one-size sand. Unfortunately, the
permeability of mortar sand, while greater than that of most subgrade soils
by several orders of magnitude, is still low for high-flow situations. Con-
sequently, for adequate drainage, a perforated pipe is typically required.
Such pipes are usually wrapped in a geotextile sock to prevent the sand
particles from washing into the pipe.

Drain rock, on the other hand, essentially consists of one-size stone
material (i.e., greater than % inch [4.75 millimeters]), which results in very
high permeability. It is apparent, however, that drain rock will never meet
the retention criteria for most subgrade soils. As a result, the fine particles
from the adjacent subgrade soils will wash into the drain stone and clog
the material. This loss of soil will result in settlement above and/or beside
the subdrain.

The permeability of a granular filter material is reduced very rapidly as
the percentage of fine material is increased. To prevent these issues, a filter
fabric is sometimes placed between the drain stone and the subgrade soils.
The choice of geotextile and the proper installation are critical to the suc-
cess of this type of construction. An inappropriate geotextile will still allow
fines from the subgrade to pass and result in clogging and settlement.

Subsurface Drainage Schemes

The following approaches to subsurface drainage are suggested.

As noted previously, the installation of continuous subdrains under each
curb in pavements designed with an urban cross section will extend the
life of the pavement. If the expense of a continuous system is prohibitive,
a system of stub drains could be used. For large paved surfaces, this same
stub drain system installed in a cruciform pattern at the catch basins is
also recommended.

For rural cross sections (e.g., shoulders and side ditches), adequate depth
of the ditches is critical. The invert of the trench must be below the base of
the pavement structure and typically at least 3 feet (1 meter) below centerline
grade to promote adequate drainage. In addition, if granular layers are used
in the pavement structure, these layers should be daylighted (G.e., placed full

Subsurface water can collect at the toe of cuts if appropriate drainage

localized conditions, subdrainage may still be required.

Drainage Related to Frost Action

When the frost depth exceeds the thickness of the pavement structure, a
subsurface drainage system may be required to lower the water table to pre-
vent the formation of ice lenses and the damaging effects of frost heaves.

Frost heaves are a serious problem. It should be noted that all saturated
or partially saturated soils will expand in volume (heave) when the water
in the pores expands by 9 percent on freezing. However, because the
water content of most subgrade soils is less than 15 percent, the overall
expansion is less than 2 percent. This likely applies to most subgrade
soils in North America. True frost heave, from a geotechnical perspective,
only occurs in a particular type of soil where the D,, of the soil is in the
range of 30-60 microns and the uniformity coefficient (D,,/D,,) is less than
about 6. This is a medium-size uniform silt. This type of soil has sufficient
permeability to attract water in the capillary zone to the freezing front and
is also fine enough to have a high capillary rise. This results in ice lenses
forming in the soil. The ice lenses grow in a downward direction (as water
in a pond freezes, being fed by water from below), as seen on Figure 5.10.
In order for ice lenses to grow in the soil, there must be freezing soil tem-
peratures, a water table close to the frost line, a soil favorable to the rapid
movement of capillary water, and a soil capable of retaining water. When
freezing temperatures are prolonged and the frost line penetrates suscep-
tible soil, the ice lenses that form cause a heave at the surface.

To avoid damage to drainage structures, determining the depth of frost
penetration is necessary for design purposes. The most reliable informa-
tion is obtained from local records of depth of frost penetration in the area
and for the soils concerned. When such local information is not available,
frost penetration can be estimated from charts obtained from the National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration National Climatic Data Center,
(www.ncdce.noaa.goy).

Problem soils should be removed to a depth equal to at least half, and

preferably three-quarters, of the normal depth of frost penetration for the

area of concern, and replaced with soil not susceptible to frost heave.

An alternate, but less effective, method of construction is to remove,
mix, and recompact a suitable depth of subgrade to promote uniformity.
This method will eliminate differential heave, although the whole road still
will rise and fall. Even if every care is taken, a generalized form of heave
can still occur. Frequently, ice accumulates between the granular base and
the asphalt layer. This is probably caused by the migration of water vapor
from the warm areas deep in the structure toward the colder surface lay-
ers. The sudden change in permeability at the asphalt layer restricts the
flow of vapor causing it to condense and freeze.

Another aspect of frost susceptibility is thaw weakening of subgrade
soils. This behavior is exhibited by most subgrade soils when the amount
of material finer than no. 200 sieve (75 microns) is greater than about 30
percent. Because the majority of subgrade soils are affected, thaw weaken-
ing is generally the most serious aspect of frost susceptibility. The greatest
loss of subgrade support is encountered when a wet autumn is followed
by a winter with many freeze-thaw cycles and then by a rapid spring thaw.
As frost advances into the soil, the available water in the voids freezes
and the saturation of the soils reduces; as a result, water from below
the freezing front is attracted to the freezing front and accumulates. In
the spring, the frozen subgrade thaws from both above and below. The
result is a period where the additional water drawn into the subgrade soils
is liquid but trapped over an impermeable frozen zone. The abnormally
elevated moisture content significantly weakens the soil. Soil strength will
only increase after the frost is completely out of the subgrade and normal
downward drainage occurs. The effect of thaw weakening can be reduced

by installing subgrade drains that have an invert depth below the frost

An additional adverse effect of the spring thaw may occur in granular

load-carrying capacity. Under the right conditions a sand filter blanket,
approximately 6 inches (150 millimeters) thick, or a filter fabric, can pre-

The effects of spring thaw can be minimized by using adequate depths
of granular soils or full-depth asphalt pavement and by maintaining proper
drainage.

5.3.5 Geosynthetic Fabrics

The design of subsurface drainage systems should give consideration
to geotextiles as alternative designs. Geosynthetic fabrics (geotextiles) are
manufactured from a number of different materials, including polypropyl-
ene, polyester, nylon, polyethylene, and polyvinyl chloride. The function of
geotextiles, when used as filters, is to allow groundwater removal without
the buildup of excessive seepage forces or water pressures. The geotextile
must also prevent piping or subsurface erosion of the soil which leads to
settlement. In these applications, water flows through the geotextile into
a water-conducting medium, usually a trench filled with a selected drain-
age aggregate and a slotted or perforated pipe, which quickly removes
the water.

The use of geotextiles for subsurface drainage may be categorized in
accordance with the following three general applications.

° Geotextiles are used as an envelope surrounding the single-size, clean
coarse aggregate (i.e. less than 20 percent retained on a No. 4 sieve
(4.75 millimeters)) in a trench. The geotextile is intended to prevent
subgrade soil from moving into and eventually clogging the stone while
still allowing water to pass through the geotextile. A more efficient sys-
tem is to install drainpipes in the aggregate-filled trench rather than
relying solely on the porous aggregate to carry the runoff. Typically, a
4- to 8-inch (100- to 200-millimeter) perforated flexible plastic pipe is
installed within 5 feet (1.5 meters) of the surface. The sketch on the
right in Figure 5.11 shows this configuration.

° A geotextile sock can also be used as a filter around a drainpipe, par-
ticularly for subsurface drainage in sandy or gravelly soils. However,
in the presence of subgrade soil with more than. 20 to 30 percent silt
or clay (most subgrade soils), this type of subdrain tends to allow
fines to migrate into the drain stone and to pass through the geotex-
tile and clog the pipe, thus preventing drainage and also leading to

Introduction

Thickness design for flexible pavements is an effort to economize the
amount and the type of material used to support the projected traffic,
on the given subgrade, under the specific climate conditions at the site.
Whether thickness design is applied to major interstate highways, city
streets, airports, or county roads, its objective is to strike a balance between
two competing goals—structural capacity and life-cycle cost.

The state-of-the-art method in flexible pavement design is the Mechanistic-
Empirical (M-E) method. The M-E method typically models the pavement as a
multilayered elastic system and uses the principles of mechanics to calculate
deflections, stresses, and strains at any point in the structure in response to
an external wheel load. Calculated strains are then compared with observed
pavement performance to determine the required pavement thickness. In the
case of flexible pavements, two performance criteria are widely considered
critical: limiting horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the hot mix asphalt
(HMA) layer to avoid fatigue, and limiting vertical compressive strain at the
top of the subgrade to avoid rutting.

The Asphalt Institute method encompasses two types of design proce-
dures, both of which have been incorporated into SW-1, Asphalt Pavement
Thickness Design Software.

° DAMA—Advanced Structural Analysis. DAMA is a_ powerful
computer-based M-E program that allows engineers to analyze any
pavement system having up to five layers in which the materials can
be described in terms of elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio. Engi-
neers using DAMA consider monthly temperature variation, including
freeze/thaw, and its effect on pavement materials to estimate remain-
ing life to failure. DAMA is embedded into SW-1, Asphalt Pavement
Thickness Design Software.

° Design Curves. The Asphalt Institute publishes pavement design curves
in the following manuals for specific transportation design applications:

° 1S-154 General Aviation Airports

° MS-1 Highways, Roads, and Streets

° MS-11 Air-Carrier Airports

¢ MS-23 Heavy Wheel Loads (Ports/Industrial Applications)

The curves presented in these publications were produced by presolving
a large number of problems over a wide range of input values using DAMA.
The resulting design curves provide users with a powerful solution based
on M-E, in a handy manual that can be used in the office or the field.

In order to provide a single computer-based pavement design tool, the
Asphalt Institute developed SW-1, a Windows-based program that offers a
comprehensive suite of design solutions for highways, streets, airports, and
heavy wheel loads, based on the design curves together with an improved

6.1.1 Design Principles

The following paragraphs describe the design principles that are at the
core of the Asphalt Institute methods. These principles are coded into
DAMA and are inherent in the various design curves and SW-1.

DAMA models the pavement as a multilayered elastic system with up to
five layers, including the subgrade (see Figure 6.1). The material in each layer
is characterized by two variables—a modulus of elasticity and a Poisson’s
ratio. All layers in the model are assumed to extend infinitely outward in the
horizontal plane. The subgrade layer is the lowest layer in the model and
is assumed to extend infinitely downward (vertically). A condition of full-
friction is assumed to exist at all layer interfaces.

A proposed pavement structure is subjected to traffic loading expressed
in terms of the number of repetitions of a wheel load on either a single
tire or a set of dual tires.

Temperature variability is known to affect the dynamic modulus of
asphalt-bound materials and the resilient modulus of granular base and
subgrade soils. DAMA allows for monthly variation of the mean monthly
air temperature.

The response of the pavement system to the vertical wheel load, in
terms of stresses, strains, and deflections, is calculated at a point in the
structure using the principles of mechanics. Two strains are believed to be
critical for design purposes:

° The horizontal tensile strain on the underside of the lowest asphalt-
bound layer (either asphalt concrete or emulsified asphalt base). If
the horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the lowest HMA layer is
excessive, fatigue cracking will be initiated.

° The vertical compressive strain at the top of the subgrade layer.
Excessive vertical strain on top of the subgrade layer will result in
permanent deformation (rutting) at the pavement surface.

Strain can be calculated, in both critical positions, as a response to

repetitive wheel loads.

A cumulative damage calculation is used to determine the remaining
life of the pavement structure. Miner’s Rule is used to evaluate the ratio
of the number of actual load repetitions to the number of allowable load
repetitions. Miner’s Rule states that the total allowable damage is equal to
the summation of damage done by individual load applications. When the
number of actual load repetitions is equal to the number of allowable load
repetitions, cumulative damage is calculated to be 1, and a state of fail-
ure exists. At any level of actual load repetitions, cumulative damage can

Conventional HMA
Conventional hot mix asphalt pavement refers to the manner in which
materials are typically incorporated into a flexible pavement structure.

MS-4 285

=== ORIGINAL PDF PAGE 20 ===
=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 320 ===
Economics demands that pavement designers use lower-cost unbound
granular base materials when possible to reduce the initial cost of the pave-
ment structure. But while these materials are less expensive to produce and
place, they are not as effective in distributing traffic-induced stresses to the
subgrade. As a result, thick layers are required to support even low-volume
truck traffic. Typically, conventional HMA pavements consist of thick aggre-
gate base layers (6-30 inches) (150-750 millimeters) coupled with relatively
thin HMA surface courses @-5 inches) (75-125 millimeters). In a conven-
tional design, the HMA wearing surface acts to provide a smooth riding, all-
weather, waterproof surface, while the majority of the structural capacity is
derived from the granular base layer. For many agencies in the United States,
conventional HMA pavement sections provide an affordable solution for low-
to moderate-volume roads.

Full-Depth Asphalt

A full-depth asphalt pavement is one in which asphalt mixtures are
employed for all courses above the subgrade or improved subgrade.
HMA or asphalt-treated base layers are placed directly on the subgrade.
Asphalt-stabilized base layers in a full-depth pavement need not be
constructed as thick as the comparable aggregate base layer in a con-
ventional HMA pavement. The HMA wearing course creates a strong,
durable, smooth, and cost-effective pavement structure.

Full-depth asphalt pavements are able to resist traffic loads in an efficient
“low-profile” pavement section. Asphalt-aggregate mixtures resist these
traffic-induced stresses far better than unbonded aggregate layers, which
have no tensile strength. Therefore, an HMA layer spreads the wheel load
over a broad area. As a result, less total pavement structure is required.
Additional benefits of using full-depth asphalt pavements include reduced
permeability and subsurface drainage costs, increased speed of construc-
tion, and an extended paving season when placing thick lifts.

Full-depth asphalt pavements have been in place in the United States
since the 1960s and 1970s, especially in high-volume traffic applications.
Our collective experience over 40 years of building successful full-depth
asphalt pavements has served as an important step toward developing the
Perpetual Pavement philosophy.

Perpetual Pavement

Perpetual Pavements are designed and built to last longer than 50 years
without requiring major structural rehabilitation or reconstruction. Like full-
depth asphalt pavements, Perpetual Pavements are designed using HMA or
asphalt-stabilized layers in all layers above the subgrade. What sets Perpetual
Pavements apart is the additional care taken in mix type and material selection
at all layers within the pavement structure.

The Perpetual Pavement concept improves upon the full-depth asphalt
design method by incorporating a fatigue-resistant base layer and rut-
resistant intermediate and surface layers into the pavement structure.
These mix type selection techniques will minimize fatigue and rutting in
the pavement structure.

Perpetual Pavement design also requires careful attention to material
selection. Hard angular aggregates must be carefully selected, processed,
and blended together to form a strong “stone-skeleton” to support intended
traffic loads. Properly selected Performance Graded asphalt binders will
improve rut-resistance at high temperatures and diminish the effect of
transverse low temperature cracking. The combined result of these mix
type and material selection strategies is a pavement structure that requires
only periodic surface renewal in response to distresses confined to the
wearing course.

It should be noted that the Perpetual Pavement design concept is separate
and distinct from the Asphalt Institute thickness design method. More detail
on this method is provided in Section 6.2.3.

6.1.2 Input Variables

Data-input requirements for the Asphalt Institute design method depends
on the procedure selected by the user. The design curves were produced
and intended for everyday use and require minimal information as input
into the procedure. DAMA, on the other hand, is intended for advanced
structural analysis of pavement structures with the requirement of more-
detailed user inputs.

With either procedure, there are four types of inputs required to
use either AI method: climate, traffic, subgrade strength, and material
properties. The following is a brief description of these input variables.

Climate

Air temperature is the climate-related user input in the AI method. Air
temperature affects the design of the pavement structure in two impor-
tant ways. First, the stiffness of HMA and other asphalt-stabilized layers is
directly dependent on the pavement temperature, which can be calculated
from air temperature. For example, high pavement temperatures result in
a reduction of the stiffness of HMA layers. As layers with lower stiffness
do not distribute loads as effectively as stiff layers, pavements at higher
temperature are more susceptible to plastic deformation caused by con-
centration of Joad than pavements that are at low temperature.

For granular base and subgrade soils, temperature determines whether
freezing and thawing conditions will occur. Frozen materials become stiff,
while thawing weakens unbound materials, particularly subgrade soils.

In DAMA, mean monthly air temperature is used to directly character-
ize the climatic variation for a specific pavement structure using location-
specific temperature data. For design-curve analyses such as MS-1, the
user is asked to select a mean annual air temperature appropriate for a
geographical location.

Traffic
The AI method is based on an analysis of individual wheel loads and the
response of the pavement system to the wheel loads.

Traffic input in DAMA is a single or dual wheel load. The basic user inputs
are the magnitude of the load, the contact pressure of the tire, and the
number of monthly load repetitions. For dual tires, wheel-to-wheel spacing
is also required. Its ability to analyze single or dual wheels allows DAMA to
be applicable to virtually any pavement section being considered.

The design curves published in MS-1 are specific to highway vehicles.
Rather than analyzing individual wheel loads, the curves have been devel-
oped based on the Equivalent Single-Axle Load (ESAL) concept. An ESAL
is a single axle carrying an 18,000-pound load distributed to the pavement.
Trucks, cars, and other highway vehicles with multiple axles and a broad
range of loaded weights can be equated to a corresponding number of
ESALs. By predicting the total number of ESALs expected over the design
life of the pavement, an engineer can enter the design curves to calculate
the required pavement thickness.

For air-carrier airport design MS-11, Thickness Design—Asphalt Pavements
Sor Air Carrier Airports, traffic is expressed as the number of departures of
a specific aircraft type. The design curves used for port and industrial appli-
cations in MS-23, Thickness Design—Asphalt Pavements for Heavy Wheel
Loads, are based on a single-wheel analysis but allow the user to convert a
multiple-wheel vehicle into an equivalent single-wheel load. Industrial pave-
ments must be designed with knowledge of the specific vehicle loads and
wheel geometry.

Subgrade Strength

Subgrade soils form the foundation of pavement structures. Some knowledge
of a soil’s suitability to resist deformation under loading is required to
complete a pavement design.

The basis for subgrade-strength measurement in the Asphalt Institute
method is the resilient modulus, M,. This applies for both DAMA analysis
and for solutions using the design curves.

In order to facilitate the use of other widely used field and laboratory
tests, correlations have been established between resilient modulus and
the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) and the resistance (R) value. Proce-
dures for performing CBR and R-value tests are detailed in MS-10, Soils
Manual for the Design of Asphalt Pavement Structures.

Material Properties

As stated earlier, DAMA allows up to five layers in the pavement section,
each represented by an elastic modulus and a Poisson’s ratio. Because
Poisson’s ratio is not expected to vary for a layer, the emphasis should be
on properly characterizing a layer’s elastic modulus.

When working in the DAMA advanced structural-analysis mode, the
user can characterize the elastic modulus of a layer by a number of means.
For asphalt-bound layers, the dynamic modulus can be entered directly
for each month in the year or the user can use a modulus-temperature
relationship to calculate the dynamic modulus based on the monthly aver-
age temperature. For specialized situations, mix volumetric properties
can be prescribed to further refine the modulus-temperature relationship.

Compare surface and reuse approaches
Trend card N1 · Paving stones. Modular paving uses individual units rather than one continuous asphalt
layer. Some systems are designed to manage water through joints and underlying layers; the word paver
alone does not establish permeability. Ask about the whole pavement system, loading, drainage,
accessibility and maintenance. A patterned appearance is not environmental evidence.

Trend card N2 · Porous asphalt. FHWA's April 2015 technical brief describes porous asphalt pavements
with stone reservoirs as systems intended to provide a surface and manage stormwater. Water moves
through interconnected surface voids into the underlying reservoir. This differs from an open-graded
friction course on a dense pavement, where water moves through the surface course and out toward the
side. The brief identifies potential clogging and the need for specialized maintenance, as well as site/design
limitations. A material name does not guarantee infiltration at an unsuitable site.

Trend card N3 · Recycling. Recovered asphalt may enter an approved plant or specialist in-place process.
Recovered concrete may be processed as aggregate. Potential reductions in new-material demand need to
be examined alongside processing, transport, suitability, maintenance and end-of-life. “Recycled” is a
property of the material pathway, not a complete environmental score.

Rewrite these statements so they are bounded and useful: “All paving stones absorb rain”; “Porous asphalt
never needs drainage planning”; “Any recycled material is automatically the lowest-impact choice.” For
each, identify a mechanism, a condition and one question for a qualified reviewer. Skills: Reading, Problem
Solving, Writing.

Begin with the work and the people affected
A hazard is a source or situation with the potential to cause harm. Risk considers the likelihood and
consequences of that harm under the actual conditions. A control changes those conditions to prevent or
reduce exposure. A control needs an owner, a way to check it and a response if it fails. “Be careful” does
not tell a new worker which action is required.

The paving crew works around moving equipment, hot materials, changing surfaces and other people. A
task that was controlled yesterday may change with wind, visibility, traffic, equipment condition or a
different worker. Before work begins, the crew needs a shared understanding of the task and the current
controls. During the work, a changed condition needs attention rather than silent improvisation.

The examples in this chapter teach recognition and decision making. The workplace must provide its own
orientation, procedures, training, qualified supervision and emergency arrangements. Read the complete
applicable WorkSafeBC requirements when preparing actual work. A classroom exercise does not qualify a

7.1 · PPE is selected for a hazard and a person
Personal protective equipment (PPE) is equipment worn to protect a person against specified hazards.
Selection starts with an assessment of the exposure and the work. A product that protects against one
hazard may not protect against another. A general-purpose glove is different from a glove selected for a
particular chemical; eye protection and face protection have different roles; hearing protection must suit
the assessed noise exposure and the required program.

Fit and compatibility matter. A respirator must be appropriate for the contaminant and conditions and used
within the required respiratory-protection program. A generic “dust mask” description does not establish
adequate protection against a gas, oxygen deficiency or a particular dust. A facepiece, eyewear, headgear
and other items must work together without making one another ineffective. Training includes the limits of
the equipment, not just how to put it on.


Know the provision responsibilities

WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation 8.2 makes the worker responsible for clothing needed for the natural
elements, general-purpose work gloves, appropriate footwear including safety footwear, and safety
headgear. The employer provides all other PPE required by the regulation at no cost to the worker.
Agreements may provide for the employer to supply some or all of the worker-responsibility items. If
employer-provided PPE causes allergenic or other adverse health effects, the employer must provide
appropriate alternatives or safe measures. Read the actual agreement and the complete regulation before
deciding how an item is supplied.

Responsibility to provide an item does not remove the employer's duties concerning suitable protection,
instruction and the workplace. Under 8.3, PPE must provide effective protection, be compatible, avoid
creating a hazard and be maintained in good working order and sanitary condition. Where workplace
evaluation is required, 8.4 addresses consultation. 8.5 specifies program elements for chemical exposure
or oxygen-deficient atmospheres, including selection, use, inspection, cleaning, maintenance, storage and
training.

Inhalation brings an airborne contaminant into the respiratory system. Carbon monoxide can occur with
combustion equipment; hydrogen sulphide can be a concern in particular petroleum-related or confined-
space conditions; welding produces fumes specific to the process and materials. Dust-generating work can
create respirable crystalline silica exposure. Disturbing suspect asbestos-containing material requires
identification and the applicable controls. These examples identify possible hazards to assess, not a claim
that every paving site contains every substance. Smell, appearance and a photograph cannot establish a
safe airborne concentration.

Ingestion can occur when contamination reaches the mouth through hands, food, drink or other objects.
Absorption occurs when a substance enters through skin; its significance depends on the substance.
Injection can occur when high-pressure fluid penetrates skin. A small injection wound can conceal serious
injury: report the event and obtain emergency medical care promptly under the emergency procedure.
Never use a hand to search for a pressurized leak.

Hazards also act through noise, vibration, heat, cold and physical contact. Noise exposure needs
assessment and controls; hearing protection is one part of the required response. Vibration can affect the
hands and arms or the whole body, depending on the equipment and exposure. Heat risk depends on
workload, weather, clothing and other conditions; a plan may include controls, acclimatization, suitable
breaks, hydration and emergency response. Cold and wet conditions can affect health, dexterity and
traction. A calendar month alone does not establish a safe exposure.

Biological hazards may include bacteria, other microorganisms or viruses encountered in the actual work
environment. Contaminated water, waste or sharps require the relevant assessment and procedure.
Workers should report an unexpected condition and keep others from exposure while the responsible
person arranges a response. Do not handle an unknown sharp or contaminated material as an improvised
classroom demonstration.

Musculoskeletal injury (MSI) involves muscles, tendons, joints or related tissues. Force, repetition,
posture, duration and work organization can contribute. A shovel's load, a rake's reach and a wheelbarrow
route affect the demand on a person. A change in tool, load handling, layout or work organization can
reduce that demand. An awkward movement may cause a strain or sprain; a crush, impact or fall can cause
trauma. Reporting early discomfort or fatigue helps the crew address a developing problem.


Put the control before the exposure

levels and applicable exposure limits using the specified control approaches. A substitute must have known
hazards and reduce the risk. PPE as the primary control is permitted only under the conditions in the
clause; it is not an automatic first choice. Effective controls often work together.

7.3 · Orientation connects policy to a real workplace
A company safety policy states commitments and responsibilities. A procedure explains how a particular
task is controlled. A manual may contain equipment or program instructions. Orientation connects a
person to the workplace: who supervises them, what they are assigned, what hazards apply, what
procedures they need and how to get help. Reading a general policy cannot replace a demonstration of the
actual task.

WorkSafeBC 3.22–3.25 addresses young and new workers, including workplace-specific orientation and
training before work begins, additional training when needed and records. Topics include rights and
responsibilities, supervisor contact, hazards, safe work procedures, emergency procedures, first aid and
instruction/demonstration of the work. A person experienced elsewhere can still be new to this workplace.
Asking for more instruction when a task is unfamiliar is part of responsible work.

7.4 · WHMIS and first aid are working systems
WHMIS connects hazard classification, labels, safety data sheets (SDSs), education and workplace training.
A supplier label identifies a hazardous product and communicates prescribed hazard information. A
workplace label is required in specified workplace circumstances and has its own information
requirements. An SDS gives structured information about the product, hazards, handling, exposure
controls, first aid and other matters. The label, SDS and actual product identifier must agree.

A pictogram is a starting point for understanding a hazard. It does not give a complete response procedure,
exposure limit, glove selection or first-aid instruction. Some classified hazards do not require a pictogram.
The environmental symbol may appear on an SDS or label but is not mandatory under WHMIS. Product
classification is not determined by choosing whichever picture looks most alarming.

7.5 · Rigging starts with qualified people and known information
Rigging connects and controls a load for lifting or handling using suitable equipment and arrangements. A
sling connects the load to the lifting system. A shackle, hook or other fitting has an identified function,
compatibility and working load limit. Working load limit (WLL) describes a permitted working load under
stated conditions; it is not a universal number detached from configuration, angle or component condition.

WorkSafeBC Part 15 is the starting regulatory reading for this task. Under 15.2, rigging and slinging must
be done by or under the direct supervision of qualified workers familiar with the rigging and code of signals.
The complete provisions address identification, design factors, WLL, operation, slings, components and

7.6 · Recognize the site and machine hazards
A paving machine has areas that move, heat, pivot or hold stored energy. A stopped engine does not prove
all hazardous energy is controlled. Electrical energy, hydraulic pressure, elevated components, springs,
gravity, hot surfaces and pressurized systems can remain hazardous. Isolation, lockout, blocking and
verification require the applicable procedure and trained people. Do not reach into a feeder, auger or other
moving mechanism to clear material.

At the end of a work period, the crew still has responsibilities. Equipment must be parked and secured

communicate the changed route. The handover records both the machine status and the remaining site
condition.

Gradation Adjustment for Different Specific Gravities

Aggregate gradations and grading curves are determined and expressed
in percentages by weight for convenience of measurement. The grad-
ing curve of each material is determined by weight using sieve analysis.
However, grading specifications are established to require certain percent-
ages of material to achieve the various volumetric sizes in the hot mix
asphalt. The blending process assumes that all the aggregates have the
same specific gravity. As long as the specific gravities of the individual
aggregate stockpiles are reasonably alike, the percentages by weight will
approximate the percentages by volume for all practical purposes.

However, when the specific gravities of the individual aggregates differ
significantly (by 0.20 or more) and they are to be blended together to
make a target gradation, the aggregate proportions that are set up in the
plant controls (percentages by weight) should be adjusted for this vari-
ance. Without this correction, the blend being made by weight at the
plant to produce final volumetric proportions in the mix could deviate
significantly from the blend of sizes designed in the laboratory on paper,
since the different sized materials have different unit weights or densities.
The adjustment is based on the fact that

VOLUME x SPECIFIC GRAVITY = WEIGHT

Reclaimed Asphalt
Pavement

3.3.1 Introduction

Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) is removed and/or reprocessed
pavement that contains asphalt and aggregates. RAP has become a very
valuable commodity in the paving industry. Properly processed RAP yields
high-quality, graded aggregates and small-aggregate agglomerations coated
by asphalt cement (sometimes called black sand). Black sand contains a
high percentage of (old) binder. Black sand alone is not considered RAP.
Its addition to the new mix in high percentages will result in a dry and
noncompactable mix. When RAP is included as a component in new HMA
mixtures, the needed amount of new or virgin materials is reduced. Not
only does this save money and natural resources, but it also eliminates
a waste product that otherwise would require disposal. RAP has been
used extensively in new HMA pavements for several decades and has
consistently exhibited equal performance to mixtures made with all new
material.

3.3.2 Reclaiming Existing Pavements

Processing RAP

The processing of RAP entails the removal of the existing asphalt pave-
ment material and the crushing and screening of the material to reduce it
to an appropriate size. Removal is generally accomplished by milling or
full-depth conventional ripping.

Milling is achieved using a rotary-drum, cold-milling machine. The mill-
ing or cutting drum on a milling machine contains a patterned layout of
carbide teeth. As the milling machine passes over the pavement, the teeth
on the rotating drum grind into the pavement to a controlled depth. Typi-
cal mill depths have been up to 2 inches (50 millimeters) for a single pass,
but equipment is now available to mill 12 inches (00 millimeters) or
more. The milling or grinding action of the rotating teeth crushes the RAP
as the material is removed such that almost all the particles are smaller
than 2 inches (50 millimeters). Many times, the milled RAP is suitable for
use in new HMA without further crushing or screening. Typical milling
equipment (see Figure 3.12) is configured to pick up the milled material
and place it on an integral conveyor belt where the material can be loaded
directly into trucks for hauling to a production facility or stockpile site.

Conventional full-depth removal involves ripping and breaking the
pavement using earthmoving equipment, bulldozers with rippers, and/or

pneumatic pavement breakers. Front-end loaders typically pick up and
load the broken pieces of pavement into haul trucks for transportation
to a processing facility. The reclaimed material is then processed through
crushers and screens to reduce the material to the maximum acceptable
particle size and to reduce the size of the RAP pieces so that easy and
rapid heat transfer can be established within the mixing process. The goal
for equipment setup and material routing for crushing and screening the
RAP should be to break apart the larger-aggregate agglomerations that are
bound by the asphalt cement but not to change the existing aggregate gra-
dation in the RAP. Because the RAP pieces before crushing basically con-
tain aggregate with a typical dense-graded gradation, care should be taken
in the RAP crushing operation not to destroy this existing RAP aggregate
gradation nor to create new fractured faces in the aggregates themselves.
In reality, both milling and crushing cause some aggregate degradation,
with milling causing the greater amount. Hence, the gradation of milled
RAP is generally finer than crushed RAP and the gradation of pavement is
finer than virgin aggregates crushed with the same type of equipment.

Stockpiling

As is typical in all aspects of HMA construction, consistency of product
is paramount, and the same holds true for RAP. RAP may be very con-
sistent if it is removed from one project site. However, RAP may include
materials from patches and surface treatments or consist of materials
from multiple and differing projects, which can significantly increase its
variability. Depending on the percentage of RAP used, variability may be
experienced in the HMA in gradation, asphalt content, compaction, and
volumetric properties. With higher percentages of RAP, it may be necessary
to fractionate the RAP into multiple stockpiles.

Proper stockpiling procedures should control variability in the RAP.
RAP materials with different asphalt content, different aggregate type, and
different aggregate grading should be stockpiled separately. When logistics
do not allow for separate stockpiles but instead require combining RAP
products from different sources or with different properties, they should
be blended together as well as possible during the various stages of pro-
cessing and handling. This includes appropriate layer stacking during the
stockpiling of the preprocessed material, appropriate stockpile load-out
and feeding of crusher and/or screen hoppers by front-end loaders, and
appropriate stockpile layering of the processed RAP.

Regardless of the source(s) or processing methods of the RAP, consolida-
tion and moisture retention are two major areas of concern in stockpiled
RAP. Consolidation of the RAP, coupled with moisture, tends to resolidify
or “reagglomerate” the RAP particles into a mass, which can be difficult to
rebreak into its individual processed particles. Low, horizontal stockpiles
have historically been preferable to high, conical stockpiles in which it was
believed that greater consolidation of the RAP took place. However, it has
been found that, regardless of the size of the pile, a crust forms 8-10 inches
(20-25 centimeters) thick over the outer surface of the stockpile and the
RAP does not recompact or consolidate below the crust. The crust is easily
penetrated and broken by a front-end loader. The crust helps to hinder mois-
ture from penetrating into the RAP, and along with the greater slope areas of
large conical piles, water tends to shed more easily, further preventing the
compaction of the underlying material. As stated, the same thickness of crust
is present in low, horizontal stockpiles, but front-end loaders have greater
difficulty working around low piles. As with natural aggregates, stockpiles
should be built on appropriate surfaces to prevent contamination.

Moisture retention in RAP material can significantly affect the produc-
tion of HMA. Because of the asphalt binder component of RAP and the
associated problems it can cause, RAP is not put through the dryer of a
hot plant separately or with the virgin aggregates. The RAP is typically
introduced into the HMA production process after the dryer, when the
hot virgin aggregates from the dryer mix with and heat the RAP. Any
moisture in the RAP is driven off primarily by the heat of the virgin
aggregates. Because there is an upper threshold to which the aggre-
gates can be heated, the heat available to the RAP is limited. Increased
moisture content of the RAP material requires a reduction in the amount
of RAP in the HMA or a reduction in the rate at which HMA is pro-
duced. Hence, it is imperative to keep moisture out of RAP as much
as possible. Much better drainage of RAP is achieved in tall conical
stockpiles than in low, flat stockpiles. In areas of higher annual precipita-
tion, it may be beneficial to protect RAP from moisture by covering it with
tarps or by storing it under roofed structures.

Random sampling is recommended to get the best representation of the
materials present. If historical construction documents, such as construction

Before removal and recycling, pavements can be sampled by coring
or by cutouts. After milling or crushing, RAP can be sampled in any of
the same ways that virgin aggregates are sampled—from stockpiles, haul
trucks, conveyor belts, and material handling transfer/discharge points.
Regardless of the sampling scenario, it is vital that the sampling methods
provide samples that are representative of the produced material.

For samples taken from the pavement, cores are typically taken to the
full depth of the pavement and then trimmed off to the proposed depth
of removal. Standards pertaining to sampling from roadways, such as
AASHTO T 168 (ASTM D 979), can be used as a guide to obtain HMA pave-
ment cores. With pavement sampling, it is important to remember that the
milling and processing of the RAP may change the gradation of the RAP
aggregate somewhat from the gradation in the pavement cores. Some state
transportation departments have developed degradation factors for grada-
tion based on experience with the states’ local materials. Stockpiles of the
processed RAP should be checked to verify the actual RAP gradation.

RAP can be sampled from haul trucks transporting milled material from
the pavement to the plant location. When sampling from a truck, a trench
with a level bottom should be dug across the mass of the RAP. Samples
should then be obtained by shovel from three locations equally spaced
across the trench.

Sampling RAP from stockpiles is similar to sampling stockpiled virgin
aggregate. It is usually best to use front-end loaders or other equipment
for sampling from stockpiles. However, if samples are obtained by shov-
eling, care should be taken to not include any of the crusted outer layer
Gif any) of the stockpile in the samples. Shoveled samples should be taken
from at least 10 random places around the stockpile.

It is necessary to obtain representative samples of the RAP not only
for laboratory mix designs but also for determining its key properties by
testing. In a new HMA mix, the asphalt binder in the RAP contributes to
and becomes a part of the total binder. It is important to know how much
asphalt binder is present in the RAP. Depending on the amount of RAP
desired for a new mix, it may also be necessary to determine the physi-
cal properties of the asphalt binder. The aggregate in the RAP becomes a
part of the new HMA aggregate structure. Thus, it is important to know its
physical properties, such as overall gradation and specific gravity of both

3.4 Aggregate Additives

3.4.1 Introduction

Aggregate additives are materials that are incorporated with the natural
aggregate to improve the overall performance of hot mix asphalt and/or to
address various environmental concerns. These additives can be grouped
into the following categories:

e Mineral filler

¢ Antistrip additives

° Other additives

Some of these aggregate additives have long been used in HMA. Others,
although commercially available, have not been used extensively in rou-
tine highway construction, rehabilitation, and pavement preservation. As
such, it is recommended that a user agency evaluate their performance
in relation to the agency’s individual needs and expectations to prove
the usefulness of an aggregate additive regarding overall hot mix asphalt
performance.

3.4.2 Mineral Filler

As discussed in “Composite-Aggregate Size Fractions” in this chapter,
the composite-aggregate structure in HMA mixtures consists of three cate-
gories of particle sizes: coarse aggregate, fine aggregate, and mineral filler.
In this context, mineral filler is considered that portion of the composite
aggregate passing the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. The mineral filler plays
an important role in HMA mixtures because it fills a portion of the voids
in a paving mix and serves to improve the cohesion of the asphalt binder
and the stability of the mixture. It is especially important in stone matrix
asphalt mixes where it contributes significantly to compactability, perme-
ability, and in-service pavement performance.

There are times when a separate material must be included in the aggre-
gate blend to make up or supplement that mineral filler fraction. ASTM
D 242, Standard Specification for Mineral Filler for Bituminous Paving
Mixtures, states: “Mineral filler shall consist of finely divided mineral mat-
ter such as rock dust, slag dust, hydrated lime, hydraulic cement, fly ash,
loess, or other suitable mineral matter. At the time of use, it shall be suf-
ficiently dry to flow freely and essentially free from agglomerations.” This
standard requires that the material be free from organic impurities and
have a plasticity index no greater than 4. The gradation limits are shown
in Table 3.12.

Hydrated lime is a dry powder that comes from the specialized pro-
cessing of limestone. Limestone is crushed and calcinated (controlled time
and temperature heating) at temperatures above 1650°F (900°C) to create
quicklime. The quicklime is in turn treated with sufficient water to satisfy
its chemical affinity for water, creating hydrated lime. Hydrated lime is
also used as an antistrip additive. See “Hydrated Lime” in this chapter.

Hydraulic cements are fine powders that chemically react with water
creating a binder that sets and hardens into a stone-like material. The most
commonly available hydraulic cement is Portland cement.

Fly ash is the fine-grain, powdery particulate material that is produced
from the burning of pulverized coal in a coal-fired boiler or furnace and
collected from the flue gas. Fly ash has been used as a substitute mineral
filler in HMA mixtures for many years. Most fly ash sources are capa-
ble of meeting the gradation requirements and other pertinent physical
(nonplastic) and chemical (organic content) requirements of mineral filler
specifications. It is possible that some sources of fly ash that have a high
lime (CaO) content may also be useful as antistripping agents in HMA
mixes. See “Fly Ash” in this chapter.

Loess comes from sedimentary, unconsolidated deposits of windblown,
silty material. This fine material (cock flour) was created by the grinding
of rocks during glacial activity. Loess is typically thought of as the silt that
makes up the fertile topsoil in some parts of the world.

Kiln dusts are fine by-products of PCC and lime high-temperature
rotary-kiln production operations that are captured in the air pollution
control dust collection system (e.g., cyclones, electrostatic precipitators,
and baghouses). Both fresh Cement Kiln Dust (CKD) and Lime Kiln Dust
(LKD) can be used as mineral fillers in HMA mixtures. The lime compo-
nents of the CKD and LKD can assist in promoting stripping resistance.

3.4.3 Antistrip Additives

Stripping in HMA mixtures has been referred to as the “loss of adhesion
between the aggregate surface and asphalt cement binder in the presence
of moisture.” Materials added to HMA to reduce moisture sensitivity and
stripping are known as antistrip additives. Liquid antistrip is commonly
used, and typically added at the asphalt refinery or terminal upon request.
Only those additives that are added directly to the aggregate either in the
stockpile or before introduction into a batch or drum mixer are discussed
in this section.

Hydrated Lime

Hydrated lime is the most common aggregate-added antistrip material.
It reacts with aggregates, strengthening the bond between the asphalt
binder and the aggregate. Quicklime should not be added to HMA unless
it first has been completely hydrated. If quicklime remains unhydrated in
the HMA, it will change to Ca(OH), when it comes into contact with water
during the service life of the pavement. This reaction (i.e., changing from
CaO to Ca(OH),) is expansive and will create a volume change in the HMA
and losses in strength and performance.

Hydrated lime can be added to HMA at various stages during the HMA
production process. These stages range from the addition of lime to aggre-
gate several days before proportioning and mixing to adding dry lime
to the drum mixer at the point of the asphalt binder entry. Lime can be
successfully proportioned and mixed in HMA in both batch and drum
mixers. Dry lime can be added to dry aggregate and wet aggregate. Mois-
ture levels in wet aggregate are typically 2-3 percent above the saturated
surface dried condition of the aggregate. Moisture ionizes the lime and
helps distribute it on the aggregate surface. Lime-treated aggregates can
be stockpiled for “marination” or can be conveyed directly to the drying
and mixing portion of the HMA production unit.

Lime slurries made from hydrated lime or quicklime have also been
used. Lime-slurried aggregates are conveyed directly to the drying and
mixing portion of the HMA facility or placed into stockpiles for marina-
tion. The use of lime slurries has several advantages: improved resistance
of the treated hot mix to stripping, reduced dusting associated with the
addition of dry lime to the aggregate, and improved distribution of lime
on the aggregate. However, the use of lime slurries adds more water than
is typically used for conventional lime applications and can substantially
increase the water content of the aggregate before entering the drying
and mixing portions of the HMA facility. Increased fuel consumption and
reduced HMA production can result. The use of lime slurries also requires
purchasing or renting specialized equipment to prepare the lime slurry at
the mixing operation site.

The advantages of marinating or stockpiling treated aggregate before
reentry into the HMA facility include a reduction in moisture content while
the aggregate is stockpiled. Also, the lime treatment can be performed

Fly Ash

The performance of fly ash as an antistrip has been mixed. It is believed
that the calcium content, and in particular the free or available lime (CaO)
content, is instrumental in the performance of a fly ash as an aid to prevent-
ing asphalt stripping. Fly ash can be introduced into the HMA production
process in the same ways as lime.

Polymeric Treatment

Polymeric aggregate surface treatment (SBR latex) provides a protective
barrier on the aggregate that repels water and waterproofs the aggregate
while providing an improved bonding with the asphalt. When properly
applied, the polymeric-aggregate treatment will turn a hydrophilic aggregate
into a hydrophobic aggregate, increasing the water resistance of the HMA.

One of the additional benefits of using polymeric-aggregate treatment is
that the amount of asphalt binder required in the mixture may be lowered,
resulting in cost savings for the HMA contractor. As the polymer coats the
porous aggregate, less asphalt binder is absorbed into the aggregate.

The SBR latex concentrate must be diluted to 15 percent solids before
use, which is usually accomplished automatically when using an approved
application unit. The latex is then applied to the aggregate on the aggre-
gate feed belt just before entry into the drum dryer. Typical latex appli-
cation rates vary from 0.05 percent to 0.10 percent with an estimated
reduction in asphalt binder content of 0.85 percent to 0.40 percent for
marginal aggregates.

Fibers

When compared with conventional HMA, SMA mixtures generally have
a higher asphalt content and have a higher concentration of coarse aggre-
gate. Similarly, Open-Graded Friction Course (OGFC) mixtures typically also
have higher asphalt-binder content along with a uniformly graded aggregate
structure (particles essentially of the same size) and low filler content. These
conditions can cause the asphalt binder to drain from the mixture, by grav-
ity, during transportation and placement, which is commonly referred to as
draindown. To combat draindown, fibers are sometimes added to stabilize

the binder during mixing and placement. Fibers provide the added benefit
of allowing increased asphalt-binder contents and thus increased film
thicknesses, which in turn provide increased durability. Both cellulose and
mineral fibers are used as stabilizers in HMA applications. Mineral fibers are
usually made from virgin basalt, diabase, slag, or other silicate rock.

Scrap Tires (Ground Rubber)

Ground rubber may be sized from particles as large as 34 inch (19 millimeters)
to as fine as the No. 100 sieve (150 microns) depending on the type of size-
reduction equipment and the intended application. Production of fine-grain
tire rubber is achieved by buffing at ambient temperature, while larger pieces
of tire rubber are produced in a cryonic process (at a very low temperature
where rubber is brittle). The production of ground rubber is achieved by
granulators, hammer mills, or fine-grinding machines. Granulators typically
produce particles that are regularly shaped and cubical with a comparatively
low-surface area. The steel-belt fragments are removed by a magnetic sepa-
rator. Fiberglass belts or fibers are separated from the finer rubber particles,
usually by an air separator.

Ground rubber has been used as a fine-aggregate substitute in HMA
pavements. In this process, ground-rubber particles are added to the HMA
as a fine aggregate in a gap-graded friction-course type of mixture. This
process, commonly referred to as the dry process, typically uses ground-
rubber particles ranging from approximately “4 inch (6.4 millimeters)
down to the No. 20 sieve (0.85 millimeter). HMA mixes in which ground-
rubber particles are added as a portion of the fine aggregate are referred
to as rubberized asphalt. Another process is where very fine-grain tire
rubber is homogenized in the asphalt at the refinery or terminal. This is
called terminal blend asphalt rubber.

Roofing-Shingle Scrap (Mineral Matter Only)

There are two types of roofing-shingle scrap. They are referred to as tear-
off roofing shingles and roofing-shingle tabs—also called prompt roofing-
shingle scrap. Tear-off roofing shingles are generated during the demolition
or replacement of existing roofs. Roofing-shingle tabs are generated when
new asphalt shingles are trimmed during production to the required physical
dimensions. The quality of tear-off roofing can be quite variable.

The largest component of roofing shingles (60-70 percent by mass) is
the mineral material. There are several types in each shingle. These can
include ceramic granules (comprising crushed-rock particles—typically
trap rock, coated with colored, ceramic oxides), lap granules (coal slag
ground to roughly the same size as the ceramic granules), backsurfacer
sand (washed, natural sand used in small quantities to keep packaged
shingles from sticking together), and asphalt stabilizer (powered limestone
that is mixed into the asphalt).

Roofing shingles incorporated into HMA mixes not only modify the asphalt
binder but also, depending on the size of the shredded material, function like
an aggregate or mineral filler. Organic-felt and glass-felt particles in particular
tend to function like a mineral filler substitute.

Asphalt concrete is a type of hot mix that meets strict requirements.

An open-grade layer is an HMA mixture designed to have a large volume

SMA is an HMA with a high coarse aggregate content (typically 70-80 per-
cent), a high asphalt content (typically over 6 percent) and a high filler content
(approximately 10 percent by weight). The result is a mixture that has excel-
lent stone-on-stone contact and that is very resistant to rutting problems.

Evolution of HMA Design

Hot mix asphalt pavements have been built in the United States since
the 1860s. The first mix design procedure was developed by Clifford
Richardson. He recognized the importance of material selection, espe-
cially the characteristics of the fine aggregate, and the importance of air-
voids and voids in mineral aggregate. He published his procedures in The
Modern Asphalt Pavement in 1905. His procedure used the Pat Test. This
test procedure consisted of compacting samples of sheet asphalt (a hot-sand
asphalt mix) against a brown manila paper and visually assessing the residual
stains. A heavy stain indicated too much binder; a light stain indicated too
little binder; and a medium stain indicated optimum binder content. Since
Richardson’s initial work, research has continued and. additional and more
sophisticated procedures for developing a mix design have developed. The
most prominent method until World War II was the Hubbard-Field Method,
which was developed in the mid-1920s. This procedure used a stability test
that consisted of determining the maximum load developed on a specimen
2 inches (50 millimeters) in diameter by 1 inch (25 millimeters) in height
being forced through an orifice 1.75 inches (45 millimeters) in diameter.

History of the Marshall Mix Design

Before the introduction of Superpave mix design procedures in the
early 1990s, the majority of HMA pavements constructed in the United
States were designed with either the Marshall or the Hveem mix design
procedures. The Marshall procedure was developed by Bruce Marshall
of the Mississippi Highway Department in 1939. The U.S. Army Corps of
Engineers adapted the procedure during World War II for the design of mili-
tary airfields. The goal was to have a procedure that used simple portable
laboratory equipment that could be used to design pavements that would
support the increase in aircraft wheel loads and tire pressures on the new
aircraft. After World War Il, aircraft wheel loads and tire pressures contin-
ued to increase. The Marshall mix design procedure has been continually

History of the Hveem Mix Design

In the 1930s, Francis Hveem, then the materials and research engineer for
the California Division of Highways, developed a mix design procedure that
was used extensively in the western United States. Hveem introduced the
concept of the kneading compactor so that the laboratory-compacted mix
would be more representative of the field-produced mixes that are being
compacted by steel and pneumatic-tired rollers. In addition to developing
the kneading compactor, he recognized the need to have a mechanical test
that would evaluate the performance of the mix. This need led to the devel-
opment of the Hveem Stabilometer, which is used to evaluate the ability of
an HMA mixture to resist the shear forces applied by traffic. Prior to the
introduction of the Superpave mix design procedure in the early 1990s the
Hveem procedure was used extensively in the western United States.

Adoption of Superpave Mix Design System

From 1988 to 1993, the Federal Highway Administration sponsored and
the Transportation Research Board administered the $150 million Strategic
Highway Research Program (SHRP). The purpose of SHRP was to develop
technology that would result in significant improvements in the way high-
ways were designed and built to address the effect of increasing traffic on
the nation’s highway infrastructure. Approximately $50 million of the SHRP
funding was used to support an asphalt research program. The result of this
research was a new mix design system called Superpave (SUperior PERform-
ing Asphalt PAVEments). Superpave gave the highway industry new tools
for designing and constructing HMA pavements. The first projects designed
with Superpave technology were built in the early 1990s and the procedure
quickly became the standard for the design of HMA pavement mixtures in the
United States and Canada. The airfield community has not fully implemented
Superpave, but research is being conducted (2007) to adapt Superpave to the
different demands of airfields (higher wheel loads and tire pressures).

Resistance to Permanent Deformation

Permanent deformation results from the accumulation of small amounts
of unrecoverable strain from repeated loads applied to the pavement.
Resistance to permanent deformation is provided by designing and con-
structing a stable HMA pavement that will not shove or rut under traffic.
It will maintain its shape and smoothness under repeated loading. An
unstable pavement develops ruts, ripples (washboarding or corrugation),
and other signs of pavement deformation.

Resistance to permanent deformation depends on the internal friction
provided by the aggregate particles and the cohesion provided by the
asphalt binder. Interparticle friction among the aggregate particles is related
to the shape and surface texture of both the fine and the coarse aggregate
and the characteristics of the aggregate gradation. Cohesion results from the
bonding ability and the stiffness of the asphalt binder. A proper degree of
both interparticle friction and cohesion in a mix prevents the aggregate par-
ticles from being moved past one another by the forces exerted by traffic. In
general, the use of more angular aggregate particles with rougher surface
texture increases the stability of the mix.

The binding force of cohesion increases with an increasing loading rate.
Cohesion also increases as the stiffness of the asphalt binder increases or
as the pavement temperature decreases.

Fatigue Resistance

Fatigue resistance is the pavement’s resistance to repeated bending
under wheel loads (traffic). The result of a fatigue failure is fatigue crack-
ing, often called alligator cracking. This type of cracking occurs when the
pavement has been stressed to the limit of its life by repeated load appli-
cations. Air-voids and asphalt stiffness have a significant effect on fatigue
resistance. As the percentage of air-voids in the pavement increases, either
by design or by lack of compaction, pavement fatigue resistance is drasti-
cally reduced. Likewise, a pavement containing asphalt binders that signif-
icantly age or harden have less resistance to fatigue. Research has shown
that the use of a polymer-modified asphalt binder can improve the fatigue
resistance of an HMA mixture.

The thickness and the strength characteristics of the pavement and the
support of the subgrade also have a great deal to do with determining

well-supported pavements do not bend as much under loading as thin
or poorly supported pavements do. Therefore, they have longer fatigue
lives. Fatigue cracking typically initiates at the bottom of an asphalt layer
and migrates toward the surface. This is the result of high-tensile strain at
the bottom of the HMA layer. In recent years, fatigue cracking has been
observed in some pavements, to begin at the top of the pavement and to
migrate down. This cracking is due to high-tensile strains in the surface
of the HMA. It is generally thought that thin HMA pavements experience
fatigue cracking that starts at the bottom of the HMA and that thick HMA
pavements experience fatigue cracking that starts at the HMA surface.

Low-Temperature Cracking

Low-temperature cracking normally occurs when the temperature at the
surface of the pavement drops sufficiently to produce thermally induced
stress in the HMA layer that exceeds the tensile strength of the asphalt mix-
ture. The result is transverse cracks that are perpendicular to the roadway
centerline and are often equally spaced. These cracks start at the surface
and work their way downward. The magnitude and frequency of the low
temperatures and the stiffness of the asphalt binder are the major factors
affecting the severity of the cracking. Low-temperature cracking can be
the result of a single event or of repetitive cycles of cold temperatures that
result in a fatigue type of failure in the mixture. In general, the solution
to this problem is the proper choice of Performance-Graded binder. Using
highly absorptive aggregates can aggravate low-temperature cracking.

Moisture Resistance ;

A major durability problem is associated with moisture damage (commonly

referred to as “stripping”). Stripping involves water or water vapor getting
between the asphalt film and the aggregates, thereby breaking the adhesive
bond between the aggregate and the asphalt binder film. This will “strip” the
asphalt from the aggregate. Pavement failure due to stripping occurs in two
stages: (1) stripping failure, and (2) the failure of the pavement under traffic.
If stripping within the pavement becomes excessive, severe pavement defor-
mation and fatigue cracking will occur as the result of repeated loading. The
failure of the pavement under traffic will generally occur in stages. The first
stage will be a staining of the surface. A local failure is followed by potholes
or by extensive rutting of the pavement in the wheel paths.

The factors that affect moisture damage in an HMA pavement are:

° The properties of the HMA use components (aggregates, asphalt binder,
and the properties of the HMA mixture—voids, voids mineral aggregate
[VMA], and roadway density)

¢ The environmental effects before, during, and after construction (for
example, the stripped layer becoming saturated before placement of a
second lift versus becoming saturated after construction)

¢ Pavement drainage (subsurface and through the layer to a day-lighted

Durability

The durability of an asphalt pavement is its ability to resist factors such
as aging of the asphalt, disintegration of the aggregate, and stripping of
the asphalt film from the aggregate. These factors result from weather,
traffic, or a combination of the two.

Generally, the durability of a mixture can be enhanced by three methods:

° Designing the mix using a dense gradation of moisture-resistant

aggregate

° Maximizing the asphalt film thickness on the aggregate

° Compacting the in-place mixture to 8 percent or less air-voids

Asphalt film thickness is related to the asphalt content and the grade
of asphalt binder. Thick asphalt films do not age and harden as rapidly as
thin ones do. Also, increased film thickness effectively seals off a greater
percentage of interconnected air-voids in the pavement, making it dif-
ficult for water and air to penetrate. A certain percentage of air-voids
must remain in the pavement to allow for expansion of the asphalt in hot
weather and the pressure of traffic acting on the surface.

A dense gradation of sound, tough, moisture-resistant aggregate
contributes to pavement durability. It provides closer contact among
aggregate particles, enhancing the impermeability of the mixture. And
a sound, tough aggregate resists disintegration under traffic loading.

Skid Resistance

Skid resistance is the ability of an asphalt surface to minimize skidding
or slipping of vehicle tires, particularly when the roadway surface is wet.
For good skid resistance, tire tread must be able to maintain contact with
the aggregate particles instead of riding on a film of water on the pave-
ment surface (hydroplaning). Pavement skid resistance is typically mea-
sured at 40 miles per hour (65 kilometers per hour) with a standard tread
tire under controlled wetting of the pavement surface.

A rough pavement surface with many little peaks and valleys will have
greater skid resistance than a smooth surface. Best skid resistance is
obtained with rough-textured aggregate in an open-graded mixture with
an aggregate of about 3% inch (9.5 millimeters) to % inch (12.5 millimeters)
maximum size. Besides having a rough surface, the aggregates must resist
polishing (smoothing) under traffic. Calcareous aggregates polish more eas-
ily than siliceous aggregates. Unstable mixtures that tend to rut or bleed
present serious skid resistance problems.

to place and compact; those with poor workability are difficult to place

15.4.3 Pavement Thickness

A thorough soils investigation should be conducted to determine the
strength of the existing soils and drainage characteristics. The investigation
and design recommendations should make sure that the project is not being
built on unstable soils (such as expansive clays or collapsible soils) that will
cause significant problems. These must be dealt with during design and con-
struction. The drainage of the site must be thoroughly evaluated because
saturated soils can have a dramatic effect on the load-carrying capacity of
the pavement. When areas of poor soils are encountered, their removal and
replacement with a higher-quality material should be considered.

For bicycle/walking paths, the type of traffic that will operate on the sur-
face should be considered. Periodic vehicular traffic, such as emergency,
patrol, snow removal, maintenance, or other vehicles, must be considered in
the design. For most paths, full-depth asphalt provides an excellent surface
because of its speed of construction. The thickness of the HMA surface will
vary from 3 to 5 inches (75 to 125 millimeters) depending on the traffic and
the soil conditions of the site. It may be desirable to place a base course of
crushed stone or reclaimed asphalt pavement to help distribute the load or as
a construction aid to ease the grading and layout of the path.

Tennis courts should generally have 3 to 4 inches (75 to 100 milli-
meters) of HMA placed on a 4- to 8-inch (100 to 200 millimeters) layer
of compacted base course (consisting of crushed aggregate or reclaimed
asphalt pavement). It is also recommended that the subgrade be leveled
and compacted to provide a finish elevation of 4 to 6 inches (100 to 150
millimeters) above the surrounding ground surface. This will provide for
water runoff and vegetation control around the perimeter.

Asphalt Mix Design

The type of mix to be used for bicycle/walking paths and other recre-
ational facilities should be different than that used for parking lots or high-
way facilities. The mix-design criteria used for asphalt pavements designed
for vehicular traffic will not, in most cases, provide a mix of the needed
durability and workability required for recreational facilities. A mix devel-
oped for recreational use will generally contain more asphalt binder, have
less air voids, and be less prone to oxidation, raveling, and cracking than a
typical roadway mix. The objective of the asphalt mix is to provide a cost-
effective mix that will have the following characteristics:

° Sufficient asphalt to provide for a durable surface

° A smooth uniform surface texture

° Sufficient workability to allow ease of handling during construction

and proper compaction

Hot mix asphalt for recreational facilities should have a fine-graded aggre-
gate blend with a nominal maximum size of % inch (12.5 millimeters) or
less. For some applications such as tennis courts, it is recommended that a
nominal maximum size of % inch (9.5 millimeters) or less be considered.
Aggregates used in surface mixes should be free from impurities such as

facility.

Construction Considerations

Before construction, all vegetation and all unsuitable soils should be
removed and the subgrade should be scarified and compacted to at least
95 percent of modified proctor density (ASTM D 1557/AASHTO T 180). After
compaction, the subgrade should be proof-rolled with a heavy pneumatic
roller or dump truck to ensure that there are no soft spots. Any soft spots
identified should be removed and replaced with a stable material. After
compaction, the subgrade should be treated with an herbicide. This must be
done carefully to insure that surrounding vegetation is not adversely affected.
If an aggregate base course is used, it should also be compacted to at least
95 percent of modified proctor density.

For bicycle/walking paths, the asphalt layer can be placed in one lift.
To enhance surface smoothness on tennis courts and running tracks, an
asphalt layer placed in two equal lifts is recommended. Most tennis court
specifications require that tolerance not vary more than % inch in 10 feet
(3 millimeters in 3 meters) when measured in any direction.

15.5. Asphalt Roofs

15.5.1 History

Roofing was probably the world’s first craft because shelter has always
been a primary need of people. Asphalt, with its extraordinary combina-
tion of waterproofing, preservative, and cementing qualities, was an early
discovery of ancient people and has been used extensively in the con-
struction of their buildings and roads.

Today, asphalt is recognized more than ever as an outstanding roofing
material and is used extensively to cover all kinds of buildings, from homes
to factories. Asphalt roofing materials and construction methods are fully
covered by specifications of the American Society for Testing and Materials
(ASTM) and many government and private agencies. In addition, all types of
asphalt roofing are sanctioned by federal and other public agencies.

Asphalt roofs may be built in single- or multiple-layer systems with
impregnated fabrics or with manufactured shingles.

15.5.2 Types of Asphalt Roofing

Prepared Roofing

Asphalt shingles. This type is composed of three basic materials—
asphalt, felt, and mineral granules. The felt is impregnated with asphalt.
Both sides of the saturated felt are then covered with a harder, tougher
coat of asphalt, in which are embedded mineral granules. This composi-
tion is then cut into individual shingles or strips. Asphalt shingles are
available in almost any color from pastels to deep rich tones. Factory
techniques for the application of granules have improved with the devel-
opment of new permanent colors, and granules may be applied in almost .
any blend or pattern of colors desired.

Asphalt roll roofing—smooth-surfaced. This type consists of a single
layer of roofing felt saturated with asphalt and coated on both sides with
a harder asphalt.

Asphalt roll roofing—mineral-surfaced. This type consists of smooth-
surfaced roll roofing in which mineral granules have been embedded on
either one or both sides.

Prepared roofings are those products manufactured and packaged ready
to apply to the roof deck, usually by nailing only. Asphalt shingles and cer-
tain roll roofings are in this category. They consist of a base of saturated
felt to which has been added an asphalt coating and, usually, a surfacing
of mineral granules.

Of the types listed, the shingle roof has been the most common. Asphalt
shingles are applied to the steep roofs of homes, barns, and similar
structures. They fill the need for attractive, efficient, but low-cost roofing
for residential construction.

Asphalt Built-Up Roofing

An asphait built-up roof is composed of several layers of asphalt-saturated
felt applied principally to a flat-roof deck. Asphalt is applied between the felt
layers for adhesion. Over the top layer, a flood coat of asphalt is added and
then covered with gravel or slag.

The built-up roof is a contrasting type, constructed on the flat or nearly
flat tops of very large buildings—industrial, public, and many others.

For built-up roofing, only the components are manufactured and deliv-
ered to the job. The roofing itself is built up directly on the roof deck in
a series of layers or plies, sandwiched between layers of asphalt. A cover
of aggregate is then applied over the final layer. The mopping on of the
asphalt and the spreading of slag or gravel may be accomplished with
the most readily available and simplest equipment. All operations can be
performed quickly by hand application, although mechanical felt-laying
machines, hot-asphalt applicators, and gravel spreaders will do the job
more easily and rapidly on very large roofs.

The heart of the built-up roofing operation is the kettle for heating the
asphalt to the proper application temperature. For efficient operation, the
kettle must be able to bring the asphalt up to this temperature in a short
time without overheating at the start—or at any time during the kettle’s
operation. Overheating may seriously damage the asphalt and consider-
ably reduce the life expectancy of the roof. Overheating may also cause
the asphalt to be applied too thinly, resulting in poor adhesion between
the roofing felts and possible later separation of the plies.

Properly constructed, an asphalt built-up roof will remain in service
condition for many years with little or no maintenance required.

Either prepared or built-up roofing can be used on many roofs, but the
choice is limited somewhat by the slope of the deck to which it is to be
applied. Built-up roofs are usually applied only on the flatter slopes, while
the application of asphalt shingles and other prepared roofing is typically
used for the steeper slopes.

@ Pavement Noise Mitigation

15.6.1 Introduction

Research in Europe and in the United States has shown that it is pos-
sible to build asphalt pavement surfaces that will provide reduced noise
levels. Throughout the world, sound caused by transportation systems is
the number one noise complaint. Noise generated by traffic is one of the
prime offenders. Engine (power train), exhaust, aerodynamic, and tire/
pavement noise all contribute to traffic noise. Modification of pavement
surface type and/or texture can result in significant tire/pavement noise
reductions. A quiet road surface can be built while at the same time con-
sidering safety, durability, and cost using one or more of the following
approaches:

¢ A smooth surface texture using small top-size aggregate

* An open-graded friction course (OGFC) with a high air-voids content

15.6.2 Nature of Noise

Noise, like all other sounds, is a form of acoustic energy. It differs from
pleasant sounds only in that it often disturbs us and has the characteristics
of an uninvited guest. Understanding noise requires a basic knowledge of
sound physics and how humans respond to it.

Sound is fluctuation of air in a wavelike motion. Associated with this
motion is acoustic or sound pressure that is measured in decibels. The
decibel combines the magnitude of sound with how humans hear. Because
human hearing covers such a wide range of sounds, it does not lend
itself to measurement with a linear scale. If a linear scale were used, most
sounds occurring in daily life on a scale of 0 to 1 would be recorded
between 0.00 and 0.01. Thus, it would be difficult to discriminate between
sound levels in our daily lives.

Instead of a linear scale, a logarithmic scale is used to represent sound
levels and the unit is called a decibel or dB. The term dB(A) is used when
referring to the sound levels that have been A-weighted. The ear has its
own filtering mechanisms, and the inclusion of the A after dB indicates
that the scale has been normalized or “fine tuned” to hear like a human.
The decibel scale ranges from 0 dB(A), the threshold of human hearing, to
140 dB(A) where serious hearing damage can occur. Table 15.9 represents
this scale and some of the levels associated with various daily activities.

Alongside a freeway, the sound level (i.e., noise) might be in the range
of 70-80 dB(A). As a rule, an individual can differentiate between two
levels of similar sound that are at least 3 dB(A) different in level.

Noise from traffic is due to a large number of moving noise sources
spaced along an entire roadway. This type of noise is classified as a line
source because noise is transmitted along the entire roadway. As the dis-
tance from the line of source increases, the noise level decreases at a lower
rate than it would from a single-point noise source. This is because the noise
from the more distant sources in the line begins to contribute more noise
relative to that of the closest individual point source. The net result is that
for line sources, a doubling of the distance results in a 3 dB(A) reduction in
the noise level instead of 6 dB(A). Thus, if a point that is 5 meters from the
center of the noise source (the center of the lane) has a noise level of 85
dB(A), then a point 10 meters from the edge of the roadway would have a
noise level of 82 dB(A). This is illustrated in Figure 15.14.

Pavements

reduction in the noise level alongside the roadway. The selection of pave-

As a pavement ages, its noise level increases because of changes in the
surface texture. The HMA surface may start to lose particles, crack at the
surface, and in the case of an OGFC, result in clogged pores from accumu-
lated dust or deicing materials.

Open-Graded Friction Courses

Open-Graded Friction Course (OGFC) surfaces typically have lower
noise levels than dense-graded HMA surfaces. An OGFC can reduce the
noise level 3-5 dB(A), when compared with nonporous HMA. This is
accomplished because the air voids in a pavement provide a means for
air trapped between the tire and the pavement surface to escape, which
provides for increased sound absorption. To achieve this improved noise
performance, the voids need to be interconnected. The added advantage

Miscellaneous Asphalt Uses

Some types of asphalt construction require special consideration of
either the construction methods or the composition of the asphalt mix-
ture. Asphalt as an engineering material is versatile and, when designed
properly, is ideal for many forms of paving and other applications.

15.7.1 Bridge-Deck Paving

General

Asphalt surfaces are desirable for bridge decks because they are not
affected by salts used for de-icing, and if properly constructed they may
waterproof the bridge floor, thus preventing water and salts from damag-
ing it. Asphalt surfaces can also be highly skid-resistant, providing a safe
bridge deck.

Asphalt Mixes

Dense-graded asphalt mixtures are recommended for leveling and inter-
mediate courses. In areas where chemicals are expected to be used to
de-ice the pavement, aggregates that do not react with de-icing chemicals
should be selected. A lightweight aggregate may be desirable to reduce
the weight of the asphalt pavement on the bridge.

Both dense-graded and stone matrix asphalt HMA mixtures have been
used for bridge decks. In many cases on special projects, very stiff polymer-
modified asphalt binders (up to PG 82-22) have been used to prevent
rutting or other forms of permanent deformation in that HMA layer. Also
specially designed asphalt waterproofing membranes have been placed on
the deck before the HMA layer is placed.

15.7.2 Construction Methods

The surface to be paved, whether it is a PCC or a corrugated steel bridge
deck, must be cleaned of all loose and foreign matter. Power brooms and
compressed air tools may be necessary to accomplish this task. The sur-
face of the PCC deck is then given a tack coat of SS-Ih or CSS-1h asphalt
emulsion diluted with an equal amount of water and applied at a rate of
0.05-0.20 gallons per square yard (0.2-0.9 liter per square meter).

After mill scale is removed, steel decks usually require painting with a
heavy asphalt paint. Vertical surfaces are also painted to a depth greater
than the thickness of the subsequent paving. The tack coat is scrubbed in,
thus providing a continuous asphalt film with no holes in the coating.

The next step is to apply a tack coat and then place and compact the

15.7.3 Industrial Floors

General

Asphalt floors are particularly adaptable for use in many factories and
warehouses. Above ground level, they are ordinarily laid on PCC decks. At
or below ground level, they may be designed and constructed similarly to
roadways or parking lots. Asphalt floors are waterproof, highly resistant to
wear, and dustless. The use of a highly siliceous mineral aggregate in the
mix may make it resistant to certain dilutions of acid.

Asphalt Mastic Floors

Originally, an asphalt mastic was prepared using crushed-rock asphalt.
The term now generally applies to a mixture of graded sand, mineral filler,
and asphalt cement quite similar in character to sheet-asphalt paving. Fac-
tory and warehouse floors are frequently subjected to very heavy unit
loads, carried on small-diameter narrow metal wheels. When confronted
by this condition, the regular sheet-asphalt mixture is modified by using
a harder asphalt binder (such as a PG 70-22 or PG 76-22) and a higher
proportion of very fine mineral filler. A maximum thickness of less than
1 inch (25 millimeters) is typically recommended.

Asphalt Floors

Dense-graded asphalt such as that used for paving highways and air-
ports is particularly well adapted for paving warehouse and factory floors.
Asphalt itself is unaffected by acids, but the mineral aggregate should be
acid-resistant. Care should taken to design and compact the mixture to
produce high density and waterproofing.

15.7.4 Roof Deck Parking

General

The roofs of many modern structures serve as parking areas. Asphalt
paving is particularly adaptable to roof parking lots for reasons similar to
those for bridge-deck paving.

The importance of proper drainage cannot be overemphasized. To ensure
rapid runoff of water to drains, the surface should be pitched or graded at
a 2 percent slope or % inch per foot (20 millimeters per meter).

Asphalt pavement transfers large moving and static loads to the roofing
membrane. Any lateral movement in the membrane causes the pavement
to move. Any differential heaving under an asphalt pavement under load
causes distress or failure to the underlying membrane. Also, any excess
or free asphalt in the membrane is absorbed into the wearing surface and
causes it to soften and possibly bleed.

If an asphalt pavement is placed over a deck area constructed of prestressed
concrete beams, reflection cracks are likely to come through the pavement
at the junction between successive beams. To minimize reflection cracking,
some type of stress-absorbing membrane can be placed over each joint. Such
a membrane also helps provide a tighter seal against the passage of water.

The two types of roof-deck paving are (a) slab and pavement with a
built-in slip plane and (b) slab and pavement bonded together.

Free-Movement Plane Construction

Free-movement plane construction provides a distinct, free-movement
plane between the structural roof slab and the pavement.

A positive bond breaker, such as a lime or fine sand, is spread over the con-
crete deck to allow freedom of movement between the concrete deck and the
pavement. A conventional built-up roof consisting of four or five plies of light-
weight roofing felt is placed on the roof. However, the final flood coat and
aggregate cover is not applied. In its place, a very light tack coat of diluted
SS-1h or CSS-1h asphalt emulsion is applied to the top felt layer. On top of
this, an HMA layer is placed and compacted. A minimum thickness of 2 inches
(50 millimeters) is recommended, and a very densely graded mix having a
maximum aggregate size of about % inch (9.5 millimeters) should be used.

Bonding Roof Deck and Pavement

To bond pavement to a roof deck after cracks and joints have been
sealed, a conventional four- to five-ply built-up roof is applied. Each layer,
except the surface of the top layer, is spot mopped with asphalt. No mop-
ping is done within 15 inches (375 millimeters) of a PCC slab joint. The
top layer is then covered with 2~3 inches (50-75 millimeters) of a dense-
graded HMA. Another method is to use an asphalt membrane that contains
mineral filler and fibers for body and strength. The membrane is carefully
spread to ensure a uniform thickness. Often, a protective board %—4 inch
(3-6 millimeters) thick is placed over the membrane. The membrane or
board is overlaid with one or two lifts of HMA having an asphalt content
higher than normal. The total thickness of the mix should not exceed
3 inches (75 millimeters). The aim is to obtain an impervious mixture that
weathers well and has high stability or strength.

Small rollers or sidewalk rollers are often used because of the restricted
working area and the maximum allowable loads for the building. Small
hand-operated electric or gasoline-powered vibratory compactors are used
to work around areas inaccessible to rollers.

15.8 Leaching Pads

Because asphalt is an inert material, it can be used to construct all sorts
of structures for handling very corrosive materials.

15.8.1 Uses of Leaching Pads

Leaching pads are used to extract metals from low-grade ores, such as

copper, gold, etc. Acid solutions are sprayed over stockpiles of the ore, and

‘the solutions leach out the metals. The metals are then recovered from the
leachates.

15.8.2 Design of Leach Pads

Leach pads are designed using hot mix asphalt with a high binder con-
tent of a soft grade to assure a flexible pavement capable of withstanding
some subgrade settlement.

To ensure that the pad pavement is impermeable, leach pads are sealed
with a high softening point (polymer-modified) asphalt.

Also included in the design are dikes, channels, and retention ponds for
gathering and holding the leachate.

15.8.3 Materials

Mix Binder—The binder selection should be one or two grades softer
than what would usually be used for asphalt pavements in the climatic
area where the pads are to be constructed. Example: If a PG 70-10 is the
normal paving grade used, then select either a PG 64-10 or a PG 58-10.

Mix aggregate—Use a %-inch (19 millimeters) nominal-size aggregate,
with a dust-to-binder ratio of at least 1, and design for 0 air voids.

Membrane seal—Use a 170/200 pen or high softening point material
that is applied at the rate of 0.35 gallon per square yard (1.10 liters per
square meter).

15.8.4 Construction of Leaching Pads

The following steps should be followed in constructing a leaching pad.
1. Prepare the subgrade and compact to a uniform density.
2. Place 4 inches (100 millimeters) of the hot mix asphalt and compact
to 96 percent of maximum specific gravity.

15.9 Environmental

Applications of Asphalt

15.9.1 General

For decades, many civil engineering solutions to environmentally sensi-
tive projects have incorporated asphalt. It has been used because it is flex-
ible, strong, chemically stable and nonreactive, impermeable, recyclable,
economical, and readily available. All sorts of asphalt materials are used in
environmental applications: sprayed-on membranes of emulsified asphalt,
asphalt cement, and modified asphalt, dense-graded HMA, open-graded
HMA, hydraulic mixes, sand mixes, and mastic asphalt liners.

Contrary to the fears of some environmental groups, scientists have
established that asphalt, in the form of an emulsion or incorporated into a
mixture of aggregates, does not leach into water, contaminate soil, or kill
fish. The results of leachability tests of new HMA show very low levels of
leachable compounds, well below any current Environmental Protection
Agency guidelines. Scientists have proved that asphalt is an environmen-
tally safe material.

15.9.2 Examples of Environmental Uses of
Asphalt

1. Fish hatcheries. Oregon and Washington have more than 35
asphalt-lined fish hatchery ponds. These ponds are used to raise
fingerling fish until they are large enough to be released. A typical
hatchery pond is 5-7 feet (1.5-2.1 meters) deep and covers about
a half-acre (0.2 hectares). The pond liner is constructed of 2 or
3 inches (50-75 millimeters) of HMA over 8 inches (200 milli-
meters) of aggregate base course. Most liners are sealed with an
asphalt emulsion so that bacteria cannot inhabit the tiny surface
irregularities in the HMA liner.

2. Water reservoir liners. Throughout the world, many asphalt-
lined water reservoirs have been constructed over the past 100
years. The design of a reservoir liner must address permeability,
durability, flexibility, resistance to weathering, the ability to absorb
stresses during filling and drawdown cycles, long-term loading
stresses caused by settlement in the supporting subgrade, and the
weight of maintenance vehicles when the reservoir is periodically
cleaned. The composite liners required for large drinking water
reservoirs are typically designed using both dense-graded and
open-graded HMA. Dense-graded mixes provide an impermeable

Landfill caps. HMA can be used to protect the environment by

15.10 Uses of HMA for Race

and Test Tracks

15.10.1 Introduction

Whether a racetrack or a test track, a track almost certainly will be
paved with HMA—the only material that can consistently provide the
smooth surfaces necessary for vehicles traveling at such high speeds (see
Figure 15.16). HMA pavements can be constructed to smoothness stan-
dards unmatched by any other surfacing type.

Each track has its own unique characteristics as to length, shape, and bank-
ing angles. Racetracks are usually oval in shape but can be quad- or tri-ovals
and have banking angles that range from 12 to 36 degrees. Test tracks, on the
other hand, usually are oval; however, some older tracks are circular in shape.
Slopes on test tracks can range from the normal 2-percent cross-slope typi-
cal of a normal pavement, to as much as a 70-degree cross-slope. The greater
slopes are needed to provide neutral steer though the turns while vehicles are
tested at high speeds.

Test facilities not only have high speed tracks but generally incorporate
road courses, stability testing pads, and/or segments of pavements that
duplicate existing pavements. These test facilities are a way to compare
how vehicles react to different pavement conditions.

Pavements for test tracks and racetracks are constructed in all sorts of
climate conditions, and these conditions must be considered during pave-
ment design and construction. These tracks provide very wide, paved sur-
faces, which make them susceptible to thermal cracking. The pavement is
subjected not to heavy loads but to the scrubbing action of the side thrust
of the tire as the vehicles go through the turns of the track. Therefore,
track pavements need to be stable and durable.

Drainage is another important factor. The pavement design should
include a drainage layer within the pavement structure. Special attention
must be given to the areas behind the crash wall and under the stands.
Any water from these areas must be intercepted.

15.10.2 Materials Requirements

Binders

The selected binder should be at least two PG grades higher than that
commonly used for the area where the track will be constructed. The
binder should have a minimum softening point of 180°F (82°C).

The selected binder should be two PG grades higher on the high-
temperature side and can be one grade less to minimize thermal cracking;
the low-temperature grade should be one or two grades lower than that
usually selected, compared with a normal highway binder grade in that
climate. For instance, instead of using a PG 64-22, a PG 76-28 or PG 82-28
should be considered.

Binders for the underlying layer of the pavement structure can be the
same unmodified binder as would be selected by the local DOT for that
region of the state where the track is located. ,

Aggregates

The aggregates used in track surfacing must be a hard, durable material
that provides good surface friction. Granite and slag make good aggre-
gates; however, other sources of hard, non-polishing, durable aggregates
can be used. Very hard, angular aggregates should be avoided because
they tend to shred rubber from tires, resulting in slower races with more
frequent pitstops for tire changes.

The same aggregates used for highway construction can be used for the
underlying layers of the pavement structure.

Mix Design

Mix designers should consider high stability and high density as their main
objectives. Stone matrix asphalt mixes with their high binder content and
thicker mortar should be considered when the local paving industry has
experience using this type of mix regularly. The Indianapolis Speedway used
an SMA mix.

A 75-blow Marshall or a minimum 100 gyration Superpave mix design
is appropriate for race track mixes. A gradation of % inch (19 millimeters)

45.10.3 Construction Practices

Race and test track construction is very complex construction work. This
is especially true for the paving contractor. The contractor not only deals
with closer tolerances, a smoother surface, and higher densities, but also
must accomplish those tasks while working on steep slopes. Construction
work certainly tests the ingenuity of the contractor’s personnel.

Anyone contemplating being involved in race or test track design or
construction should consult with a person who has previously built a
track.

For slopes 18 degrees or less, paving procedures are similar to normal
highway paving. On slopes between 18 and 24 degrees, the rollers need
lateral support, which can usually be accomplished from the lower side
(Figure 15.17). Care must be taken to ensure that the lower portion of
the roller does not dig into the mix. Material transfer vehicles are needed
to deliver the mix to the paving machine. Adjustments must be made to
the paving machine, to ensure proper delivery of the mix to the screed.
Once the slope is steeper than 20 degrees, all the equipment from the
top or bottom of the track must be anchored. Crawler tractors, backhoes,
or cranes make good deadmen that can move along the top of the track
at the paving speed. Heavily weighted crawler-mounted machines con-
nected by I-beams of different lengths can be used to anchor the paver
from the bottom of the slope. Large crab-steering, shoulder-widening
machines can be used in the same configuration to hold up compaction
rollers.

Test tracks pose some added problems. To maintain neutral steer, they
require parabolic cross sections. Normal screeds on paving machines can-
not be configured to the required shape. This limitation necessitates the
use of a screed with several sections. These special screeds usually have a
break at 2-foot (0.6-meter) intervals. Compaction of the pavement on these
parabolic sections also poses a problem. Either a roller with split drums or
a series of smaller, narrow rollers are required.

Consideration should be given to diamond grinding the underlying layer
before placing the surface course, eliminating the need to solve roughness
problems with just the final surface.

Joints are very important and must be properly constructed to ensure
good smoothness and proper density. The number of transverse construc-
tion joints should be minimized, and where production allows, a single
paving lane should extend one full lap around the track. All joints should
be tack-coated.

15.11 Porous Pavements

15.11.11 Introduction

A porous asphalt pavement can be used to reduce the runoff from
parking lots and paved roads. A porous asphalt pavement consists of a
permeable asphalt surface placed over a granular working platform on top
of a reservoir of large single size stone. The asphalt surface is an open-
graded friction course designed using the principles in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix
Asphalt (HMA) Design.” The lower reservoir of large single-size stone has
sufficient voids to create adequate storage capacity. A properly designed
porous asphalt pavement can provide a solution to storm-water runoff
problems as well as groundwater table recharge. The National Asphalt
Pavement Association’s Design, Construction, and Maintenance Guide
for Porous Asphalt Pavements, Information Series 131, contains detailed
information on the design and construction of porous asphalt pavements.
The following summarizes that information.

15.11.2 General Design Principles

A porous asphalt pavement consists of a porous asphalt course (usually
an OGFC), a top filter course (of %-inch [12.5 millimeters] aggregate), a
reservoir course of aggregate (114-3 inches; 37.5-75 millimeters thick), a
filter fabric and the existing soil or subgrade material. Figure 15.18 shows
a typical porous pavement. The site conditions, such as soil characteristics,
local topography, climate, and traffic are the key items in determining the
applicability of a porous pavement for a specific site. In addition, local
storm-water regulations must be followed. The porous pavement should be
designed for a 25-year/24-hour storm event. Other environmental factors
must be considered. Frost penetration must be evaluated in determining
the reservoir course thickness. Porous pavements are not recommended
for barren areas subject to moderate to high winds because of the pres-
ence of blowing dust in those areas. Dust, sand, and salt used for snow
and ice removal should be kept off a porous pavement because they can
clog the voids in the porous pavement surface.

Soil and Subgrade Conditions

The subgrade on which the porous system is placed is a major fac-
tor in the success of a porous pavement. A thorough soil investigation
must be conducted, including determining infiltration measurements per-
formed at the approximate bottom of the porous pavement structure. These
measurements can include simple percolation tests. These tests should be

15.11.3 Construction

Constructing the porous pavement near the end of any other construc-
tion work prevents other construction debris from clogging the porous
surface. The site area should be protected from excessive heavy equip-
ment operating on the surface, which compacts the soil and reduces per-
meability. The following is a summary of the recommended construction
sequence:

1. Excavate the subgrade using either track equipment or vehicles
with over-sized tires to reduce the amount of compaction of the
subgrade.

2. Place a filter fabric on the uncompacted subgrade.

3. Place 1% to 3 inches (37.5 to 75 millimeters) of rock on the filter
fabric in lifts to the design thickness, keeping construction equip-
ment off the fabric. It should be lightly compacted with plate
compactors or light rollers.

4, Place a thin 1- to 2-inch (25 to 50 millimeters) layer of %-inch
(12.5 millimeters) crushed aggregate over the rock layer.

5. Place a 2- to 4inch (50 to 100 millimeters) layer of an OGFC. The
OGFC should be designed as discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix
Asphalt (HMA) Design.” A polymer-modified asphalt should be
considered.

15.11.4 Maintenance

The porous pavement should be inspected periodically to check for
surface ponding, which indicates possible clogging. The porous surface
can be vacuumed, flushed or jet washed to help maintain the pavement’s
porosity.

15.12 Colored Asphalt

Although not widely done, asphalt pavements can be colored something
other than the usual shades of black and grey. The first coloring method
is to use a natural-color aggregate and the wearing (surface) course. As
the asphalt binder wears way from the surface with traffic, the aggregate
is exposed, revealing its natural color. A second way to color asphalt is
to use an additive in the asphalt binder such as an iron oxide (rust) to
give it a reddish tint. Green, yellow, and orange are also possible with
different ores. Other colors require different metal compounds. A spe-
cial synthetic binder (resin) that contains no asphaltenes has been used
because it colors more readily. By tinting the asphalt binder, the hot mix
asphalt pavement is colored full depth, so there are no surface wear-off
concerns. A third way to color asphalt is to coat the surface with a material
that penetrates the voids and bonds well to an asphalt pavement, such as
an epoxy-fortified acrylic emulsion. Many colors are available with these
types of surface coatings. Care should be taken that these surface coatings
do not negatively affect surface friction if the pavement is used for vehicu-
lar traffic. One possible disadvantage of these systems is that they may
wear off with time and need to be renewed. The second and third options
require the use of specialty products, and more information about them
can be obtained by contacting individual manufacturers.

Warm Mix Asphalt
Pavements

New technologies have been developed that allow asphalt mixtures to be
produced and placed at significantly lower temperatures. These technologies
are called warm mix asphalt (WMA). With these technologies, temperatures
can be reduced by as much as 30 percent but still allow the asphalt binder
to adequately coat the aggregate during mixing at the plant and achieve the
desired workability at the paving site. These methods reduce the viscosity and
increase the workability of a given asphalt binder at a given temperature.

15.13.1 Potential Benefits

The following benefits in reducing mix production and paving tempera-
tures through WMA are most often mentioned:

° Decreasing the energy and fuel consumed to make HMA

° Reducing possible emissions and odors from plants

¢ Improving the working conditions at the plant and paving site

° Allows for cold-temperature paving

Reducing emissions is especially critical in and around nonattainment
areas such as large metropolitan areas and cities that have tight air quality
restrictions.

Other potential benefits of WMA are less recognized. Some contractors
and agencies are exploring the use of WMA in order to extend the paving
season in cool weather. Depending on location and climate, paving proj-
ects are typically prohibited after a certain calendar date because colder
temperatures lessen the time available for the rolling operation to compact
the mix to the desired in-place density. Through WMA technology, mixes
can remain workable at cooler temperatures, increasing the window of
opportunity for compaction. This may make WMA a feasible option for
those end-of-season projects whose completions before winter without
compromising quality are so important.

Early indications are that WMA technology can provide all these benefits
without sacrificing the long-term performance of the asphalt pavement.
However, before its widespread adoption, WMA technology must prove
that is provides the same long-term performance as regular HMA.

15.13.2 Various WMA Technologies

WMA was first developed and used in Europe and came to the United
States in 2002. The following sections provide a brief overview of the various
WMA processes. Many are proprietary and not available in all geographic

areas. Specifying engineers and agencies should check with the local binder

Organic (Wax-Like) Additives

Special organic wax-like additives are mixed with the binder to reduce
viscosity at temperatures above about 212°F (100°C) and then increase
the viscosity at temperatures below about 212°F (100°C). The shift from
viscosity reduction to viscosity increase in the binder occurs near the con-
gealing point of the wax.

Mineral Additives

A number of minerals that have water bound internally have been used
as additives in order to introduce water into an HMA mixture. The water
is liberated gradually over time, resulting in an almost continuous foam-
ing action. This foaming action of the liquid binder acts as an extender
and lubricant that enables the mix to be workable at lower temperatures.
Production temperatures can be reduced to the 266°-293°F (130°-145°C)
range. The materials are added in a manner that produces up to 0.6 percent
water by weight of total mix.

Low-Energy Asphalt

Another WMA process, developed in France, uses wet fine aggregate.
This process is called Low-Energy Asphalt (LEA). A key to the energy say-
ings in this process is that it takes five times more energy to turn water into
steam than it takes to heat the aggregate from 32°F to 212°F (0°-100°C).
Fine aggregates tend to have the highest moisture contents. In the LEA
process, the coarse-aggregate fraction is dried and heated to a tempera-
ture of approximately 300°F (149°C). A specially formulated surfactant is
then added to the binder. The coarse aggregates are then coated with the
binder, which results in a thick film coating because the fine aggregate
has not yet been added. The wet fine aggregate is then mixed with the
asphalt-coated coarse aggregate, resulting in a foaming action that aids in
the coating of the fine aggregate.

Foam Processes

Foamed asphalt is created by injecting cold water, typically 1-5 percent,
into the hot asphalt binder using special equipment. The water rapidly
evaporates, producing a large volume of foam, which slowly collapses.
Foamed asphalt differs from foaming mineral additives in that the water
and asphalt binder are directly mixed together in the presence of com-
pressed air. The expanded foamed binder is then injected directly into the
mixing chamber containing the aggregate. In a variation of the foamed
process, the aggregates are heated and coated with a very soft binder. A
very hard asphalt binder is then foamed into the mixing chamber. The
combined soft and hard binder produces the intended binder grade.

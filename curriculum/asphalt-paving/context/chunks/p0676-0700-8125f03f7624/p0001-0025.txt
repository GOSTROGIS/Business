Hot In-place Recycling (HIPR) is an on-site, in-place method for
rehabilitating pavements. The process is used to correct surface distress
such as surface cracking, raveling, low friction values, and distortion con-
fined to the wearing course such as corrugations and shoving. It cannot
be used to correct structural failures. By eliminating the cost of transport-
ing, processing, and stockpiling the RAP and new HMA, HIPR can be very
economical compared with alternative treatments.

The process softens the existing HMA pavement structure with heat,
removes or scarifies the surfacing material, mixes the material with a
soft asphalt or a recycling agent and additional aggregate or HMA (if
required), and places and compacts the new recycled asphalt surface on
the roadway. One advantage of this process is that the thickness of any
new overlay pavement can be reduced because the surface distress in the
existing pavement is eliminated. Thus, current elevations and the overhead
clearances are preserved.

The three categories of HIPR are surface recycling, repaving, and remix-
ing. The first two processes involve heating, scarification, rejuvenation,
and replacement of the top 1-1% inches (25-37.5 millimeters) of the exist-
ing pavement. The repaving process combines surface recycling with the
placement of a new HMA lift directly on the recycled but yet uncompacted
material. The new material is not mixed with the recycled material but is
compacted as a single layer. The recycled depth in the repaving process is
still 1-1% inches (25-37.5 millimeters) and the new overlay is placed up to
a depth of 2 inches (50 millimeters). The remixing process involves blend-
ing virgin HMA directly with the scarified existing material in a pugmill
and placing it to a depth of 2 inches (50 millimeters).

13.4.1 Pavement Assessment

HIPR is used to correct surface defects in an HMA pavement surface. It
is not the appropriate treatment for a pavement that has base-course or
subgrade failures. The thickness of the existing HMA layer should be at
least 3 inches (75 millimeters) and should not contain a geotextile inter-
layer within the recycling depth. The presence of surface treatments, rub-
berized crack sealants, thermoplastic paint stripes, and specialty mixes
(such as an open-graded friction course or an SMA mix containing an
asphalt-rubber binder), may present problems and require approaches to
surface rehabilitation other than HIPR.

13.4.2 Material Evaluation

The mix design of an HIPR project is developed using samples obtained
from the existing roadway. The frequency of the sampling program will
vary with the size and uniformity of the project. Samples should be
obtained at a minimum of two sampling locations per lane-mile (or about
one per lane-kilometer). The amount of material obtained from each sam-
pling location will vary according to the amount of laboratory testing
to be done. The sample for a mix design test should be larger than that
needed when only thickness, gradation, and asphalt content are needed.
Both core samples and chunk samples (obtained without water) should
be taken. Large core samples are preferred (greater than 6 inches [150
millimeters] diameter). The samples should be tested to determine the in
situ moisture content.

The core samples should be evaluated to determine the thicknesses of
the various layers, the presence of paving geotextiles, and evidence of
stripping or anything that may lead to delamination. After inspection, the

Binder Evaluation and Blending Charts

The first step in developing a mix design for an HIPR mix is to deter-
mine how to rejuvenate the asphalt binder in the existing pavement. As the
existing pavement has been exposed to the elements, the asphalt binder in
the surface mix has been exposed to air, water, and sunlight, resulting in
the oxidation and hardening of the asphalt binder. One of the benefits of
the HIPR process is the ability to rejuvenate this binder and return it to its
original properties. This can be accomplished by adding a recycling agent,
a soft asphalt, or a combination of the two.

Mix Design

When the amount of asphalt, new aggregates, and new HMA has been
determined from the blending processes previously described, they should
be combined and compacted using appropriate mix design procedures for
the traffic volume of roadway for which the mix is being designed. The
volumetrics of the mix (air-voids, voids mineral aggregate, and voids filled
with asphalt) should be determined and the properties of the combined
asphalt binder should be checked to ensure that the needs of the project
are being met. If these properties do not meet established criteria, the mix
proportions should be adjusted.

of an existing pavement, scarifying the softened HMA, adding a recycling

The drying and heating of the existing pavement surface is accom-

length of time the pavement surface is exposed to the heat. If the ambient
conditions are such that the pavement is not being properly heated, the
time the surface is exposed to the heat source must be increased. This can
be done by slowing the rate at which the source moves across the surface
or by adding heating units.

In the single-stage method, the loose material is directed into a mixing

The multistage process (Figure 13.16) consists of one or more pre-heater

machine and compaction equipment. The pre-heater provides initial heat-

The second unit follows directly behind the first unit and continues
the heating and removal process. The distance between these two units
must be kept to a minimum to ensure the least amount of heat loss. The
second unit picks up the milled material from the first unit as it mills
1-inch below the windrow. This unit lifts and removes the loose material.
If required, new HMA is dropped into a hopper on the second unit. From
the hopper, it is dispensed evenly over the windrow from the first unit.
This material is carried over the heating chamber by a slat conveyor that
has a variable-speed control that matches the speed of the second unit. It
is dropped into the windrow created by the final milling head. This milling
head mills the second 1-inch layer of the softened material into a windrow.

13.5 Cold-Mix Recycling

13.5.7 Introduction

Cold-mix recycling is the process of using RAP as a component material
when producing a cold mix asphalt material. The RAP is obtained either
by excavating the asphalt pavement from an existing pavement and then
crushing and screening it to the desired gradation or by milling the RAP
from the roadway using milling equipment, as previously discussed. After
the RAP has been removed from the roadway and processed as necessary,
it is mixed with an asphalt emulsion, a recycling agent, or with a foamed
asphalt and additional aggregate (if necessary). The cold-mix recycling
process can be accomplished in either a central plant—where the RAP is
hauled to a central location similar to how RAP is used as a component in
HMA—or in an in-place process on the roadway.

13.5.2 Central Plant (CP) Cold Recycling

General

Millings and recovered asphalt pavement that may need crushing are an
excellent source of material for constructing an asphalt-treated base or as a
patching material for maintenance of HMA pavements. A discussion of cold
mixing is included in Chapter 15, “Special Uses of Asphalt and HMA.”

Materials

RAP is produced by milling, ripping, breaking, crushing, or pulverizing
the roadway. RAP is hauled to a central location. RAP material handling
and storage is similar to that of conventional aggregate. It should be stored
in conical stockpiles of 10 feet (3 meters) or less. Conical piles are rec-
ommended because RAP has a tendency to re-agglomerate in large piles.
A crust will form on the first 8-12 inches (200-250 millimeters) of the pile.
The crust and the use of conical piles will help shed water from precipita-
tion. The crust is easily broken with a front-end loader. The RAP stockpile
will hold water and not drain like a conventional aggregate stockpile. The
use of a horizontal stockpile will result in a greater accumulation of mois-
ture (up to 7-8 percent).

The sources of RAP for central plant cold recycling vary (they can come
from different projects, contain chip seals and slurry seals, and/or mainte-
nance patches). Thus, many agencies require that the RAP not be blended or
commingled with RAP from other projects. When this policy is followed, the
stockpiles should be kept separated and identified by source (preferably on a
map and with signs identifying the material in each stockpile). When they are

13.6 Cold In-Place Recycling

13.6.1 General

The Cold In-Place Recycling (CIPR) process consists of milling the exist-
ing pavement to a depth of 3-5 inches (75-125 millimeters), screening,
crushing, and mixing with an asphalt emulsion, foamed asphalt, additives
(lime or cement), and adding aggregate if needed. The recycled mixture is
then placed and compacted using conventional paving equipment or by
motor grader.

Project selection is critical to the success or failure of a CIPR project. It must
include an assessment of the existing pavement condition, types of distress,
and modes of failure. To design the required overlay thickness, the pavement
materials (base, subbase, and subgrade) must be sampled and tested. In addi-
tion, an evaluation of the history of the pavement maintenance operations
and the expected traffic volume must be included in the analysis.

Most pavement distresses, such as fatigue cracking, transverse cracking,
reflection cracking, and raveling, can be successfully corrected using CIPR.
Using CIPR eliminates damaged layers, resulting in a crack-free layer that pro-
vides a base for the HMA surface layer. For CIPR to be effective in providing
a long-life pavement structure, as much of the existing HMA layer as possible
should be treated. When considering the depth of recycling, the designer
must consider the ability of the pavement structure to support the recycling
equipment operating on a weakened pavement section as the existing materi-
als are being processed. It is suggested that at least 70 percent of the existing
pavement thickness be treated and that at least 1-2 inches (25-50 millimeters)
of pavement remain in place to support the weight of the recycling train. If
there are paved shoulders, they should be treated to prevent propagation of
cracks into the driving lane. See Figure 13.20 for a typical pavement for cold
in-place recycling. The right side of the figure shows the cold in-place pave-
ment and the left side is the pavement before recycling.

Badly rutted pavements with excessively high asphalt content can be cor-
rected with CIPR, but additional aggregate may be necessary to offset the
high-asphalt content in the existing roadway. Hydrated lime, Portland cement
or Class C fly ash are often used as additives to stiffen the mixtures and
reduce curing time for CIPR. If additional aggregates are used, they should
be crushed angular materials in an amount limited to 25 percent by weight of
the RAP. A pavement that failed because of a wet unstable base, subbase, or
subgrade is generally not a suitable candidate for CIPR. A pavement exhibiting
this type of failure should be considered for full-depth reclamation (discussed
in the next section) or for complete reconstruction, along with pavements
where the underlying soils are expansive in nature and/or where the asphalt
layer is badly stripped.

13.6.2 Mix Design

There is no nationally recognized method for the design of CIPR mixes,
although many agencies have developed procedures for doing so. The
simplest methods consist of using empirical equations or the experience
of the mix-design engineer to establish suggested asphalt content for the
mix based on the amount and consistency of the asphalt binder in the
RAP. The complex methods include using resilient modulus, stability, and
moisture sensitivity tests. Adjustments are made in the field to the initial

additive (asphalt binder, fly ash, and/or lime) amounts to optimize the
performance of the mixture. The following text outlines some of the con-

Sampling Existing Pavement

The first step in obtaining samples of the existing roadway is to conduct
a visual survey of the project so that the sampling plan will result in suffi-
cient samples to fully describe the materials in the roadway. It is suggested
that samples be taken at a minimum of one per each half mile (kilometer)
of roadway. The sample should be full-depth—either a core sample, a bulk
sample obtained by sawing or milling, or a combination. A core sample
should be at least 6 inches (150 millimeters) in diameter. Samples obtained
from coring or sawing can be processed in a laboratory crusher to simu-
late the effect of a milling machine during construction. The samples used
in the mix design should be from the actual layer(s) of the pavement that
will be cold recycled. Samples should be obtained during construction to
compare with the mix design materials and thus provide a basis for revi-
sion of the design quantities for the additives.

Materials Selection and Mix Design Process

The properties of the aggregate and recovered asphalt binder content
should be determined. The asphalt binder should be extracted from the
RAP using ASTM D 6307 or AASHTO T 308, “Test Method for Asphalt Con-
tent of Asphalt Cement of Hot Mix Asphalt by the Ignition Method.” After
extraction, the asphalt binder content and the gradation of the extracted
aggregate should be determined. If additional aggregate needs to be added
to improve the mix stability of the final CIPR layer, this is the gradation
that should be used. The addition of an aggregate with a high percentage
of crushed coarse aggregate can make significant improvements in the
stability of the mix. The percentage of new aggregate to be added to the
mix should be determined using standard blending procedures to provide
a mix with the desired gradation.

Additional asphalt binder is necessary for facilitating compaction, improv-
ing the cohesion of the new mix, and reducing the stiffness of the aged
asphalt binder in the RAP. Typically, 0.5-1.5 percent additional residual binder
is needed. The more uncoated new aggregate used in the mix, the higher
the additional binder content will be. For most mix designs, this amount is
based on previous experience in ensuring the coating of the RAP material.
The amount of binder selected during the mix design for most projects is a
starting point, with changes made in the field. The binder can be an emulsi-
fied asphalt cationic or anionic slow-set emulsion, or a high-float emulsion.
Polymer-modified versions of these materials are also used to improve early
strength and to reduce rutting and thermal cracking. The binder can also be
a foamed asphalt.

When using emulsions, some moisture generally must be added to provide
for coating of the RAP particles. The water can be added at the cutting
head of the milling machine or directly to the CIPR mixture. A coating test
is recommended. This test consists of mixing a quantity of RAP (typically
2 pounds; 1000 grams) of RAP with various amounts of water at 0.5 percent
increments. The water is mixed into the RAP and then the asphalt binder
is added. Any admixtures (lime, fly ash, or cement) to be included should
be added to the mix before the asphalt binder is added. Adding dust-sized
materials will generally increase the amount of moisture needed. They
are mixed by hand in a bowl, and the dispersion of the materials is visu-
ally monitored. The lowest moisture content that results in no additional
increase in curing is selected. The result of the asphalt binder and the
added moisture is referred to as the fluids content in the mix.

Mix design samples are manufactured using the RAP, the proposed
asphalt binder, and water content (which make up the total fluids con-
tent). Generally, the mixing is done at ambient temperatures of 68°-77°F
(20°-25°C). The samples are mixed at the total fluid content discussed
previously, and the proposed asphalt binder is varied above and below the
initial additive content by 0.7 percent. The mix water is adjusted to keep
the total fluids content the same for all samples. The samples are either
manufactured using a 50-blow Marshall compaction at ambient field con-
ditions or can be compacted with a Superpave gyratory compactor. Studies
have shown a compaction level of about 35 gyrations is appropriate.

Before further testing, the samples must be cured to eliminate their
excess water and for the emulsions to set up to develop their maximum
strength. The curing process varies with the agency doing the testing.
The samples are cured in the mold for 4 hours at 140°F (60°C) and then
extruded from the mold and cured until they reach a constant mass.

After the samples are manufactured, the volumetric properties are deter-
mined using the procedures discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA)
Design.” CIPR samples may break up during the standard bulk specific
gravity test, and thus the CoreLok procedure discussed in Chapter 4 should
be used. Strength testing—such as Marshall stability and flow, unconfined
compression, and resilient modulus tests—has been used to evaluate the
CIPR mixes. But no guidelines have been established. The job-mix formula
used as a starting point in the field is based on the coating of the RAP mate-
rials and the optimum density of the mix.

Single-Unit Train

The single-unit train consists of a milling machine that does the milling,
RAP sizing, and blending at the cutting head. See Figure 13.21. A disad-

cracking because it is so difficult to control the maximum particle size. A
spray bar or foaming nozzles are located in the cutting chamber to add the

an internal tank on the milling machine or from a tanker truck pushed or
pulled by the milling machine. Aggregate or dry additives to be added to
the RAP can be incorporated into the mix by spreading the material on the
pavement ahead of the milling operation. The processed RAP is placed in
a windrow and picked up by a windrow pickup device, or it is deposited
directly into a paver for placement on the roadway.

capability. The particle size is controlled in the same manner as in the

13.6.4 Placement of CIPR Materials

The CIPR mix can be placed in a windrow (Figure 13.23) or discharged
directly into the paving machine. The procedures discussed in Chapter 8, “Hot
Mix Asphalt Paving” should be followed. Conventional paving equipment is
used to place the RAP. A paver with a surge bin or hopper insert can be used
to reduce the fluctuations in the production of the mix. Unlike HMA paving,
the screed must be operated cold because a heated screed causes the asphalt
emulsion to break and the mix to stick to the screed. The mix should be
handled in such a manner to avoid segregation.

13.6.5 Compaction of CIPR Layers

The compaction of a CIPR mix should be done at a slow and uniform
speed. Any change in the direction of the rollers should be done carefully
to avoid displacement of the CIPR. Solvents should not be used on the
roller wheels or drums to prevent pickup of the CIPR. To prevent adhesion
of the CIPR to the rollers, water can be sprayed on the wheels/drums. A
small amount of liquid detergent or water-soluble release agent can be
added to the water to reduce pickup.

Initial compaction can be done with heavy vibratory, pneumatic rollers,
and steel-wheel rollers. It has been found that a CIPR mix cannot be com-
pacted to the same level as HMA. Typically, a CIPR mix will be compacted to
an in-place air-void range of 9-14 percent due to the moisture content of the

Initial compaction should be done with the roller traveling parallel to the
pavement’s center line. The rolling should begin at each edge and work toward
the center. Banked curves should be compacted with the roller starting at the
edge of the low side of the bank and then moving toward the superelevated
edge. To prevent tearing, gouging, or distortion of the pavement, a minimum
of one roller pass should be completed before any turning.

mix with either a large pneumatic roller or vibratory steel-wheel roller operat-

delay curing. Breakdown rolling is followed by intermediate rolling with a
12-ton (10,800 kilogram) or more double-drum vibratory roller in static mode
to remove the roller marks (see Figure 13.24).

13.6.6 Curing and Aeration

When using emulsions, the compacted CIPR mix must be cured before
a wearing course can be placed. Curing is needed to reduce the moisture
content in the mixture before it is sealed by the wearing/surface courses.
The time required for this process depends on daytime and nighttime
temperatures, the humidity levels and rainfall after completion of the recy-
cling process, and the amount of water added to the mix during the CIPR
process. If necessary, laydown and compaction can be delayed and the
mix aerated with a motorgrader to accelerate the curing process. If rain is
forecasted the CIPR mix can be bladed flat and sealed with a steel wheeled
roller until the weather clears up and aeration and curing can continue.

Different agencies define this moisture level on the basis of total moisture
in the mix or on the increase in the moisture content in the pavement
before recycling. Some agencies feel that if a dry core is obtained from
CIPR mix relatively intact, the mix has cured enough to place it on the
wearing surface. This curing/aeration process can take 10 to 14 days. In
advance, if this timeframe is a problem, hydrated lime or Portland cement
can be added during the mixing process to accelerate curing.

During the curing process, traffic is generally allowed to operate on
the CIPR surface. In fact, the kneading action of traffic can aid the aera-
tion process. If minor rutting occurs during the curing aeration period, the
surface of the CIPR mix can be lightly scarified, bladed and re-rolled using
a heavy pneumatic roller following by a static steel roller to remove any
roller marks. This rolling should be done on a hot day during the heat of
the day. Another problem that can occur is raveling of the surface aggregate
on the CIPR. If this occurs, a fog seal of diluted emulsion (50 percent with
water) can be applied at a rate of 0.05-0.10 gallon/yard? (0.23-0.45 litre/
meter’). The application of a fog seal should not be done unless absolutely
necessary and traffic can be kept off the surface until the fog seal has had
a chance to completely dry (at least 2 hours) because the fog seal can seal
the CIPR surface and impede the curing process. When foamed asphalt is
used for CIPR, no curing or aeration is required. The small amount of water
used in the foaming process flashes to steam when it comes in contact with
the hot liquid asphalt leaving no moisture behind.

13.6.7 Wearing Course

CIPR mixes are not intended to be a finished surface or wearing course
and will require a wearing course because of the high air-voids and to protect
the surface from raveling. For pavements with low traffic volumes, single-
and double-surface treatments have been used. For pavements with a higher
traffic volume, the thickness-design procedures detailed in Chapter 6,
“Thickness Design” should be used.

The weather and seasonal requirements in the specifications must

If aggregate and/or additive is being added to the mix by spreading in
front of the milling machine, periodic yield checks should be made.
The yield of the emulsion, aggregate and additive should be computed

The CIPR mix should be visually checked to ensure that it is
homogeneous, with no segregation; it should also be checked to
ensure that no large (sometimes agglomerated particles or uncrushed
pieces) are in the mix. If this occurs, the crushing and screening pro-
cess should be checked.

The coating of the mix should be checked. If the mix is not adequately
coated, the amount of mix water should be increased. Excessive water
can cause the asphalt to flush to the surface and increase the curing
time. Too little water may cause segregation. Reducing the asphalt
emulsion one grade (for example, using an HFE 300 instead of an
HFE 150) may also improve coating.

The CIPR mix should be checked for cohesion. If the mix lacks co-
hesion, the emulsion content should be increased. Too much asphalt
emulsion can result in an unstable mix; too little can cause the mix to
ravel. A simple field test can be used to evaluate cohesion. A ball of the
material is made by squeezing the material in the fist. If the ball falls
apart after the pressure is released, the mix lacks cohesion. The palm
of the hand should contain specks of asphalt, indicating proper asphalt
content. If the hand is stained, the asphalt content is too high.
Over-mixing can lead to scrubbing the emulsion off of the aggregate
particles and early emulsion break that causes the mix to be too stiff.
Under-mixing results in undercoating of the aggregate. 100 percent
coating, as in HMA, is not the goal with CIPR mixes.

The binder content can also be evaluated during placement of the
CIPR mix after initial compaction. The mat should be brown and
cohesive. Excessive raveling indicates too little asphalt in the mix.

13.7. Full-Depth Reclamation

Full-depth Reclamation (FDR) consists of pulverizing the full thickness
of the asphalt pavement and a portion of the underlying base, subbase,
and/or subgrade. The layers are blended to form a homogeneous, sta-
bilized base layer. This pulverized layer can be stabilized mechanically,
chemically, asphalt stabilized or a combination of the approaches. The
Basic Asphalt Recycling Manual prepared by the Asphalt Recycling and
Reclaiming Association is an excellent resource on the FDR process.

Mechanical stabilization, which may be accomplished by adding
crushed aggregate, RAP, or crushed PCC, increases the strength solely
through improving the aggregate interlock. Chemical stabilization uses
lime, cement, or lime/fly ash to form a binder, while asphalt stabilization
uses asphalt emulsions or foamed asphalt.

The type, severity, and frequency of pavement distress in the existing
pavement should be evaluated during the project’s design process. The
FDR process destroys the existing pavement to produce a homogenous
pulverized layer. If the existing pavement has large or frequent patching,
the variability of the final product will be affected. In areas where the
patches are the result of subgrade instability or in high water-table areas,
the cause of the problem should be fixed before beginning the FDR pro-
cess. This may require excavating the problem areas and replacing some
subgrade with improved materials, or improving the drainage system by
installing a subdrainage system or reworking the ditches in the area.

The pavement structure should be designed according to the proce-
dures detailed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design,” using the appropriate
design values for the FDR layers.

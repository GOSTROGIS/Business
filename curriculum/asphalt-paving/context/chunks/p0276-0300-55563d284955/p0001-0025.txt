10 Mix Selection

An HMA pavement surface must serve the demands of multiple
traffic loadings and environments. The HMA mixture chosen for the
project must meet the project requirements. The pavement designer
has more choices now than in the past for the type of mix selected
and for the materials (aggregates and asphalt binders) to be used on
the project. This has led to the development of specialized mixes to
address very specific problems. New design procedures such as the
Superpave design procedure and others discussed earlier in this chap-
ter have been implemented. In many cases, higher-quality materials
are being specified than are needed for the particular project. Because
of these concerns, the National Asphalt Pavement Association—in a
coordinated effort with the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA)
and an Expert Task Group consisting of representatives from the pav-
ing industry and Departments of Transportation—developed National
Asphalt Pavement Association Information Series 128, HMA Pavement
Mix Type Selection Guide. This document provides detailed information
on mix selection. As described earlier in this chapter, there are three
primary types of mixes used for the construction of HMA pavements:
fine and coarse graded dense mixes, stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes,
and Open-Graded Friction Courses (OGFC) mixes.

4.10.1 Pavement Layers and Traffic Levels
(definitions)

The following provides a short description of each of these layers in a
pavement structure. Generally, materials of lesser quality are needed for
each layer as they progress down in the pavement structure.

* Surface layer—contains the highest-quality materials. It pro-
vides friction, smoothness, noise control, rutting resistance and
durability.

° Intermediate layer or binder layer—consists of one or more lifts of
HMA below the surface layer. It provides structural strength to the
pavement.

° Base layer—consists of one or more lifts of HMA placed at the
bottom of the pavement structure.

The mix selected for use in an HMA pavement will vary depending
on the traffic level of the pavement. Table 4.12 provides some defini-
tions and the typical roadway application represented by that traffic
level.

4.10.2 Dense Graded Mixes

A dense graded mix is a mixture where the aggregate particles have an
even distribution from coarse to fine. The goal is that the mix chosen will
be stable and durable. To achieve these, it is generally necessary to intro-
duce enough asphalt into the mix to insure durability but not so much as
to reduce the stability of the mix below that needed for the traffic levels
on the pavement. To insure that the chosen mix performs properly, it also
must be built as it is designed and proper quality control procedures must
be implemented. A special concern is the need for obtaining proper com-
paction on the roadway.

Dense graded mixes are defined by their nominal maximum aggre-
gate size (defined earlier in this chapter and in Chapter 3, “Mineral
Aggregates and Mixture Additives”) and can be further defined as either
fine-graded or coarse-graded. A fine-graded mix will have a higher pro-
portion of fine sand-sized particles than a coarse-graded mix. Table
4.13 presents the criteria for fine/coarse graded HMA mixes. Coarse
mixes are defined as having a combined aggregate gradation that
passes below the primary control sieve control point, and fine mixes
are defined as having a gradation that passes above or through the pri-
mary control sieve control point.

In general, the fine gradation mixes with the smaller nominal maximum
aggregate sizes will be used for the surface mixes (top 1% to 2 inches).

These mixes will have higher asphalt contents and lower permeability (due
to greater aggregate surface area and smaller size voids), which will result
in improved durability (ess block and shrinkage cracking). For applica-
tions where the surface will receive little or no vehicle traffic, such as bike
trails and walking paths, consideration should be given to designing the
mix with 22 percent air-voids rather than 4 percent air-voids (standard
requirement) to increase the durability of the mix further and reduce non-
load associated cracking.

The lower levels (binder and base courses) should use the larger aggre-
gate sizes. These mixes provide increased resistance to pavement deforma-
tion. This allows the placement of thicker lifts and requires less asphalt
cement due to less surface area.

The lift thickness for all mixes (surface, binder and base) is dictated by the
nominal maximum aggregate size. If the nominal maximum aggregate size
is too large with relation to the lift thickness it will be very difficult to obtain
proper compaction of the mat. It is recommended that the lift thickness-be
three times the nominal maximum aggregate size for fine graded mixes
and four times the nominal maximum aggregate size for the coarse graded
mixes. These same criteria will apply for SMA mixes. For OGFC mixes the
thickness should be two times the nominal maximum aggregate size.

5.1.4 Introduction

The performance of an HMA pavement is directly related to understanding
the soils upon which it is built. The strength of both the untreated base course
and the subgrade soil are directly related to the moisture content; therefore,
the drainage of the pavement is extremely important.

Likewise, drainage is also an important aspect in asphalt pavement
design and construction. The latter part of this chapter reviews some key
issues of pavement-related drainage.

If not thoroughly knowledgeable about soil mechanics and the effect
of different soils and their drainage, the reader is encouraged to consult a
qualified geotechnical engineer for further guidance.

5.1.2 Objectives for Classifying Subgrade

The objective behind using any soil classification system for road and
airport construction is the ability to predict performance of a given soil
subgrade on the basis of a few simple tests. These test results, along with
the soil classification procedure and criteria, allow an engineer to identify
the soil type and characterize it according to other soils with similar char-
acteristics and properties.

Grouping soils into a classification system is not the final objective, but
it should be regarded as a tool to understand soil action and behavior as
they relate to subgrade performance.

5.1.3 Classification Systems

The two classification systems commonly used to describe soils used
for pavement construction are the American Association of State High-
way and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) Classification System for Soils
and the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS). Both systems are
described in detail in many soil mechanics textbooks. The reader is
referred to An Introduction to Geotechnical Engineering, by R. D. Holtz
and W. D. Kovacs.

The principal soil classification system used in the United States by
highway engineers is the AASHTO system. This system was originally
developed by the U.S. Bureau of Public Roads in about 1928. The present
AASHTO system, which dates from 1945, was the result of an extensive
revision by the Highway Research Board.

Under the AASHTO system, soils having approximately the same general
load-carrying capacity and service characteristics are grouped together to
form one of seven basic groups (A-1, A-2, A-3, A-4, A-5, A-6, and A-7). In
general, the best highway subgrade soils are classified as A-1. Poorer soils
are rated in numerical order, with the A-7 group being the poorest. The
exception is the A-3 group. Soils classified A-3 are better subgrades than
soils in the A-2 group. From this, it can generally be assumed for a given
set of conditions that the thickness of the pavement section must progres-
sively increase as the soil classification group increases from A-1 to A-7,
except as noted.

The USCS is based on the Airfield Classification System developed by
Professor Arthur Casagrande of Harvard University during World War I.
In 1952, Casagrande, together with the Bureau of Reclamation and the
U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, modified the system for a broad range of
applications, including dams and foundations.

The USCS groups soils based on grain size and plasticity. The system is
rooted in the concept that coarse-grained soils can be classified accord-
ing to grain-size distribution, while it is more appropriate to classify fine-
grained soils based on plasticity characteristics. These characteristics are
evaluated by plotting the plasticity index (PD versus the liquid limit (LL)
on a standard plasticity chart. The position of the plotted points yields
information for predicting soil behavior as an engineering construction
material. The following properties form the basis of soil identification:

° Percentages of gravel, sand, and fines passing the No. 200 (75 microns)

¢ Shape of the grain-size distribution curve (well-graded or poorly-

graded

® Plasticity and compressibility characteristics

A soil is given a descriptive name and letter symbol to indicate its
principal characteristics.

5.2 Methods for Evaluating

Subgrade Strength
and Stiffness

5.2.1 Introduction

Thickness design of asphalt pavements depends to a large extent on
the strength and/or stiffness of the finished subgrade. Common empirical
methods of thickness design depend on a subgrade strength index such
as the CBR or R-value. Mechanistic-empirical methods use the subgrade
resilient modulus, a measure of material stiffness, to establish pavement
thickness. In either method, characterization of the subgrade strength or
stiffness is a fundamental part of thickness design (for further information,
see Chapter 6, “Thickness Design”).

The concept of testing soils at their anticipated moisture and density
should be the governing criterion for thickness design.

Subgrade strength or stiffness of a soil or aggregate base or subbase
depends on soil type and is closely related to density and moisture con-
tent. Variable strength or stiffness can be expected, depending on the
degree of compaction and moisture content after the pavement has been
completed. If experience has indicated that a certain soil cannot be com-
pacted economically to high density, it would be unwise to perform design
tests with the soil at a high-density value.

Design strength or stiffness should be based on values obtained from
soil samples in their worst anticipated condition. To obtain such values
generally involves testing a sample that has been soaked in water.

Design methods used by different agencies vary widely, and their
approach to evaluating the strength or modulus of the unbound materials
for design purposes also varies. Evaluation methods discussed here are
probahly the most widely used methods for asphalt pavement design for
airfields and highways.

Both field and laboratory test methods have been established by vari-
ous agencies over the years. Each has advantages and disadvantages that
must be considered. Field-test methods have the advantage of testing the
unbound layers (base and subgrade) in situ at the confining pressure and
moisture content that exist at the site. This may also be a disadvantage
in that the existing conditions may not represent the intended conditions
after construction. For example, if a pavement is to be built over a satu-
rated soil, a field test would provide a strength index for the soil in a
saturated condition; however, drainage improvements to the site will likely
change the subgrade moisture content after construction.

Laboratory tests may be advantageous because the unbound layers are
tested under a wide range of confining pressure and moisture conditions,

5.2.2 Laboratory Test Methods

The CBR, with its numerous variations, is probably the most widely used
strength index for designing asphalt pavement structures. This method was first
developed by the California Division of Highways in the 1920s. It was modified
by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for use on airfields during World War Il
and is still widely used in airfield pavement design and for highway and road-
way design outside the United States. While primarily a laboratory test, field
CBR test methods have been developed for use in evaluating soils in situ.

The Resistance Value (R-Value) test method for evaluating treated and
untreated materials for bases, subbases, and subgrades was developed by
F. N. Hveem and R. M. Carmany of the California Division of Highways.

The Resilient Modulus (M) method was developed by the Asphalt Insti-
tute based on a test originated at the University of California by H. B. Seed,
C. K. Chan, and C. E. Lee. This laboratory test is used to determine the stiff-
ness of a soil specimen for conditions that represent stress in pavements
subjected to moving wheel loads.

The CBR is a comparative measure of the shearing resistance of soil. The
test consists of measuring the load required to cause a piston of standard
size to penetrate a soil specimen at a specified rate. This load is divided
by the load required to force the piston to the same depth in a standard
sample of crushed stone. The result, multiplied by 100, is the value of the
California CBR. Usually depths of 0.1 or 0.2 inch (2.5 or 5 millimeters) are
used, but depths of 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 inch (7.5, 10, and 12.5 millimeters)
may be used if desired. Penetration loads for the crushed stone have been
standardized. This test method is intended to provide the relative bearing
value, or CBR, of base, subbase, and subgrade materials. Procedures are
given for laboratory-compacted swelling, nonswelling, and granular mate-
rials. These tests are usually performed to obtain information that will be
used for design purposes.

The CBR value for a soil will depend upon its density, molding moisture
content, and moisture content after soaking. Because the product of labo-
ratory compaction should closely represent the results of field compac-
tion, the first two of these variables must be carefully controlled during the
preparation of laboratory samples for testing. Unless it can be ascertained
that the soil being tested will not accumulate moisture and be affected by
it in the field after construction, the CBR tests should be performed on
soaked samples.

Certain routine soil tests should be performed before conducting the
CBR test. These tests are shown in Table 5.2.

Resistance Value (R-Value)

General

The R-value method of design is based on two separate measurements:
the R-value (or resistance value) and the expansion pressure test.

The cover thickness or structural section required to prevent plastic
deformation of soil under imposed wheel loads is determined by the
R-value. An expansion pressure test is used to determine the thickness or

Test Procedure

The R-value test (ASTM D 2844) is made on a relatively small sample,
and it is necessary to remove all large material. If 75 percent or more of the
material passes the 34-inch (19.0 millimeters) sieve, the material retained
on that sieve is removed. If less than 75 percent of the material passes the
34-inch (19.0 millimeters) sieve, then all material retained on the 1-inch (25.0
millimeters) sieve is removed. The soil is then separated over the No. 4 (4.75
millimeters) sieve but recombined in the proper proportions with the coarse
sizes to obtain 1200-gram (2.65 pounds) samples plus an extra 200 grams
(0.44 pounds) for moisture content determinations.

In each R-value test, four test briquettes at different moisture contents are
prepared. Each briquette has one-half to two-thirds the amount of water nec-
essary to saturate the sample or exude moisture between 100 and 800 pounds
per square inch (689 and 5516 kiloPascals). After mixing, loose material is
allowed to cure overnight in a covered container. Then more water is added
and mixed until the estimated saturation moisture is attained. The first bri-
quette is used as a pilot specimen to obtain specimen heights of 2.5£0.1
inches (63.52.5 millimeters) and to have exudation pressures both above
and below 300 pounds per square inch (2068 kiloPascals).

Specimens are compacted by a kneading compactor, which is the same
as that used for the Hveem method of asphalt mix design. The compaction
procedure provides a kneading action intended to give soil particles the
same orientation and contact pressure obtained during field compaction.

The prepared soil sample is placed in the compactor feeding trough with
the material distributed evenly along the full length. A 4-inch (101.6 milli-
meters) diameter by 5-inch (127 millimeters) tall mold is fitted into a special
holder for placing in the compactor. Material is deposited in the mold by a
special procedure while the kneading compactor is operating and the foot
pressure is set at 250 pounds per square inch (1724 kiloPascals).

When all material has been placed in the mold and 10 extra blows have
been added to level and seat the material, the sample is compacted by
using 100 blows with a foot pressure of 350 pounds per square inch (2413
kiloPascals).

Resilient Modulus (M_)

General Theory

The resilient modulus (M) is used to characterize subgrade soils and
unbound base/subbase materials. The resilient modulus test provides a
material stiffness that more closely simulates the behavior of the material
under a moving wheel. It can be used in the mechanistic analysis of mul-
tilayered systems for predicting roughness, cracking, rutting, etc. This test
covers the determination of resilient modulus of untreated fine-grained
soils for conditions that represent stress in pavements subjected to mov-
ing wheel loads. The results of the test describe the resilient character of
a soil specimen when a repeated axial deviator stress of fixed magnitude,
frequency, and load duration is applied while the specimen, in a triaxial
chamber, is surrounded by pressure that acts as a static, all-around stress.

Test Procedure

Resilient modulus testing of unbound materials is performed in accor-
dance with one of two current procedures: (AASHTO T 307), Determining
the Resilient Modulus of Soils and Aggregate Materials or (AASHTO T 292),
Resilient Modulus of Subgrade Soils and Untreated Base/Subbase Materials.
A triaxial cell suitable for use in resilience testing of soils is shown in
Figure 5.5. The equipment is similar to most standard cells, except that
it is somewhat larger to facilitate the internally mounted load and defor-
mation measuring equipment, and has additional outlets for the electrical
leads from the measuring devices. (Triaxial cells with suitable externally
mounted load and deformation measuring equipment also may be used.)
For the type of equipment shown, air is used as the cell fluid.

The deformation measuring equipment consists of Linear Variable
Differential Transformers (LVDTs) attached to the soil specimen by a pair
of clamps. Two LVDTs are used for the measurement of axial deformation.
The clamps and LVDTs are shown in position on a soil specimen in
Figure 5.5. Preferably, load is measured by placing a load cell between
the sample cap and the loading piston. In addition to measuring devices,
suitable signal excitation, conditioning, and recording equipment must
also be maintained.

The external loading source may be any device capable of providing
varying repeated loads in fixed cycles of load and release. These devices
range from simple cam and switch control of static weights or air pistons
to closed loop electro-hydraulic systems. A load duration of 0.1 second and
load-release duration of 0.4 to 3 seconds has been found to be satisfactory
for most applications.

The confining pressure and deviator stress used should approximate the
expected in-place state of stresses. A confining pressure of 2 pounds per
square inch (14 kiloNewtons) and deviator stress of 6 pounds per square
inch (41 kiloNewtons) are reasonable values for most testing.

5.2.3 Field Test Procedures

Dynamic Cone Penetrometer

Field Test Methods

The Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP) is a field-testing device to
quickly estimate the in situ strength and deformation characteristics of
pavement subgrades. The test can be performed on native soils or on com-
pacted materials. The DCP Index that results from completing the test can
be readily correlated to a CBR for use in the empirical CBR design method.
Developed in South Africa in 1975, the DCP has since found its application
in many parts of the world because of its portability, speed, and ease of
use in flexible pavement design.

For field evaluation of subgrade stiffness, the Falling Weight Deflectometer
(FWD) is commonly used by highway agencies and airports throughout the
United States. The test is conducted by raising and dropping a weighted
package on the pavement surface and measuring vertical deflections on
the pavement surface. Using principles of mechanics, the stiffness of the
HMA layers, aggregate base, and subgrade layers can be back-calculated
at the testing location. Due to the speed and nondestructive nature of
the test, numerous tests can be conducted on a pavement feature in a
relatively short amount of time.

General

The DCP is a testing device used to estimate the in situ strength and
deformation characteristics of native soils or fills placed as part of a pave-
ment subgrade; it generally is not useful to evaluate the properties of
bases and subbases. If the test is performed on existing pavements, the
asphalt concrete surface layer must be removed, typically by coring an
appropriate-size hole in the asphalt concrete. It relies on correlations with
various soil types and the relative compaction or density of soils. Though
commonly used to estimate the structural capacity of soils, this test is now
being considered as a quality-control tool for pavement subgrades during
construction, in lieu of more common in situ density testing.

The test involves driving a cone-shaped probe (see Figure 5.7) into a
soil using a dynamic load and measuring the advancement of the probe
for each applied load. The strength of the soil being penetrated has a
direct impact on the penetration observed. Thus, the strength of the soil is
related to the depth of penetration achieved with each blow used to drive
the DCP. As previously discussed, the strength of the soil depends on den-
sity, moisture content, and soil type or classification.

The data is presented as a plot of the number of blows versus the depth
of penetration. The penetration rate has been correlated to engineering
properties of soils or other derived measurements of strength and modu-

wide variety of cohesive and granular materials. The penetration rate can be
correlated to resilient modulus through correlations between CBR and resil-

caution. Several studies have also correlated penetration rate, or index, to
the elastic modulus of soils.

Falling Weight Deflectometer

General

The FWD, as shown on Figure 5.9, is an impulse load-deflection device
that delivers a transient impulse force to the pavement. A weight is lifted
to a given height on a guide system and then dropped onto a buffering
plate on the pavement. A transient impulse force, which can be changed by
varying the magnitude of the falling weight or by varying the drop height,
is generated in the pavement by the impact of the falling weight. A series of
sensors located at various distances from the load plate are used to measure
pavement deflections. Commercial impulse load-deflection devices include
the Dynatest, KUAB, JILS, and Phoenix.

A major advantage of the impulse loading devices is their ability to more
accurately model a moving wheel load in both magnitude and duration,
thus producing a deflection that simulates the pavement deflection caused
by a moving vehicle. The advantages of the FWD are the following:

¢ The ability to better simulate a moving wheel load

© The ability to measure deflections at varying loads over time

® The ability to record a deflection basin

° The speed, repeatability, and robustness of the equipment

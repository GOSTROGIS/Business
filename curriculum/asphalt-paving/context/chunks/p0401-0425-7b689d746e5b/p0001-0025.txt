Horizontal-discharge hauling units have a conveyor system that moves
the material from the hauling unit into the paver hopper without the need

Avoiding Heat Loss

The temperature of the HMA must be maintained within specified limits
during production, storage, transportation, and laydown. Loaded hauling
units must deliver the HMA with minimal heat loss. If delivery time to the
paving site is such that the material cools below acceptable placement and
compaction temperatures—or if adverse conditions are such that the mate-
rial will cool in the hauling unit—steps must be taken to retain the heat.
Tarps and insulated truck beds are commonly used to reduce heat loss.
The temperature of the HMA in the hauling unit should be monitored. If
the temperature fluctuates beyond the limits allowed in the specifications,
steps must be taken to reduce or eliminate the excessive temperature
variations.

Condition of Haul Trucks

Numerous styles of haul trucks exist today, each with unique mainte-
nance procedures. Some basic maintenance procedures, however, apply
to all styles. The dumping lip should be cleaned with a scraper between
loads, and tailgate-latching devices should be lubricated properly so they
release on command. An approved release agent must be applied to the
inside of the hauling unit before the next load is dumped into the haul-
ing unit. The floor of the hauling unit should be flat and free of dimples
that might cause the release agent to puddle. The truck should be in good
condition and capable of safely and legally transporting material to the job
site. The lifting device should be maintained so that the bed can be lifted
to continuously move the material from the truck bed to the paver hopper.

8.3.2 Delivery of HMA to the Paver

When the loaded hauling unit has arrived at the paving site, the HMA
must be moved into the paver without disturbing the HMA gradation or
without losing temperature. The driver, after arriving in the staging area,
should “break the load” by raising the truck bed slightly until the HMA
moves against the tailgate. This procedure allows the HMA to exit the
truck bed in a mass rather than in a dribble, avoiding segregation as the
HMA flows into the hopper (see Figure 8.6).

The paver operator or dump operator, should position the loaded truck
12-18 inches (300-450 millimeters) short of the paver, with the dump bed
partially raised. This positioning keeps the truck from knocking the paver
backward and creating an indentation in the mat. As the paver moves
forward and makes contact with the hauling unit, the driver should be
instructed to release the tailgate and raise the dump bed as quickly as
possible. When the tailgate opens, the material will move in mass into the
hopper, thereby reducing the chances of segregating the material. When a
wheel-locking device is not being used, the experienced truck driver will
anticipate this sudden rush of material and briefly apply additional brake
pressure to prevent the hauling unit from being forced out of the hopper
and spilling material on the pavement directly ahead of the paver. While
the dump bed is emptying, the driver should maintain just enough pres-
sure on the brakes to keep the truck from rolling away from the paver.
The flow of HMA is coordinated between the paver and truck driver. The
bed should be raised to the fully extended position as quickly as possible
without overflowing the paver hopper. Once the bed is empty, the paver
signals for the truck bed to be lowered before pulling out of the paver hop-
per. The hauling unit should be driven to a predetermined cleanout area,
where remaining HMA can be removed from the truck bed and around the
tailgate area and apron. Do not dump HMA in front of the paver or allow
beds and aprons to be cleaned in front of the paver. See Figure 8.7 for the
steps associated with properly loading the paver.

Windrow Paving

Windrow paving places the HMA in a longitudinal windrow directly on
the grade in front of the paver as it arrives on the project, and as with the
MTD, this surge aids in achieving a nonstop paving operation. An elevat-
ing windrow device or pickup machine (see Figure 8.9) in front of the
paver picks up the material and places it in the hopper. The quantity of
HMA on the grade is controlled by a dump operator as the hauling unit is
unloaded and the size of the windrow is adjusted according to the level
of material in the hopper. A well trained crew can successfully pave for
hours, producing a high quality HMA pavement without stopping.

Windrow Paving—Remixing Ability

The windrow-building process can allow the mixture to segregate with
the larger aggregate accumulating along the bottom outside edges of
the windrow. Therefore, steps must be taken to ensure that the windrow
pickup device picks up the entire windrow cleanly and that the segregated

Advantages and Disadvantages of Windrow Paving

The advantage to windrow paving is the continuous aspect of the
operation, allowing the paver to lay a consistent high quality HMA pave-
ment. The problems can be heat loss into the grade, the inability to size
the windrow, the inability to cleanly pick up the material off the grade,
and finally vibrations caused by the powered pickup machine that affect
the paver’s performance. All these issues can be overcome with proper
attention to detail. A shorter windrow will reduce heat loss; proper lay-
down. crew training about windrow sizing will solve quantity problems;
and a properly adjusted pickup machine, isolated from the paver, will pick
up the material cleanly without adversely affecting the paver. Windrow
paving operations are usually confined to warm dry climates, where the
chance of a surprise rainstorm cooling the material in the exposed windrow
is minimal.

8.3.5 Visual Inspection of the HMA

Inspection of the HMA as it flows from the hauling units into the MTD,
into the windrow in front of the paver, or directly into the paver hopper
will help determine the consistency (temperature, gradation, aggregate
coating, asphalt content) of the HMA. Observing how the HMA is laying
in the hauling unit reveals load inconsistencies. Watching the material as
it breaks out of the hauling unit is also important—one load may break
uniformly as the bed is raised while the next load might break in larger
chunks or move all at once into the hopper, windrow, or MTD. Uncoated
aggregate is easily identified and shows that the material has not been
properly mixed during production. A visible fume as the HMA is moving
out of the hauling unit is a normal condition. But if the level of fumes is
inconsistent from one load to the next, the HMA temperature might be
fluctuating and should be checked for compliance. The temperature of
the HMA should be kept to +25°F (£15°C). Finally, segregation should be
noted, especially at the beginning and at the end of each load. If end-of-
load segregation is apparent, the loadout techniques at the HMA plant
should be adjusted to reduce or eliminate end-of-load segregation.

parts and adjustments, but all have similar operating principles. The paver
receives the HMA directly from the hauling unit, MTD, or windrow pickup
machine and moves the HMA through the paver to place and spread the
HMA on the roadway before beginning the compaction process (see Figure

8.4.1 Asphalt Paving Machine—Basics

An HMA paver is a self contained, self-propelled pavement finishing
machine made up of three basic units: the tractor, a material handling sys-
tem, and the screed. The tractor portion of the asphalt paver contains the
power unit that provides the power needs of all three components, plus the
drive train that propels the machine. The material handling system receives
the HMA material and moves it from the hopper to the spreading augers in
front of the screed. The screed levels and profiles the material being laid,
provides some initial density, and seals the surface ready to be compacted to
the final specified density as described in Chapter 9, “HMA Compaction.”

identification of Components of the Paver

The following is a description of each component of the asphalt paver:

° Hopper. The hopper is the area where the HMA is deposited by the
hauling unit, pickup machines, or material transfer device.

° Hopper insert. The hopper insert is a metal bin inserted inside
the paver hopper to receive material from the MTD or a windrow
pickup machine to expand the hopper storage capacity by 50 to
70 percent (see Figure 8.12).

° Push rollers. These rollers mounted on the front of the paver push
on the hauling unit tires as the HMA is being transferred from the
hauling unit to the paver hopper.

8.4.2 Types of Tractors

Only two types of drive mechanisms power today’s asphalt pavers—either
a track or a rubber-tire propulsion system (see Figures 8.12 and 8.13).

The selection of either a rubber-tire or a track-mounted paver is up to
the individual user. Either unit will provide the uniform forward speed
required to lay smooth asphalt pavements. A track system provides a lower
ground pressure with greater traction, while a rubber-tire paver has greater
mobility and a lower operating cost. The mobility of track machines has
been improved by the introduction of a continuous rubber-track system.
On rubber-tire pavers, both drive tires should be inspected each morning
for cuts, punctures, and other damage that might result in a flat tire dur-
ing the work shift. To achieve maximum traction from drive tires, most are
filled with calcium chloride or some other weight-producing material. The
air pressure must be closely monitored and checked with special pressure
gauges and should never vary by more than 2 pounds per square inch
(13.8 kilopascals) between the two drive tires. Caution must be exercised
when checking the air pressure in liquid-filled tires to avoid the liquid’s
spraying out and coming in contact with the skin and eyes. Track systems
on track pavers should also be checked daily to ensure that they properly
align, have correct tension, and have no gouges in the track pads.

Material Handling System

The material handling system consists of the mechanisms used to move
the material from the hopper to the augers in front of the screed. Slat con-
veyers are used on most pavers to move the material through the paver.
One paver manufacturer uses four counterrotating augers in place of slat
conveyers to remix the HMA to minimize segregation as the material moves
from the hopper to the augers. The volume of HMA required to match the
width, depth, and speed of placement is precisely controlled either by
flow gates mounted at the rear of the hopper or by varying the speed of
the slat conveyer system. When flow gates are not used, the drives of both
the auger and the conveyer are separated and controlled independently to
transfer the correct volume of HMA. Split systems use an additional mate-
rial sensor to control the volume of material carried by the conveyer into
the auger area. As the HMA flows into the area in front of the screed, the
augers move it transversely to the outer ends of the screed. The material
sensors at the outer ends of the screed control the amount of HMA in this
area by speeding up, slowing down, or stopping the rotation of the augers.
The auger systems on most pavers can be raised or lowered to provide
clearance for loading and unloading the paver from equipment hauling
trailers. This vertical adjustment can also be used when paving deeper
lifts of HMA. The augers should always be 1% or 2 inches @8 to 50 mil-
limeters) above the HMA pavement thickness being placed. When paving
is done with a screed wider than the basic screed, auger extensions and
material confining plates known as tunnel extensions should be added to
uniformly carry the HMA the full width of the screed. Auger tunnel exten-
sions (slide-out or bolt-on) are located in front of the augers when running
at extended widths (14 feet [4 meters] or more). Ideally, the auger tunnel
extensions should be 12-18 inches (0.3-0.5 meters) from the edger plate
of the screed to minimize the chance that the material will segregate.

The volume of material in front of the screed is automatically controlled
by material-control sensors that signal the material-feed system to deliver
the proper amount of HMA to the front of the screed. These sensors should
be properly positioned to make sure that the volume is not allowed to
fluctuate beyond operational limits in achieving a quality mat.

Utilization of the Material-Control Sensors

The material-control sensors precisely control the volume of material
(head of material) in front of the screed. The augers on the right and on the
left are independent of each other and are controlled by separate material-
control sensors. The sensors control the movement of material by starting
and stopping and running at variable speeds in order to furnish the correct
volume of material to match the paver’s width, depth, and speed.

To operate properly, the sensors must index from the live head of material
that is moving constantly to fill in an empty area in front of the screed. Paver
manufacturers use different types of sensors and mount them in different
positions to monitor and control the flow of material. Two types of mate-
rial sensors are used today: contact sensors and ultrasonic sensors. Contact

Screed Components and Adjustments

Crown

The main screed has the ability to be crowned at the center of the
screed. With this crown, the screed can be set up to give either a positive
or a negative crown to the profile of the HMA pavement being laid. Posi-
tive crown is used when the centerline of the screed is intended to be the
centerline of the road and when fall or drainage is required in both direc-
tions. Negative crown is used if the center of the HMA mat being laid is
used for drainage.

Vibratory and Tamper Bar Systems

Vibrators on the asphalt screed provide some initial density. The com-
pactive force, which is generated by offset weights as they are spun at
high rates around a central shaft, is applied to the HMA as it passes under
the screed. Most vibrators on today’s pavers are adjustable. The vibrators
should not be used when paving a thin lift because fracturing of the aggre-
gates might result. A nuclear density gauge can be used to check behind
the screed at various vibrator settings to evaluate the density. If density
does not increase, the vibrators should be turned off. This trial-and-error
process helps establish the correct setting for each combination of HMA
mixture and lift thickness and should be done on each new mixture that
is required to meet a density specification.

Some screeds have tamper bars at the leading edge of the screed that
serve a dual purpose. They strike off the HMA as it passes under the
screed and give a greater initial density than a vibratory screed. The only
shortcoming to the tamper-bar system is that higher rates of paving speed
do not give the tamper bars enough time to tuck the material under the
screed. This can result in a torn HMA pavement surface or one with a
dragging texture. The tamper bar should protrude below the leading edge
of the screed just enough to catch a fingernail on it, or %—42 inch (0.4 to
0.8 millimeter). Dual tamper-bar screeds have one tamper bar in back of
the other at the leading edge of the screed. This second tamper bar gives

Screed Heating Systems

At the start of a paving shift, the screed is much colder than the HMA being
laid. To keep the asphalt material from sticking to the colder metal of the
screed, it must be preheated. Two types of heating systems are used to pre-
heat the screed before each day’s paving: diesel and electrical. Diesel burners
are simple and easy to use, but they require proper operational procedures so
that excess fumes are not produced when air and fuel in the burner chamber
are out of balance. Electrical heating systems have heating elements immedi-
ately above the screed plate that are surrounded by special heat transfer oil
that helps heat the screed uniformly without hotspots. This system minimizes
fumes and provides a more even heat across the entire screed plate. Electrical
heating systems operate in a couple of different modes. In one, the screed
is heated to operational temperature and the heating system shuts off. The
other heats the screed to the desired temperature and cycles on and off to
maintain this temperature throughout the paving operation.

Free-Floating Screed

Free-floating screeds (see Figure 8.16) have been a standard feature of
HMA pavers since they were first introduced in the 1930s. The basic prin-
ciple of the free-floating screed is that, when it is pulled into the material
deposited in front of it by the augers, it will automatically ride up or down
seeking the level where the path of its flat screed plate is parallel to the
direction of pull. At this point, the forces (listed below), which act upon
the screed, will be in balance and the screed will continue to lay a uniform
mat thickness until one or more of the forces change.

When a free-floating screed is used, five factors must be considered:
° The angle of attack

° The head of material

¢ The forward motion (paving speed)

° The screed weight

° HMA stability (temperature and gradation)

Angle of Attack

The angle of attack is the angle of the screed in relation to the paving
grade. The screed pivots about the screed pivot point. The angle can be
adjusted by the depth-screw assembly or by raising or lowering the screed
tow point. The depth-screw is adjusted manually by the screed operator.
Tow-point changes are generated by automatic grade and slope control.
The angle of attack creates an extrusion angle under the screed; once the
extrusion angle can support its weight, the screed is free to float at that
average depth. Pavers have a natural leveling effect, placing asphalt thin-
ner over the highs and filling in the lows of the base. Increasing the angle
of attack allows more material to flow under the screed, causing the screed
to rise until it reaches the elevation that finds equilibrium of the forces that
act upon the screed. The paver will continue to place HMA at the newly
established depth until one or more of the forces acting on the screed
is changed. Reducing the angle of attack results in less material passing
under the screed, and the screed will drop until the new depth is reached
and a new equilibrium of forces is established.

Head of Material

The head of material is the depth of material being evenly spread in
front of the screed by the auger. Controlling the head of material is the
most important factor in laying a smooth HMA pavement. The head of
material is affected by the volume of the HMA in the auger chamber. Once
the head of material has been established, it must be maintained within a
tolerance of +1 inch at all times. Starving the screed of material will result
in the screed settling and will cause a dip in the pavement. Immediately
placing a large amount of HMA in front of the screed will guarantee a
bump as the screed attempts to crawl over the excess material deposited
in its path. Ideally, the head of material should be maintained at a height
that exposes one half of the auger and not vary by more than +1 inch
(25 millimeters).

Paving Speed

Nonstop paving at a uniform forward speed results in the highest-
quality and smoothest pavements and should be the goal of every paving
crew. Changing the paving speed will change the angle of attack on the
screed, causing a corresponding change in the mat thickness. Manual adjust-
ments to offset this depth change must be made with the depth screws.
When equipped with automatic controls, these devices immediately sense
the change in depth caused by speed changes and make the appropriate
adjustments to maintain the required depth. Normal operations require that

Screed Weight

The screed weight is usually translated into pounds per square inch

HMA Stiffness (Loose)

A change in the temperature of the HMA or the gradation of the aggre-
gates can result in a change in the stability or stiffness of the loose HMA
during paving. This change in stiffness will cause the screed to either rise
or fall as it reacts to a change in vertical force and seeks to rebalance all
of the forces acting upon it.

Screed Extensions

Screed extensions are rigidly mounted, fixed-width, or hydraulically
powered variable-width attachments that extend beyond the width of
the main screed to facilitate paving widths that are wider than the basic
machine width. Both types of extensions provide full screed plates with
heat and compaction similar to the cross section of the main screed. This
allows the HMA placed by the extension to have the same characteristics
in terms of texture, smoothness, and precompaction as the material placed
by the main screed. Both types of extensions can be angled differently
than the main screed, allowing adjacent lanes and shoulders to be paved
at a different cross-slope than the main screed.

Variable-Width Screeds

With a variable-width screed, the paving width can be changed with the
push of a button while still maintaining a uniform surface across the entire
HMA mat being placed (see Figure 8.17). Extensions on the variable-width
screeds can be either front- or rear-mounted and will require different
adjustments to achieve a quality HMA pavement. The extensions can usu-
ally be sloped positively or negatively at the outer edges of the main screed
so the adjacent lane(s) can be paved at the same time as the mainline.

Fixed-width screeds with rigidly fixed extensions are used for extended-

both sides of the screed to the desired paving width. The structural stabil-

and maintain better control of the desired profile. Screeds with rigidly
fixed extensions cannot change paving width unless the paving operation
is halted long enough to add or detach the extension(s) needed to change
the required paving width. Rigidly fixed extended screeds are best suited
to long stretches of fixed paving widths.

Front- and rear-extendable screeds are widely used. These screeds have

8.4.4 Automatic Screed Controls (Types
and Operational Differences)

Automatic Grade Controls

Automatic grade controls take a continuous reading from a known lon-
gitudinal reference and transfer it to the paver’s control system, which
continually changes the depth of the HMA pavement being placed. The
controls are either mechanical or electronic.

The mechanical device uses a spring-loaded rotational potentiometer
attached to a wand as a sensor to measure changes in the vertical distance
between the reference line and the sensor mounted on the side arm of
the paver. In response to these changes, the sensors send a signal propor-
tional to the amount and direction of change to the paver’s grade controls.
The grade control then actuates the hydraulic cylinder, which makes a
corresponding change in the height of the tow point. On a free-floating
screed, as the tow point rises or falls, the angle of attack changes and the
depth increases or decreases accordingly. The screed changes depth until
the distance between the reference and the sensor is reestablished.

Electronic control equipment can also be used to automate the screed.

Automatic Slope Control

An HMA paver equipped with an automatic slope control can hold the
screed at a different cross-slope from the slope of the surface that the
paver is riding on. The slope control uses a pendulum or bubble level
device to establish a perfectly vertical reference, from which a perpendicu-
lar plane will establish a true horizontal line. Using this true horizontal
line, a cross-slope can be created and maintained across the screed width
to build pavements with a cross-slope, such as superelevated pavements
on curved sections of roadway. Because the slope can be adjusted while
the paver is moving, the cross-slope can be “dialed” in or out and held con-
stant as the paver enters and exits a transition from flat to superelevated
pavements. When automatic slope control is used in conjunction with an
automatic grade control setup, the side of the screed closest to the refer-
ence line will be the pivot side and the cross-slope will be set positively or
negatively from this side as required by the project specifications.

References Available for Grade and Slope Control

Longitudinal reference lines for the automatic grade and slope-control sen-
sors to follow allow a pavement to be built to a specific vertical and hori-
zontal alignment. Fixed reference lines that are surveyed and set in advance
of the paving operation are the most accurate and used mainly on new con-
struction. Mobile references are devices attached to and travel along with the
paver as they reference off of the existing surface that the paver is traveling
on. Consequently, their use is limited to projects that have prepared surfaces
that are acceptable to use as a reference. Whichever reference system is used,
it is important to remember that the paver’s automatic controls will follow
the reference line accurately and closely duplicate the lines and grades in the
HMA pavement. Consequently, the finished HMA pavement will be no better
than the reference in terms of line, grade, or smoothness.

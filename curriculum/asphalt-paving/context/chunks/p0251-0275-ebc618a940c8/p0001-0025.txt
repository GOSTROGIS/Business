4.8.1 Stone Matrix Asphalt Mix

Stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixtures have been used in the United States
since 1991. These mixtures have performed well in Europe for many years,
and experience in the United States is similar. SMA is a rut-resistant, gap-
graded HMA. The mix relies on stone-on-stone contact to provide its internal
strength. The mix uses a high asphalt binder content of 6 to 7 percent or more.
This high binder content provides improved durability. In most situations, the
asphalt binder is a polymer-modified asphalt. Mineral filler and either mineral
or cellulose fibers are added to form a stiff mortar and prevent draindown of
the asphalt cement. The in-place air-voids are kept low—to less than 6 per-
cent to insure that the mix is impermeable to water. SMA is routinely used in
many states as the surface of choice for high-volume roadways and intersec-
tions. Figure 4.24 shows an SMA mix in Colorado.

gate used in SMA. AASHTO requires a maximum Los Angeles Abrasion loss of
30 percent. But some states—because of a lack of high-quality aggregates—
have built successfully performing projects with a less-stringent requirement.

4.8.2 Open-Graded Friction Course

Description and Usage

Open-Graded Friction Courses (OGFC) provide an open mix with permeable
voids that provide drainage on the surface of an HMA pavement under wet
conditions. The water drains vertically through the OGFC to an impermeable,
underlying layer and then laterally to the day-lighted edge of the OGFC. In
addition to minimizing hydroplaning and providing high friction resistance on
wet pavements, an OGFC layer will also provide the following advantages:

° Reduce splash and spray (see Figure 4.28)

° Reduce the potential for hydroplaning

° Enhance visibility of pavement markings

° Reduce nighttime surface glare in wet weather

° Reduce tire-pavement noise
While open-graded mixes provide many advantages, they have the following
limitations:

¢ Snow- and ice-control procedures may need adjustment when they

are used,

° They may ravel and shove when used at intersections, locations with

heavy turning movements, and airfield ramp terminals.

° They may require special patching and rehabilitation techniques to

maintain adequate drainage.

Open-graded mixes have been used throughout the United States since
1944. In many states they have been used extensively for many years. In
the early 1970s, many states experienced problems with OGFC layers.
These problems were raveling, delamination, loss of permeability after
a few years of service, and stripping of the underlying layers. These
problems have been addressed by one or more of the following: use of
polymer-modified asphalt binders, relatively high asphalt binder contents

Mix Design Characteristics and Procedures
The two published mix design procedures for the design of open-graded
friction courses include the procedure outlined in the FHWA Technical Advi-
sory T 5040.31 and a procedure based on research by the National Center
for Asphalt Technology (NCAT). Most OGFC surface layers placed in the
United States are designed by the specifying agency, using its own internal
techniques and experience to accomplish the mix design. It is recommended
that a designer check with the specifying agency about its procedure when
designing OGFC mixes. The mix design consists of four steps:
1. Materials selection
2. Selection of the design gradation
3. Determination of the optimum asphalt content
4. Evaluation of moisture susceptibility

Materials Selection

The materials used for OGFC include aggregates, asphalt binders, and
additives (such as fibers). The aggregates must be high-quality, polish-
resistant aggregate materials. The NCAT procedure requires the coarse
aggregate used in an OGFC to have a Los Angeles Abrasion value of less
than 30 percent. Soft aggregates should be avoided due to the poten-
tial for excessive breakdown during mix production and compaction in
the field. The aggregate should consist of 100 percent fractured faces
to provide high internal friction. If crushed gravel is used, it should
have at least 95 percent of the particles with two fractured faces and
100 percent with one fractured face. The fractured faces on gravels should
be produced by mechanical fracturing of the rock. The percentage of flat
and elongated particles should not exceed 5 to 20 percent corresponding
to ratios of 5:1 and 3:1. The fine aggregate used in the mixture should
have a fine aggregate angularity of at least 45 percent.

The asphalt binder used should be based on the environment and
traffic levels of the roadway. A stiffer binder than those recommended
based on the procedures outlined in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt” is
recommended for use. It is suggested that a binder two grades stiffer than
the grade recommended for dense-graded mixes be used and that they
be made with polymer-modified asphalts. The addition of fiber may be
desirable to reduce the amount of draindown during construction. Either
a cellulose fiber is added at about 0.3 percent of total mix or a mineral
fiber added at about 0.4 percent of total mix. The dosage rate for the fibers
depends on the results of the draindown test.

tact is defined as the point at which the voids in the coarse aggregate fraction

4.8.3 Asphalt-Treated Permeable Base

General Description

An asphalt-treated permeable base (ATPB) is built as a base for an HMA
pavement. The purpose of this layer is to intercept the water that enters
through the pavement surface before it can reach the unbound layers, result-
ing in lowering the strength of those layers. The water enters the pavement
through cracks in the surface and/or a poorly compacted and therefore
permeable HMA layer. The layer should be designed to provide sufficient
air-voids to make the layer permeable (approximately 10 percent). The per-
formance and maintenance of these systems is discussed in more detail in
Chapter 5 of this handbook.

Mix Design Characteristics and Procedures

The mix design of an ATPB is very similar to that used for an OGFC
layer. The goal is to provide a free-draining layer with sufficient asphalt
binder to cement the particles together and to prevent stripping of the
asphalt binder from the aggregate.

Table 4.11 presents gradations being used by different agencies for an
ATPB layer.

The asphalt binder can be either the standard grade of binder used by the
agency (such as a 64-22) or a modified binder. The primary concern in the
choice of asphalt binder is insuring the coating of the aggregate and minimiz-
ing the amount of draindown during construction. Coating is evaluated by
visually observing the mix in the laboratory during mixing. Different binder
contents should be evaluated. The draindown test discussed in the sections
on SMA and OFGC can be used to evaluate the draindown characteristics of
the mix. If the draindown exceeds 0.3 percent by weight of aggregate, steps
should be taken to reduce the draindown, such as using fibers or a polymer-
modified asphalt binder.

The air-voids in the mix can be evaluated in the same manner as with
the OGFC mixes. The mix should be compacted using 50 gyrations with
a SGC and the bulk specific gravity of the ATPB determined using the
CoreLok® procedure.

4.9 Performance

Testing of HMA

4.9.1 Introduction

Performance testing of HMA mixes is conducted to predict the perfor-
mance of HMA prior to use on a transportation facility and to evaluate new
materials and design tools to improve the performance of HMA pavements.
HMA mixture testing can be divided into five general categories:

* Modulus—shear, dynamic and resilient modulus

° Creep—static and dynamic creep

° Flexural beam fatigue

¢ Empirical

* Wheel-tracking tests

4.9.2 Modulus Testing

The modulus of a material is used to calculate the expected deformation
from a specified load. The modulus of elasticity is determined by applying
increasing loads at a specified rate and measuring the resulting deformation.
Loads are converted to stress by dividing by the cross-sectional area of the
specimen section under load. Deformation is converted to strain by dividing
the change in length (deformation) by the original (non-deformed) length. In
shear testing, the shear modulus (G) of a material is determined by dividing
the shear stress (7) by the shear strain (y). In axial testing, the modulus of elas-
ticity (E) is determined by dividing the axial stress (o) by the axial strain (e).

Shear Modulus

The specimens used for shear and dynamic modulus testing are gener-
ally manufactured using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC). An
HMA sample is obtained by mixing the materials in the laboratory or from
a hot mix asphalt mixing facility. Mixtures prepared in the laboratory are
subjected to short-term oven aging (STOA). For shear modulus testing a
STOA of four hours at 275°F (135°C) is used. This is longer than the two
hours used in Superpave for mix design. Stiffening of mixtures and binder
frequently occurs in any HMA facility; therefore the STOA procedure is not
used for plant-produced mixtures.

The shear modulus test is used to describe the viscoelastic behavior of
an asphalt mix. It is described in AASHTO TP 7. A sinusoidal shear strain of
0.01 percent (peak-to-peak) is applied to the specimen at ten frequencies
(0.01 to 10 Hz). The shear stress and strain are then used to calculate the
complex shear modulus (G*) of the asphalt mixture. The loading rate for
this test is selected based on what the material will experience under

Dynamic (Axial) Modulus

The dynamic modulus, sometimes called complex modulus, is determined
by applying sinusoidal (haversine) vertical loads to the specimen at different
frequencies and measuring the recoverable vertical strain. The test proce-
dure for determining the dynamic modulus is provided in AASHTO TP 2 or
ASTM D 3497. The axial stress and strain are used to calculate the dynamic
modulus. The dynamic modulus is calculated by dividing the repeated stress
by the repeated strain. The difference between this test and the shear mcdu-
lus test is the type of loading (axial versus shear loading) as well as the fact
that the dynamic modulus test is stress-controlled while the shear modulus
test is strain-controlled. This test is used to describe the viscoelastic behav-

to evaluate the rutting resistance of an HMA mixture.

4.9.3 Creep Testing

Creep testing is a simple version of a controlled stress test. There are two
types of creep tests—static creep and dynamic creep. In the static creep test
a constant axial load is applied to the test specimen throughout the duration
of the test. In the dynamic creep test, a large number of repeated axial load
applications are applied to the specimen. It is called “dynamic” because the
specimen is being continually loaded and unloaded throughout the dura-
tion of the test.

Static creep

In the static creep test an axial load is rapidly applied (within 0.05
sec) to the test specimen until a desired axial stress is achieved (usually
between 69 and 207 kiloPascals). This axial stress is then maintained until
the total axial strain reaches approximately 2 percent (20,000 microstrain)
or tertiary flow occurs in the test specimen. The general shape of the axial
strain curve is shown in Figure 4.30.

At any time during the test, the applied stress can be divided by the
observed strain to arrive at a creep modulus or stiffness. Because the
strain changes with time, so does the creep modulus. In creep testing,
many engineers prefer to compute creep compliance rather than creep
stiffness. Creep compliance can be portrayed as the inverse of creep stiff-
ness (although it is not strictly correct, theoretically). In other words, creep
compliance is the strain at any time during the test per unit of stress. At
the conclusion of a creep test, the test specimen will partially rebound to
its original shape. For many materials, however, some permanent deforma-
tion remains in the test specimen.

The rate of change of creep compliance versus loading time is then
calculated and plotted as shown in Figure 4.31. The minimum point of the
curve occurs where the rate of creep compliance changes as a function of
time is zero. This point is identified as the flow time and is the time when
shear deformation occurs under constant volume. Higher values of flow
time (FT) from the axial static creep test are thought to be related to better
rutting resistance in the HMA mixture.

4.9.4 Flexural Fatigue Test

The flexural fatigue test is conducted to evaluate the fatigue charac-
teristics of an HMA mixture. Fatigue cracking of pavement is considered
to be more a structural problem than simply a material problem. Several
external factors influence the fatigue cracking in pavements, such as poor
subgrade drainage, time of placement, and method of compaction and
placement of the asphalt mix.

increasing load cycles. In this method, the samples do not break during
the test. Failure is defined when the applied load is reached at a certain
percentage of the original applied load. Usually, failure is defined as 50
percent loss in initial beam stiffness. The test results are applicable to thin
pavements—less than 2 inches (50 millimeters) thick. A typical plot of fatigue

4.9.6 Wheel-Tracking Tests

Hamburg Wheel-Tracking Device (HWTD)

This machine was developed by the Germans in the 1970s. The test pro-
cedure is AASHTO T 324. The HWTD measures the combined effects of rut-
ting and moisture on an HMA mixture. A steel wheel rolls across the surface
of the HMA core, which is immersed in hot water. The steel wheel moves
through a crank connected to a flywheel. The steel wheel has a diameter
of 8 inches (204 millimeters) and is 1.85 inches (47 millimeters) wide with
a 154-pound load applied. The testing is done with the samples in a hot
water bath at 50°C. The test is run for 10,000 cycles (20,000 passes) or until
a deformation of 0.787 inch (20 millimeters) is reached. The machine is
shown in Figure 4.35.

Figure 4.36 is a sample of the typical data obtained from the HWTD. Three
points are identified on the curve: the creep slope, the stripping slope, and
the stripping inflection point. The creep slope occurs before the onset of strip-
ping and is the inverse of the rate of deformation in the linear range of the
deformation curve. This portion of the curve is where rutting will occur due
to consolidation and plastic flow. The stripping slope is related to the severity
of the damage due to moisture. It is the inverse of the rate of deformation in
the linear region after the onset of the stripping to the end of the test. The
stripping inflection point is related to the resistance of the HMA tested to the
effects of the moisture. This point is determined at the number of passes at
which the creep slope and the stripping slope intersect.

In its executive summary on performance testing of HMA mixes, NCAT
suggested rut-depth criteria of less than 0.393 inch (10 millimeters) after
10,000 cycles (20,000 passes) for conventional dense-graded HMA mixes.

Asphalt Pavement Analyzer (APA)

The Asphalt Pavement Analyzer (APA) is a modification of the Georgia
Loaded Wheel Tester (GLWT), which was developed in a cooperative study
of the Georgia Department of Transportation and the Georgia Institute
of Technology in 1985. It has been used primarily to evaluate the rutting
resistance of an HMA mixture. Studies have been conducted to evaluate
the use of the equipment for evaluating fatigue and moisture resistance of
HMA mixtures. The APA is shown in Figure 4.37.

A loaded wheel is placed on a pressurized hose, which sits on the test
specimens, and then tracked back and forth to induce rutting. Generally,
the specimens are tested to 8,000 cycles. Samples can be tested dry or
while submerged in water.

The test specimens for the APA can be either beam or cylindrical
specimens. The beam samples are generally compacted using the Asphalt
Vibratory Compactor (Figure 4.38). The cylindrical specimens are made
with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor. The beams are compacted to
7 percent air-voids, and the cylindrical specimens are compacted to both 4
and 7 percent air-voids. Cores from an in-service roadway can also be tested.

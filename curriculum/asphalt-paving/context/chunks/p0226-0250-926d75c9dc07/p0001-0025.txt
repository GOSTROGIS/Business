Calibration of Compactors

A number of manufacturers produce Superpave Gyratory Compactors.
See Figure 4.15 for a composite photo of different compaction equipment.

In the fabrication of the SGC specimens, a constant consolidation
pressure is applied to the sample while the mold gyrates at a nomi-
nally constant angle (the angle of gyration). To provide specimens of
consistent density, it is very important that the SCC maintain a constant
pressure and a known constant angle of gyration during the compac-
tion process. Each model of SGC uses a different method of setting,
inducing, and maintaining the angle of gyration. Each model also uses
a unique calibration system to measure the angle of gyration externally
in relation to the mold. These calibration systems cannot be used uni-
versally on all models of SGCs. Inconsistencies in the density of asphalt
specimens produced by different SGCs can be attributed to variations
in the angle of gyration. This can be very troublesome when comparing
mix designs prepared by different compactors or during quality con-
trol, when the quality control specimens are manufactured using one
brand of compactor and the acceptance specimens are manufactured
using another brand of compactor. The American Association of State
Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) have developed a pro-
cedure for evaluating the internal angle of a SGC. It is AASHTO PP 48
“Evaluation of the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC) Internal Angle
of Gyration.”

Preparation of Aggregate

Each aggregate blend being evaluated should have a batch sheet show-
ing the batch weights of each aggregate component and the asphalt binder.
Three mix sample sizes are needed to complete a mix design. For the com-
pacted mix specimens that will be used in the Superpave mix design, the
specimen size is 150 millimeters (diameter) by 115 millimeters (height)
and requires approximately 4700 grams of aggregate. Samples of mix to be
used for the determination of the maximum specific gravity using AASHTO
T 209 or ASTM D 2401 will remain uncompacted, and their size will vary
by aggregate size, ranging from 1000 grams to 2500 grams. Moisture testing
using AASHTO T 283 or ASTM D 4867 requires a mix specimen height of
95 millimeters and will require approximately 3700 grams of aggregate.

Compaction of Volumetric Specimens

Detailed procedures for the preparation of the volumetric specimens are
contained in the SP-2 manual. The specimens are compacted to either N,,.,
or N_,, 48 required by the specifying agency. A minimum of two specimens
are compacted at the trial blend’s estimated asphalt content, at 0.5 percent
of the estimated asphalt content, and at 1.0 percent above the estimated
asphalt content. Four asphalt contents are required for the accomplish-
ment of a Superpave mix design. Many designers will estimate the trial
binder content based on experience.

The specimens are mixed and compacted at temperatures using a plot of
viscosity versus temperature (Figure 4.16). This data is available from the
asphalt binder supplier. The mixing and compaction temperatures are those
corresponding to a binder viscosity value of 0.17 + 0.02 Pascal-second and
0.28 + 0.03 Pascal-second, respectively. These viscosity ranges are not valid
for modified asphalt binders. The designer should consider the manufacturer's
recommendations when establishing mixing and compaction temperatures
for modified binders. Practically, the mixing temperature should not exceed

Superpave mix design requires that all samples, prior to compaction,

Data Analysis and Presentation

After compaction of the samples, the volumetric properties of each spec-
imen are determined. The results of the laboratory testing are plotted to
provide curves of asphalt content versus air-voids, voids mineral aggregate,
and voids filled with asphalt. Typical curves are shown in Figure 4.17.

4.3.4 Design Asphalt Binder Content

After the data has been plotted, the designer will pick an asphalt binder
content that will provide 4 percent air-voids at N,,,. Then the designer
determines the values for voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA) and voids
filled with asphalt (VFA) from the other curves. These values will then be
checked against the mixture specifications. The recommended Superpave
volumetric mixture design requirements are shown in Table 4.5.

4.3.5 Steps That Can Be Taken to Adjust _
Properties

If the testing indicates that the mix does not meet the mix design require-
ments, the following suggestions are provided (AASHTO R 35):

° Adjusting the VMA—A change in the aggregate gradation or aggre-
gate source is usually required to adjust the VMA. There are three
likely options:

o Change the gradation. Moving the gradation curve toward the maxi-
mum density line will generally reduce the VMA; moving it away
from the maximum density line will generally increase the VMA.

o Change the amount of minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeters) mate-
rial. Increasing the No. 200 material usually lowers the VMA, and
decreasing it will typically raise the VMA.

o Change the surface texture and/or the shape of the particles of
the fine aggregate fraction. The increased use of an aggregate with
a rough texture (higher FAA) will generally increase the VMA, and
the increased use of a rounded aggregate will reduce the VMA.

4.3.6 Moisture Sensitivity

The loss of bond, or stripping, caused by the presence of moisture
between the asphalt and the aggregate is a problem in some areas and
can be severe in some cases. Many factors, such as aggregate character-
istics, asphalt characteristics, environment, traffic, construction practices,
and drainage, can contribute to stripping.

The moisture susceptibility test used to evaluate HMA for stripping is
AASHTO T 283 or ASTM D 4867. This test is not a performance-based test,
but it serves two purposes. First, it identifies whether a combination of
asphalt binder and aggregate is moisture susceptible. Second, it measures
the effectiveness of antistripping additives.

In the test, two subsets of test specimens are produced. Specimens are
compacted to a height of 95 millimeters and to an air-void content of 6.5 to
7.5 percent with a target value of 7 percent. Test specimens should be sorted
so that each subset has the same average air-void content. One subset is
moisture conditioned by vacuum saturation to a constant degree of saturation
in the range of 70 to 80 percent. This is followed by an optional freeze cycle.
The final conditioning step is a hot-water soak. After conditioning, both sub-
sets are tested for indirect tensile strength. After conditioning, the specimen
is removed from the water bath and placed on its side between the bearing
plates of the testing machine. Steel loading strips are placed between the
specimen and the bearing plates. A load is applied to the specimen at the
rate of 2 inches (50 millimeters) per minute. The maximum load is recorded.
This is the indirect tensile strength of the specimen. This test is conducted on
the conditioned and the unconditioned specimens. The average strength for
the conditioned and the unconditioned specimens is determined. The ratio
of these two values is the tensile-strength ratio or TSR. Superpave requires a
minimum TSR of 80 percent. Lower values are indicative of mixtures that may
exhibit stripping problems after construction.

Steps to Improve Moisture Resistance

The best way to improve the moisture resistance of an HMA mixture is
a proper mix design with sufficient asphalt binder to coat the aggregates.
This must be coupled with proper compaction in the field. The goal is to
produce as impermeable an HMA mixture as possible to prevent the intru-
sion of water. However, some asphalt/aggregate combinations will require
an additive to improve the moisture resistance of the mixture. The two
most commonly used admixtures are hydrated lime and proprietary liquid
antistrip agents. It is imperative that the mix design be accomplished using
the admixture and the asphalt binder that will be used on the project. The
reaction is chemical, and the results will vary depending on the type and
amount of additive, source of aggregate and source of asphalt binder. For
more information on moisture sensitivity, see the Asphalt Institute’s MS-24,
Moisture Sensitivity manual.

4,4 Marshall Method
of Mix Design

4.4.1 Introduction and Historical Context

The concepts of the Marshall method of designing paving mixtures
were formulated by Bruce Marshall, formerly a bituminous engineer with
the Mississippi State Highway Department. The U.S. Corps of Engineers,
through extensive research and correlation studies, improved and added
certain features to Marshall’s test procedure, and ultimately developed mix
design criteria. The Marshall test procedures have been standardized by
the American Society for Testing and Materials. Procedures are given by
ASTM D 1559.

The Marshall method has generally been applicable to dense-graded
HMA paving mixtures using Performance Graded (PG) asphalt binders
and containing aggregates with a maximum size of 1 inch (25 milli-
meters) or less. It has also been adapted to the design of SMA mixtures.
The Marshall method is used for both laboratory design and the qual-
ity control of hot mix asphalt pavements. For a detailed description of
the steps and equipment needed to conduct a Marshall Mix Design, the
reader is referred to the Asphalt Institute’s MS-2 Mix Design Methods
manual. The Asphalt Institute Mix Design Program SW-2 performs all of
the calculations needed to conduct a Marshall Mix Design. (It is recom-
mended that mix designers refer to either AASHTO or ASTM require-
ments, or to the design agency’s requirements so that those specific
procedures can be followed.)

4.4.2 Description of Method

The Marshall method uses standard cylindrical test specimens of
24 inches (64 millimeters) in height by 4 inches (102 millimeters) in diam-
eter. These are prepared using a specified procedure for heating, mixing,
and compacting the asphalt-aggregate mixtures. These specimens are
compacted using a Marshall compaction hammer (Figure 4.18) and tested
in a Marshall stability and flow device (see Figure 4.19). The two principal
features of the Marshall method of mix design are a density-voids analysis
and a stability-flow test of the compacted test specimens.

The stability of the test specimens is the maximum load resistance in
pounds (newtons) that the standard test specimen will develop at 140°F
(60°C) when tested as outlined here. The flow value is the total movement
or strain, in units of 1/100 inch (0.25 millimeter), occurring in the speci-
men between no load and maximum load during the stability test.

requirements. The design criteria in Table 4.6 are recommended by the
Asphalt Institute. If all the criteria are met, then this is the design asphalt
content. If any of the design criteria are not met, then some adjustment is

4.5 Hveem Method of

Mix Design

4.5.1 introduction and Historical Context

The Hveem method of mix design is a laboratory procedure based on
two properties of the compacted specimen: cohesion and friction. Den-
sity and voids of the compacted specimen are established before mea-
suring the stability with the stabilometer. The stabilometer is a triaxial
testing device in which vertical loads are applied and resulting lateral
pressures are measured. The concepts of the Hveem method of design-
ing paving mixtures were advanced and developed under the direction of
Francis N. Hveem, who was the materials and research engineer for the
California Division of Highways. The test procedures and their application
were developed through extensive research and correlation studies on
asphalt highway pavements.

The method shown is applicable to the design of hot, dense-graded
asphalt paving mixtures.

4.5.2 Description of Method

Hveem method test procedures have been standardized by the ASTM
and AASHTO. Test procedures are found in AASHTO T 246 or ASTM D
1560, and AASHTO T 247 or ASTM D 1561. Testing procedures presented
here are basically the same as those in the ASTM procedure. For a detailed
description of the Hveem procedure, the reader is referred to the Asphalt
Institute’s MS-2, Mix Design Methods manual. It is recommended that agen-
cies refer to either AASHTO or ASTM, or to the design agency’s require-
ments, so that those specific procedures can be followed.

The Hveem method uses standard test specimens of 2.5 inches (64 mil-
limeters) in height by 4 inches (102 millimeters) in diameter; these are pre-
pared using a specified procedure for heating, mixing, and compacting the
asphalt-aggregate mixtures. The principal features of the Hveem method of
mix design are the oil soak and Centrifuge Kerosene Equivalent (CKE) tests
on the aggregates to estimate the asphalt requirements of the mix, followed
by a stabilometer test, a cohesiometer test, a swell test, and a density-voids
analysis on test specimens of the compacted paving mixtures. The stabilom-
eter test uses a special triaxial-type testing cell for measuring the resistance
of the compacted mix to lateral displacement under vertical loading, and the
swell test measures the resistance of the mix to the action of water. The speci-
mens are maintained at 140°F (60°C) for the stability tests; the swell tests are
performed at room temperature.

The Hveem mix design process consists of three basic steps:

1. Aggregate selection. This step consists of insuring that the
aggregates used in the mixture meet the design requirements. For
a detailed discussion of the aggregate requirements for an HMA
mixture, the reader is referred to Chapter 3, “Mineral Aggregates
and Mixture Additives.” Once the designer is assured that the
aggregates meet the specified physical requirements, the next step
is to blend the aggregates to meet the requirements.

2. Asphalt binder selection. The designer identifies the asphalt
binder to be used in the mixture based on the procedures
described in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt.”

3. Test specimen preparation. A series of test specimens is pre-
pared using a particular blend or gradation of aggregate over a
range of different asphalt contents so that the test data curves
show a well-defined optimum value. Tests should be scheduled
on the basis of 0.5 percent increments of asphalt content, with at
least two asphalt contents above optimum and at least two below
optimum. The following procedure outlines the steps associated
with the determination of the optimum asphalt content using the
Hveem method:

a. In the Hveem method, the initial asphalt content is determined
by the oil soak and Centrifuge Kerosene Equivalent tests and
the calculated surface area. For example, for a mix with an esti-
mated asphalt content of 5.5 percent, one sample each would
be made at 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, and 6.5 percent asphalt by weight
for a total of five samples. A minimum of 2 additional samples
would be used for the determination of the maximum specific
gravity as discussed in Section 4.2. Compact the samples using
the California Kneading Compactor (see Figure 4.21).

b. Test each sample for stability and cohesion using the Hveem
Stabilometer and Cohesiometer (see Figure 4.22). The passing
values of stability and cohesion are specific to the agency
using the design procedure.

c. Determine the volumetric properties of the mix. Data is plot-
ted as shown in Figure 4.23.

4.6 RAP Mix Design

Hot mix recycling consists of combining reclaimed (or recycled) asphalt
pavement (RAP) with new or “virgin” aggregates, and new asphalt binder
and/or a recycling agent in a central plant to produce a new HMA mix.
The amount of RAP used in an HMA mix is related to the plant type being
used, the gradation of the RAP aggregates, and the properties of the asphalt
binder in the RAP. The ratio of RAP to virgin aggregates will vary. It is typi-
cally 15 to 25 percent for batch plants, and up to 30 to 50 percent for drum
plants. The manufacture of an HMA mix using RAP is discussed in detail
in Chapter 7, “Manufacture of Hot Mix Asphalt.” This section will discuss
the mix design procedures. The procedures outlined below are based on
AASHTO M 323, Superpave Volumetric Mix Design, and the NCHRP report

4.6.1 Materials Evaluation

The first step in the evaluation of the materials for a project that will include
RAP in the HMA is to obtain a representative sample of the RAP to be used.
A major concern with the use of RAP is the variability of the material. This is
because the RAP may come from different layers in the pavement structure
or from stockpiles of materials from different sources. Good stockpiling prac-
tices or processing the RAP by crushing and/or screening will reduce the vari-
ability. RAP can be sampled from the roadway (by coring or bulk samples),
from a stockpile, or from the haul trucks. Good sampling practices should be
followed. Sampling methods are discussed in detail in Chapter 10.

Aggregate Gradation

The properties of the aggregate after the asphalt has been extracted
from the RAP should be determined. The ignition oven (AASHTO T 308
or ASTM D 6307) can be used to determine the RAP composition (asphalt
content and gradation) if further testing of the asphalt binder will not be
accomplished. If the extracted asphalt binder is to be recovered and tested
for future evaluation, AASHTO T 164 or ASTM D 2172 should be used for
the extraction process. After extraction of the asphalt binder from the mix,
the gradation of the aggregate is determined. Any deficiency in the grada-
tion can be corrected by blending the appropriate fractions of virgin and
reclaimed aggregate to meet the gradation requirements of the final HMA
mixture. It is also recommended that the angularity of the fine and coarse
aggregates be determined. This information may influence the percentage
of RAP to be used for a particular project.

To calculate the VMA, it is necessary to determine the combined bulk
specific gravity of the aggregates (RAP and virgin aggregates). Two
approaches are used. The first approach consists of splitting the extracted
aggregate into fine (minus No. 4 [4.75 millimeters]) and coarse fractions
(plus No. 4 [4.75 millimeters]), and determining the specific gravity of each
fraction. The advantage is that this provides a direct measure of the specific
gravity of the aggregate. The disadvantages are that the extraction process
(chemical or ignition) will damage the aggregate or that the samples will
contain asphalt or solvent, which will make it difficult to accurately mea-
sure the specific gravity. The second approach consists of determining the
maximum specific gravity of the RAP (G__) using AASHTO T 209 or ASTM
D 2041, determining the asphalt content of the RAP, and then calculating
the effective specific gravity (G,.) of the aggregate. The advantage of this
method is that it is more accurate than the determination of the bulk specific
gravity of the individual fractions. The disadvantage is that using G,, rather
than G,, of the RAP aggregate will result in an overestimate of the combined
bulk specific gravity and will artificially raise the VMA of the HMA mix. The
error will increase with higher percentages of RAP.

4.7 Aggregate Packing Analysis
Using the Bailey Method

A procedure to evaluate the aggregate packing properties in HMA, called
the Bailey Method, is able to predict volumetric and compactability charac-
teristics of HMA. The system was first developed by Robert D. Bailey, who
worked with the Illinois District 5 Materials Bureau. Further refinements
have been developed by the Heritage Research Group of Indianapolis,
Indiana.

The procedure is based on the following four main principles:

1. % PCS—Percent passing the Primary Control Sieve
2. CA Ratio—The Coarse Aggregate Ratio

3. FA, Ratio—The Fine Aggregate Coarse Ratio

4. FA, Ratio—The Fine Aggregate Fine Ratio

Percent PCS

The Bailey Method is based on the concept that the coarse Cargest)
particle sizes of any mixture creates voids that are occupied by finer aggre-
gates, asphalt, and air. The Primary Control Sieve (PCS) is the aggregate
size that determines what is coarse and what is fine for any aggregate or
aggregate combination. The volume of coarse aggregate (retained on the
PCS) determines if a mix is a “coarse graded mix,” a “fine graded mix” or
a stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mix.

A coarse graded mix is defined as mixture that has a volume of coarse
aggregate great enough to develop stone-on-stone contact. The voids, in
the coarse aggregate then determine the amount of fine aggregate that can
be accommodated in the mix.

An SMA mix is a coarse graded mixture that contains a volume of fine
aggregate that is significantly less than the volume of voids created by the
coarse aggregate.

A fine graded mix is defined as a mixture that contains a volume of
fine aggregate that exceeds the volume of voids that exist in the coarse
aggregate when stone-on-stone contact is achieved. The volume of voids
in the coarse aggregate is determined utilizing methods described in
AASHTO T 19.

The Bailey Method has the ability to estimate the change in VMA (voids
in the mineral aggregate) based solely on the change in gradation between
two samples. It will not calculate VMA prior to laboratory testing. Once
a known VMA is determined on a compacted specimen, the amount and
direction of change in VMA can be estimated by the Bailey Method based
on gradation changes. However, it is extremely important to remember
that the rate of change and direction of change is different for each of the
three mix types.

CA, FA, and FA, Ratios

The CA, FA, and FA, ratios are factors used in the Bailey Method that
are calculated on specific fractions of the combined aggregate used in
HMA. The CA ratio describes the distribution of large coarse aggregate
particles and smaller coarse aggregate particles. This distribution of par-
ticles retained on the PCS determines the number and sizes of voids cre-
ated by the coarse fraction of the mix. The FA, ratio provides a comparison
of coarse sand (material passing the PCS) and fine sand. The coarse sand
fraction of the mix creates voids within the coarse aggregate structure that
are filled by fine sand. The FA, ratio is very similar to the FA, ratio but deals
with the finest fractions in the mixture. VMA movement and compaction
characteristics due to gradation change are influenced, described, and pre-
dicted by the CA, FA, and FA, ratios.

The Bailey Method is a very powerful tool to analyze VMA and com-
pactability of HMA mixtures that cannot be covered in depth in this man-
ual. Transportation Research Board Circular #E-C044 provides in-depth
discussion of the method.

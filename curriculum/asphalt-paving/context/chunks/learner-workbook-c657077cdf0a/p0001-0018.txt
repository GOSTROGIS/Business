Begin with a reliable record
A paving record is useful when someone else can follow it: where the numbers came from, which units
they use, what changed and what remains uncertain. A neat answer with the wrong area or an obsolete
ticket can lead the next person in the wrong direction. In this module, your job is to build a record that
another person can check.

You will read a specification table, compare fictional aggregate results, total ten delivery tickets, measure
two rectangles from supplied dimensions and revise the calculation when a correction arrives. You will
also explain your reasoning, hear a partner's check, work through different roles and improve the record
form after testing it.

All sample results, deliveries and dimensions below are invented for classroom practice. Limits explicitly
labelled Table 501-E and Table 501-F come from the 2006 Design Build Standard Specifications for
Highway Construction, adopted 20 February 2006. The publisher is the British Columbia Ministry of
Transportation, Construction and Maintenance Branch. This historical edition has not been established as
the governing document for a present project. Use its language as technical reading; use a real project's
confirmed requirements and authorized instructions for actual work.

This is a document-and-calculation classroom task. It does not involve sampling, plant settings,
equipment operation or approval of pavement. Work in a classroom or other agreed learning space.
Choose written, spoken or supported responses where offered. A calculator and a unit guide are welcome.
Use a partner or ask the facilitator to take that role.


Where this work fits in the trade
The trade outline's A5, “Describe basic road and highway profiles,” task 4, asks learners to review
specifications for different types of roads. Here you practise that document-reading skill with a clearly
dated B.C. specification. The unit also introduces parts of F2, “Verify slopes, crowns and grades,” tasks 1
and 4: units, drawing information and calculations. SP-02 and the area exercise provide introductory
context for F3, “Describe final grade preparation,” tasks 2 and 4: fine grading and the limit of asphalt
layout. A rectangle calculation does not demonstrate grade measurement, equipment calibration or
finished-mat layout. Keep those practical outcomes for supervised technical training (SkilledTradesBC,
2016, printed pp. 24, 49–51; PDF pp. 26, 51–53).


Your record pack
You need this workbook, paper or an editable copy, a calculator, and a device with spreadsheet software
for SP-12. The CSV data pack contains the same invented numbers printed here. Keep your first
calculation and the corrected version. Write a name or learner code only if you choose; use no personal
information in a practice delivery record.

Useful notation: mm means millimetres; m means metres; m² means square metres; g means grams; kg
means kilograms; t means metric tonnes. One tonne equals 1,000 kg. A percentage is an amount out of
100. A percentage-point difference subtracts two percentages; it is different from a relative percentage
change.

If a fictional dry sample has a total mass of 1,000 g, and 26% passes the 0.600 mm sieve, the passing

2 · Follow a running mean
A mean adds the results and divides by the number of results. A running mean uses a moving window:
when the next result arrives, drop the oldest one and include the newest. A four-test mean always has four
values.

Under 501.15.03, PDF 286–287, the mean of four consecutive tests is compared with the established
grading curve using Table 501-F. At 4.75 mm, the maximum variation is ±4.5 percentage points. The
following paragraph also says that the running mean must stay inside the applicable Table 501-E limits.
Being close to a curve does not cancel the absolute interval.

Worked example · Units determine the calculation
For an unrelated invented record, 24 t covers 120 m². Convert the mass: 24 × 1,000 = 24,000 kg. Divide:
24,000 kg ÷ 120 m² = 200 kg/m². An alternative route is 24 ÷ 120 = 0.2 t/m², then 0.2 × 1,000 = 200
kg/m². The two routes agree. Dividing area by mass would give m²/kg, a different quantity.

The historical 502.14.01, PDF 309, determines material application rate from delivered tonnage recorded
on weigh tickets divided by the covered Lot area, including appropriate allowance for entrance letdowns
and intersections. Here we practise the mass/area relationship on our ten-load check block only.

Another variant of the hot-side method is holding the roller drum
approximately 6 inches (150 millimeters) away from the longitudinal joint
on the first pass toward the paver. This leaves a narrow strip of uncom-
pacted mix. As the roller reverses direction, the roller drum is moved over
to overlap 6 inches (150 millimeters) on the cold mat, trapping the narrow
strip under the drum and forcing it into place.

For lifts greater than 3 inches (76 millimeters), the rolling process should start
12-15 inches (300-375 millimeters) from the lower unsupported edge, with
subsequent passes covering the interior portion of the mat. Succeeding passes
of the roller should then gradually progress toward the edges of the spread.
The uncompacted edge provides initial confinement during the first pass,
thus minimizing lateral movement of the mix. After the central portion of the
spread has been compacted, the mix will have cooled slightly and can sup-
port the roller. This will allow the edge to be compacted with minimal lateral
movement.

Cold-side rolling. A method that is sometimes used is to place the roller
on the previously placed lane so that not more than 6 inches (150 millime-
ters) rides on the edge of the newly spread material. Here it is operated
to pinch and press the uncompacted material into place. The roller should
continue to roll along this line, its position being shifted gradually across
the joint until a thoroughly compacted, neat joint is obtained. Vibration
should not be used when the roller is operating on a cold mat. This is
normally the least efficient rolling method because the hot mat is cooling
while the roller is mostly operating on the cold mat.

Rolling Edges

When rolling a longitudinal joint or HMA edge against an existing con-
crete gutter or curb lip, the roller should not span the joint in a manner
that puts part of the roller weight on the gutter. By bridging the roller on
the PCC curb, insufficient compaction along the HMA edge may result.
One common method to obtain good HMA compaction along an existing
curb is to require the edge of the finished pavement to have an elevation
slightly higher than the adjacent gutter. This ensures that the full compac-
tive force of the roller will be on the HMA.

9.5 Troubleshooting

This section is not meant to be an exhaustive summary of all issues
relative to compaction. Rather, it will cover the following problem areas:
pickup, low density, erratic density, and roller marks. A key to successful
compaction is the use of an experienced quality-control technician with
a density gauge and infrared thermometer. With these two tools and the
authority to work with the roller operators to adjust the rolling pattern as
needed, a quality-control technician is a major asset to a paving operation.

9.5.1 Pickup

Mix pickup leaves scars on the mat and is not aesthetically pleasing.
Pickup is defined as mix sticking to the drums or tires of the roller.
* Pick up on steel drums is typically due to a lack of water-spray sys-
tems and scrapers/pads on the equipment.
e Pick up on pneumatic-tire rollers is typically caused by tires that are
too cold, are not properly inflated, or lack pads/scrapers.

9.5.2 Low Density

In brief, if the target density is not being achieved, the operation needs to
be adjusted. Merely speeding up the rollers is counterproductive and will actu-
ally lower density. In fact, slowing the paving and rolling operation should be
among the first things to consider, along with the following:

° Establish a new test strip to evaluate the paving-and-rolling process.

e Check the temperature of the mix upon delivery and ensure that it is
adequate to achieve density and uniformity between loads.

* Check the calibration of the density gauge against a core to ensure
that the gauge is properly calibrated.

° Make sure the paver has not increased speed because the rollers will
speed up to catch up.

° Ensure that the rollers have been and continue to be compacting
using the established rolling pattern at the proper speed, frequency,
and amplitude.

° Check the environmental conditions to determine whether something
significant has changed (air temperature, cloud cover, wind speed,
shade) because this can affect the time available for compaction.

° Consider shortening the rolling zone.

Increase the number of coverages and recheck density.

9.5.3 Erratic Density

Erratic density is more difficult to troubleshoot because, by its nature, it
is not uniform. In addition to ensuring all the items listed in the previous
section have been checked, one should look for changing conditions and
erratic roller operation. Changes in density, temperature, mix characteris-
tics, paver speed, etc. should be monitored.

9.5.4 Roller Marks

Roller marks are any marks or deficiencies caused by the rollers.

* Pneumatic-tire rollers can “pick up” if the tires are not hot, properly
inflated, and clean.

Rollers sitting behind a stopped paver will leave marks in the mat that
will be almost impossible to remove. If a roller has to stop, it must
stop in an area where the mix has cooled.

¢ If a roller is turned too sharply while it is moving, the edge of the
drum can cut or tear the mat.

° If marks are still present after the finish roller has completed, the
finish roller is either too light or the time between intermediate and
finish rolling was too great (mix cooled too much).

° Poorly maintained equipment, such as worn or pitted drums, can
imprint patterns in the mat.

The key to uniform density throughout the compaction operation is
keeping track of the little details and monitoring conditions. Compacting
hot mix asphalt is not difficult, what is challenging is doing it consistently
all the time.

10.1 General

Long-term performance of hot mix asphalt GAMA) pavements is achieved
by using quality materials and placement techniques during construction.
The life of a pavement and the cost of maintenance can be very sensitive
to seemingly minor variations in materials properties, such as aggregate
gradation, asphalt content, mixture volumetrics, and compaction.

Highway agencies and other owners of asphalt pavements use physi-
cal test results to evaluate the quality of materials and the methods used
in their projects. Each agency’s overall quality-assurance program deter-
mines testing requirements. These programs generally include three types
of tests: quality-control, acceptance, and independent assurance.

10.1.1 Quality-Assurance Programs

The definitions used in this chapter have been adapted from the TRB
Transportation Circular, Glossary of Highway Quality Assurance Terms.
The following terms are presented relative to quality-assurance programs.
This chapter focuses mainly on these terms as they relate to sampling and
testing, and is limited to aggregates and HMA mixtures.

Quality Assurance. All planned and systematic actions necessary to
provide confidence that a product or facility will perform satisfactorily in
service. :

Quality Control (also called Process Control). Actions and consider-
ations necessary to assess and adjust production and construction pro-
cesses in order to control the level of quality being produced in the product.
Quality-control practices are usually provided by contractors and/or mate-
rials suppliers to monitor the product manufacturing process.

Acceptance. Sampling and testing, or inspection, to determine the
degree of compliance with contract requirements. Acceptance practices
are usually provided by the owner or his designated representative to
determine whether specific terms of the plans and specifications have
been met.

Independent Assurance. Management tool that requires a third party,
not directly responsible for process control or acceptance, to provide an
independent assessment of the product and/or the reliability of test results
obtained from the process-control and acceptance testing. This third-party
testing and inspection is usually provided by the owner but accomplished
by a laboratory that is not involved in the quality-control or acceptance.

10.1.2 Job-Mix Formula

For quality-control and acceptance of hot mix asphalt, specification lim-
its are often based on a job-mix formula (JMF) established as part of the
mixture design. The mix design will produce a JMF that establishes the tar-
get values for production of the HMA for the project. The JMF is the com-
bination of aggregate and asphalt binder materials proposed for use on a
project that, when tested using the required mix design procedures, yields
results that meet all the established design criteria. At a minimum, the JMF
should include the following target parameters from the mix design:

° Asphalt content

° Aggregate gradation

° Laboratory air voids

° Stockpile blend percentages

° Hot-bin blend percentages (for batch plants)

° Other parameters specified for quality-control such as mixture volu-

metrics or aggregate angularity

* Specific materials used (that is, sources for asphalt cement, admixtures,

ageregate descriptions, etc.)

The JMF that results from the mix design becomes the “recipe” for
producing that mixture in the hot plant. Specifications often dictate how
closely the HMA supplier must adhere to this recipe.

The specifications may require, for example, that the binder content of
the material produced shall not vary by more than 0.4 percent from the
binder content in the JMF (established in the approved mix design). The
concept is that the mix design provides evidence that a mixture made
with given aggregates, at a certain binder content and gradation, and com-
pacted with a specific amount of energy will comply with specific quality
indicator tests. These indicators can include parameters such as aggregate
absorption, tensile strength ratio (TSR), volumetric properties, and strength
parameters such as Marshall stability. The premise is that if the mixture is
controlled to be reasonably compliant with the fundamental mix-design
properties (that is, gradation, binder content, and air voids), then the mix-
ture produced will essentially comply with the quality properties (TSR,
Stability, and VMA) established in the mix design. This assumption allows
fewer tests for quality-control and acceptance than were required in the
mix design. The assumption also requires a revised mix design if any of
the quality-control or acceptance parameters change significantly.

More information on mix designs and their procedures can be found in
Chapter 4, Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design or in Asphalt Institute’s publica-
tions MS-2, Mix Design Methods for HMA and Other Hot-Mix Types or SP-2,
Superpave Mix Design.

10.2 Sampling Methods

The methods used for sampling HMA can have a significant effect on
the outcome of the test results. It is critical that good sampling methods
are used and that the specified procedures are carefully followed to get an
accurate representation of the materials being produced.

10.2.1 Representative Sampling

The primary goal is to obtain samples that are representative of the
products being evaluated. Since test results are used to adjust asphalt
mixing plants, it would be counterproductive to overall quality to obtain
a sample from a particularly “bad” or a particularly “good” portion of the
work. Consider as an example an asphalt plant quality-control sample
taken from a nonrepresentative portion of the asphalt mixture with an
unusually low binder content. The proper response from the plant opera-
tor to a low asphalt content test result would likely be to increase the
amount of asphalt binder being incorporated into the mix. After such an
adjustment, he would inadvertently have the asphalt plant set too high for
asphalt binder content.

Representative samples of the average production are critical for the
effective use of quality-control and acceptance samples. Inspection is
more appropriate for identifying non-uniform portions of the work. Once
inspection has identified a portion of the work that is non-uniform (dif-
ferent from the rest of the work), or for which defective materials are sus-
pected, additional samples representing that specific element of the work
should be taken. Test results from such samples should never be used as
part of the lot averages, and certainly not for lot acceptance. They only
represent the specific problem areas identified by inspection.

As an example, consider an asphalt pavement that contains a 50-foot
(15.2 meters) length of roadway that is littered with manholes and valve
covers that do not allow the regular compaction equipment into that iso-
lated area. By visually observing the entire pavement, an inspector or
technician would be able to recognize that the isolated area will not have
density values that are representative of the pavement compaction for that
day’s placement. Density tests taken there would represent that area only.

Regardless of the methods being employed, good samples will be
obtained if a common-sense approach is combined with proper sampling
technique. Visual observations of the entire element to be sampled can
be valuable in selecting sample locations or number of sample collection
points, and in verifying that representative samples can be retrieved.

It is also important to get a sufficient quantity of material when
sampling. The sample size is important not only to obtain enough mate-
rial to complete the tests but also to achieve a representative sample. As
an exaggerated example, consider a graded gravel material that contains
15 percent of rock that is retained on the #4-inch (19 millimeters) screen
and passes the l-inch (25 millimeters) screen. Each of these +%-inch
(19 millimeters) rocks weigh about 250 grams. Assume that a sample size
of only 500 grams is obtained. If the sample taken happens to contain
one large rock, then the gradation test will yield 50 percent retained on
the 34-inch (19 millimeters) sieve. If the 500-gram sample scoop does
not happen to capture a large rock, then the gradation test would yield
0 percent of that size. In reality, the gradation is somewhere in between
0 percent and 50 percent. As the sample size increases, the test result
is no longer impacted by the size of the sample—it is only impacted by
the true gradation of the materials being tested. This is the point that
establishes minimum sample sizes. Typical minimum sample sizes for
aggregates and for HMA are presented in Table 10.2.

1. Time intervals. Typical lot sizes when sampling by time interval

10.2.2 Random Sampling

Use of random sampling techniques is an important part of obtaining un-
biased representative samples. Random sampling methods can be simple to
use if good forms and/or other tools are used. With widespread availabil-
ity of personal computers, simple spreadsheet documents can be used to
easily generate sample locations and make implementation less daunting.

Random sampling can be applied to any of the sampling interval methods
(time, quantity, and length). Random sampling is achieved by taking the
number of samples to be taken that day and distributing the times, quantities,
or locations selected for each sample in a random way. For example, assume
that the production of 5,000 tons (4,536 tonnes) of material is planned and
a sample frequency for every 1,000 tons (907 tonnes) has been selected.
Nonrandom techniques may lead planners to take the first sample at
about 1,000 tons (907 tonnes) and the second sample at about 2,000 tons
(1,814 tonnes) and so on until all five of the samples for that day are
collected. Using random sampling, a random number (numbers between
0 and 1 obtained from a chart) would be assigned to each of the five
samples. Each of the random numbers is multiplied by the total ton-
nage (5,000 or 4,536) to determine at what point in production to take
the sample. For example, if the random number for one of the samples
is 0.43218, then that sample would be taken when the day’s accumula-
tive tonnage reaches 2,160 tons or 1,959 tons (5,000 X 0.43218 = 2,160)
(4,536 = 0.43218 X 1,960).

An accurate estimate of the day’s production is needed to determine
random sample locations. In some cases, lots with different sizes may have
a different number of tests. For example, if the project estimate for produc-
tion is 6,520 feet (1,978 meters), and the quality-control plan establishes
the sample frequency for coring at every 1,000 feet (305 meters), then that
lot will contain seven core locations.

Conveyor Belt Sampling

Conveyor belt sampling techniques are generally used to obtain aggre-
gate samples (Figure 10.3). They can be used to sample individual aggregate
materials in production, as they are being transported to a stockpile, or
combined aggregates that have been blended and are being transported to
the dryer of a hot plant. They generally provide good samples if the belt
is properly cut and cleaned, but the procedures are inconvenient because
the belt conveyor must be stopped to retrieve the sample.

10.2.5 Asphalt Binder Sampling

Asphalt binder can be sampled from delivery truck tanks, from the plant’s
asphalt storage tank, or from delivery lines from the tank to the asphalt
mixing plant. Samples from the delivery trucks are more representative of
the binder delivered by the supplier, but samples from the storage tank or
the delivery lines to the hot plant are more representative of the binder that
is incorporated into the mix. The procedure involves opening a valve that is
usually located near the bottom of the tank. Sample containers must be new,
clean, and dry. At least one quart of asphalt should be allowed to flow out
of the valve and discarded before filling the sample container. It should be
sealed immediately after sampling, wiped with a clean dry cloth, and properly
marked to identify the material for shipping to the laboratory.

Appropriate personal protective equipment should be worn around the
hot asphalt. Generally, representatives of the asphalt supplier or HMA pro-
ducer will obtain the samples because of safety and insurance concerns.
The quality-control or acceptance technician then takes immediate posses-
sion of the sample.

Procedures identified in AASHTO T 40 or ASTM D 140 should be followed
when sampling asphalt binder.

10.3 Testing Methods

The ultimate goal of HMA testing is to accurately measure the appropriate
quality of the materials used. Laboratories must strive to reduce the varia-
tions caused by testing so that test results can be confidently used to evalu-
ate the materials and production methods. However, all parties involved in
quality-control and the acceptance testing process must understand the vari-
ability of test results that comes from the testing methods themselves. All
parties must recognize that the variability related to the test method can be
reduced but never eliminated. This is especially important for test parameters
that will be used for pay factor determination, rejection or acceptance of the
materials produced.

The test methods used will be established in the quality-assurance plan
and the project specifications. Agencies or owners may change or modify
the standard test procedures. It is important that the laboratories get a
clear understanding of the methods to be used before starting the project.
Agencies modify standard methods to reduce variation from one lab to
another. ASTM D 979, Standard Practice for Sampling Bituminous Paving
Mixtures, for example, covers sampling of HMA at points of manufacture,
storage, delivery, or in place. A variety of sampling methods are included
within this one standard, so an agency’s project specifications might stipu-
late which method is required. Many agencies hold pre-paving meetings
for projects. These meetings are a good forum for clarifying the methods
and the roles of the parties involved in quality-control and. acceptance
testing.

The following sections describe testing usually provided for quality-
control and acceptance of HMA.

10.3.1 Aggregate Gradation

A tremendous amount of work goes into processing the aggregate mate-
rials to achieve a specific gradation in the finished product. The final gra-
dation can be impacted by processes in the mining, crushing, washing,
stockpiling, and handling, or in the blending of the aggregates. Quality-
control must be exercised throughout all processes in order to achieve the
final gradation.

Different sampling methods may be used in the various stages of process-
ing aggregates, but the test procedures are generally the same. An excep-
tion to this is the use of unwashed gradations for quality-control testing.
Washed gradations are more accurate and are generally used for accep-
tance tests; however, it is appropriate in some instances to use unwashed

gradations for quality-control purposes. Consider, for example, gradation
testing performed for quality-control after crushing. If the materials are
crushed in a dry state, and they are separated into stockpiles containing
certain gravel sizes, it may be appropriate to test these gravels without
washing. A visual observation can verify that little dust remains adhered
to the aggregate particles, that the gradation will not likely be affected by
more than one percent, and that the quality-control manager can estimate
that the percentage passing the No. 200 screen would be slightly higher.
Quality-control testing used to evaluate a washing operation’s effective-
ness at removing fines, on the other hand, may require washed gradations.
It is important to remember that this is not the acceptance point. The
aggregates still have to be blended and further processed before being
incorporated into the HMA mixture, and the quality-control manager must
also estimate aggregate degradation during this processing. One can see
that the added accuracy gained by washing gravel materials may not bene-
fit controlling the crushing operation. Using non-washed gradations could
actually provide more benefit because it gives more timely results and
might allow the technician to provide a higher test frequency.

Test procedures for aggregate gradation can be found in AASHTO T 27
and T 11 or ASTM C 117 and C 136.

10.3.2 Fractured Faces and Aggregate
Angularity

Aggregate angularity has long been known to affect the void proper-
ties and compactability of HMA mixtures. Traditionally, aggregate angu-
larity is tested by physically separating the aggregate particles in a given
test sample containing fractured surfaces from those containing no frac-
tured surfaces. Then the two masses are weighed and the percentage of
the sample containing fractures is determined as a percentage by weight.
Typical ranges of fractured-faces specifications are between 75 percent
and 100 percent. Some specifications require a certain percentage of
particles with two or more fractures. The fracture faces test can be per-
formed on coarse and fine aggregate, but the fine aggregate testing can
be very tedious and is prone to technician error. There is also a practical
limit to the smallest particle sizes that can be evaluated.

The difficulty of testing the fractured faces of fine aggregates, combined
with the knowledge that the fine-aggregate texture affects the volumetrics
of mixtures more than the coarse aggregate texture, led to the develop-
ment of the uncompacted voids test. In this test, the fine aggregate is
poured through a standard funnel into a standard cup. The cup is gently
struck off and the weight recorded. One can imagine that a highly angular
aggregate would bulk up in the cup and render a lighter weight, whereas
a rounded aggregate would pack into the cup and render a heavier weight.
The aggregate specific gravity must be known for the aggregates tested,

10.3.3 Asphalt Content

In the past, testing for asphalt content was done with chlorinated indus-
trial solvents such as trichloroethylene or methylene chloride to wash the
asphalt out of an HMA sample. This provided an asphalt cement content
and left the washed aggregate for gradation testing. Because of the high
cost of purchasing and disposal, and the human health risks to those han-
dling the solvents, the industry is increasingly turning to different methods
of testing asphalt content.

One method that has been developed is the nuclear asphalt content
gauge. These gauges have a radioactive source and backscatter detectors
that are sensitive to the presence of hydrogen ions. Asphalt binder con-
tains a significant number of hydrogen ions and therefore the gauges can
be calibrated to provide asphalt content measurements. These gauges are
currently used by some agencies as a replacement for solvent extractions.
They also have an added benefit for quality-control purposes in that they
provide results within minutes of sampling. In order to own and oper-
ate these gauges, however, users must have radiation safety licenses with
their respective regulatory agencies and the operators must be trained and
certified to use the equipment. Recent changes to licensing rules in the
United States have added additional security requirements to the already
stringent radiation safety rules.

Another recent development for testing asphalt content is the ignition
method. This method uses a large muffle furnace to heat the asphalt mix-
ture specimen to temperatures exceeding 1000°F (538°C); at these temper-
atures, the asphalt cement is completely burned up and all that remains
is a light ash. The ash can be easily washed from the remaining aggre-
gate and the aggregate tested for gradation. This method also requires
calibration because some of the rock particles can burn at the elevated
temperatures. Therefore, calibration factors for asphalt cement content
and for gradation must be applied. For most aggregates, the calibration
factors are small and manageable, but certain aggregates, especially those
containing dolomites and limestone, require special handling and modifi-
cations to the calibration and testing methods.

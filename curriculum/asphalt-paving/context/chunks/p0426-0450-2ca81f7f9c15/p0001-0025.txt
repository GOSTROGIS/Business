rough base. The wheelbase of the paver develops what is called the “line
of pull,” which can be thought of as the vertical center of gravity of the
paver traced longitudinally along the path of the paver. Vertically lifting only
the front or the back of a paver moves the center of gravity of the paver
up approximately one-half the distance lifted. When the tow point of the
screed is located near this central fulcrum point, this averaging effect makes
proportionally smaller changes in the height of the tow point and explains
how the paver’s wheel base is used to level uneven surfaces. The small front
wheels or bogies under the hopper that can move across a depression or a
ridge will only reflect a small amount of vertical movement at the tow point.
When the drive tires in the rear pass over this same point, the vertical move-
ment is also reduced. Track pavers also provide this leveling effect. They
have a series of small bogie wheels that are free to move up and down inde-
pendent of one another as they run inside of the flexible continuous track.

the paver manufacturers, but overall they are about 10 feet (3 meters) in

Distance for Screed Change to Occur

When adjusted properly, the forces acting on a free-floating screed are
balanced and it will lay a smooth, high quality HMA pavement over a
rough base. As previously discussed, when the forces acting on the screed
become unbalanced, the depth of the HMA pavement changes gradually
as the paver moves forward and the screed again seeks equilibrium with
the forces acting upon it. The distance the paver must travel to reach
this equilibrium is generally 4 to 5 times the length of the side arms of
the paver (see Figure 8.23). With the typical side arm length of 10 feet
(3 meters), the paver will move 40 to 50 feet (12 to 15 meters) before the
screed is again in equilibrium. This gradual transition over an extended
length should eliminate the need for the screed operator to crank in large
adjustments on the manual depth-screw mechanisms. When a paver is
properly set up and paving continuously without stopping, the screed will
remain in balance and the depth screws on the screed will not need man-
ual adjustments. When paving with automatic grade controls and when a
depth change is needed, the change can be “dialed in” by changing the
depth setting in the grade control box.

Establishing the Proper Paving Speed

Planning is necessary for achieving a high quality asphalt pavement.
Planning begins with establishing the amount of material to be produced,
the number of trucks needed, the hauling distance, and the amount of
HMA that can be placed and compacted before the temperature falls
below compaction levels. The most common paver operator error is exces-
sive paver speed. The paver operator must monitor the arrival rate of the
material to the job site, the flow of material through the paver, and the
pace of the compaction train. Paver speed should be closely monitored to
match the consumption rate of material through the paver with the HMA
production and delivery.

Calculating Paver Yield

During HMA placement, the paving supervisor and/or the inspector
should periodically check the yield of the paving operation. The purpose
is to cross-check the thickness measurements to ensure that the HMA is
being placed at the proper average thickness. Some agencies use paver
yield (or spread rate) as an acceptance criteria in lieu of thickness. This
check can be made on each truckload of HMA, but adjustments to the
thickness should not be made that often. A longer distance (1,000 feet; 300
meters) may be more appropriate.

The first step in calculating paver yield is to determine the post-
compaction unit weight (pounds per square yard; kilograms per square
meter) of the HMA. This can be determined from the bulk-specific grav-
ity from previous cores or from nuclear density measurements. The post-
compaction unit weight of the HMA and the mat’s thickness, width, and
length can be used to calculate the yield. The following is an example
problem and its solution:

the project. This HMA covered 3,500 feet (1,066.9 meters) of pavement.
The paver is placing the HMA 12 feet (3.66 meters) wide and 1% inches
(38.1 millimeters) thick (compacted). The HMA has a unit weight of 151.5
pounds per cubic foot (2,426.8 kilograms per cubic meter).

8.4.6 Fumes Control

Fumes control systems are designed to draw the excess heat and fumes
generated in the auger area away from the workers and exhaust it above
their heads or draw it into the intake manifold of the paver’s engine.
Baffles, ductwork, and fans are used to capture the excess heat and fumes
and move them up and out of the work area. Eighty-five percent of fumes
generated in the auger area are captured and exhausted out of the work
area. Maintaining this level of control demands that the fumes control sys-
tem be properly maintained and operated.

8.4.7 Observation of the HMA Pavement
Behind the Screed

The asphalt in the head of material in the auger area must flow without
hesitation under the screed into the HMA pavement being laid. Any cooler or
slightly compacted chunk of asphalt that finds its way into this area must be
broken up before it passes under the screed. This breaking-up process disrupts
the normal flow of HMA under the screed and results in a dragging or tearing
of the HMA pavement surface. Identifying and eliminating the source of the
chunks is the only solution to eliminating these HMA pavement blemishes.
Segregation is another HMA pavement fault revealed by observation. Longi-
tudinal streaks of segregation usually indicate an auger-feeding problem. An
accumulation of segregated material in and around the center auger drive box
can result in a streak of segregated material 6 to 8 inches (150 to 200 millime-
ters) wide appearing in the longitudinal center mat. Readjusting the volume of
HMA in this area or slightly raising the augers can improve or minimize these
segregated streaks. Excessive material pulled under the paver can cause similar
segregated streaks on the surface or on the bottom of the mat. Properly setting
the material sensors and reducing the volume of the HMA in the auger area
can reduce the amount of material pulled under the belly of the paver.

Roughness

Roughness is difficult to troubleshoot. The type of roughness must be
determined in order to find a solution. Examples of roughness include
short or long swells, ripples, load-to-load swells, or random roughness.

Short swells or ridges, sometimes referred to as ripples, are usually
the result of the screed’s angle of attack changing on its own during
the paving operation. If the screed is worn or otherwise shows signs of
being loose at its connections to the side arms, it can allow the angle
of attack to change uncontrollably and cause these ripples. The screed-
depth screws should be checked for looseness—there should be no more
than one-half turn of free rotary motion when the screed is resting on a
flat surface and both depth screws are in the null position (zero angle
of attack). The bearings, fasteners, and screed pivot pins should also be
checked for looseness. Corrections should be made to eliminate angle-
of-attack changes that are not introduced by the automatic grade and
slope or by the manual adjustment of the depth screws.

Swells that are longer than the length of the paver can usually be
attributed to a change in the volume of the head of material. If the head of
material in front of the screed is allowed to vary by more than £1 inch (25
millimeters), this type of roughness can appear. Truck exchanges should
be watched carefully. The time it takes one truck to exit the paver hopper
and the next one to get into the discharge position can be long enough
that the flow of material going to the auger area can be interrupted. This
happens because the head of material is not being replenished, allowing
the screed to drop. If this happens, roughness will result with each truck
exchange. This type of roughness is also known as long swells. The solu-
tion is to train the truck driver and the paver operator how to exchange
trucks without varying the head of material. If everyone works together
and communication between paver operator, the dump operator (if used),
and the truck driver is good, it can be done. The screed operator should
monitor the head of material and let everyone know if the volume var-
ies more than +1 inch (25 millimeters). Long swells can also occur if the
stringline being used as a grade reference is allowed to sag between sup-
ports or the grade sensors get out of adjustment. Constantly checking the
tension in the stringline and monitoring the referencing system being used
will help eliminate these issues.

Random roughness has no pattern and its cause can be difficult to iden-
tify. The first step is to mark the rough areas in the HMA pavement and
look for some type of pattern. Material being spilled on the grade and left
in piles might be one cause. This HMA can segregate and cool enough to
cause the screed to rise as it paves over the spilled material. Any changes
in the speed of the paver should be noted. If the paver speeds up when
material is available and slows down when it is not, roughness in the HMA
pavement can result. Reflective roughness should always be a concern
while paving over a poor base. A yielding subgrade can result in random
roughness during compaction.

8.5 Joint Construction

3.5.1 Transverse or Construction Joints

Good transverse construction joints, like all good HMA joints, are straight,
with a vertical face on the cold side of the joint. The cold face of the joint
should be vertical. There are several ways to establish and maintain the
vertical face on the cold side while traffic is rolling off the edge. The easiest
and most economical method is to build a wedge of sacrificial material that
ramps down to the underlining surface and stays in place until the paving
operation is resumed, at which time the material in the wedge is removed.
There are three methods of properly building the sacrificial wedge:

° In the most widely used method, the paver is stopped and the screed
is lifted over the head of material in the auger area. The excess mate-
rial, which is to be used to build the wedge, is then separated by
shoveling a trench in the hot material just downstream from the joint.
The side of the joint that remains in place should be pushed back
with a lute and a sharp vertical edge built. A bond-breaking material
(plastic, Kraft paper, or sand) should be used to line the bottom of the
wedge area and cover the vertical face (see Figure 8.24). After the area
is lined with a bond breaker, the excess HMA can be moved onto the

When paving resumes, the cold mat must be cut back at a point where
full mat thickness exists. This cutback process is done with a power
saw equipped with either a dry-cut diamond or abrasive-blade, or by
running a cold-milling machine transversely across the pavement.

The following precautions apply when constructing a transverse joint:

° Wet diamond sawing should not be used because a slurry is pro-
duced, leaving a dust film on the joint’s vertical face and staining the
pavement.

° Cold milling in the longitudinal direction to remove the wedge should
not be allowed because the cutting mandrel cannot produce a vertical
face.

° Bond breakers and wooden blockouts should not be used on airfield
pavements opened to aircraft traffic.

To resume paving from a transverse joint, the wedge and any bond breaker
material must be removed and properly disposed. If the bond breaker
worked well, this should not be a difficult task. Once the wedge is removed,
the area must be clean and dry before tack is applied to both the vertical
face of the joint and the surface to be paved (see Figure 8.25). The tack on
the vertical face will allow the hot side of the joint to adhere to the cold side
and seal out moisture and contaminants that could cause the joint to ravel.

When constructing a transverse joint, to both stop and resume paving, it
is good practice to use a 10-foot (-meter) straightedge to check the pave-
ment leading into and away from the joint for smoothness before compaction
begins. If this check is done while the material is still hot, corrections can be
made by adding or removing material to achieve acceptable mat smoothness.

If the temporary wedge is going to be used by traffic for only a short
time (overnight), the application of the tack coat for the wedge can be
eliminated, which will greatly facilitate removal.

8.5.4 Notched-Wedge Joints

The notched-wedge joint was developed to help prevent premature
failures of longitudinal joints. The notched wedge (see Figure 8.27) can
improve the joint structure over the vertical edge joint by increasing the
bond surface area between the two mats. The initial pass is laid with a
1-foot (300-millimeter) taper with a built-in 4- to 1-inch (19-25 millimeter)
notch or vertical face at the end of the taper. The notch prevents the frac-
turing of aggregate when the joint is rolled by preventing the wedge from
being tapered down to zero.

Another plus for the notched wedge is traffic safety after the first pass
is laid. The notched wedge provides a ramp for traffic to get safely from
one side of the joint to the other. Because of this safety feature, paving
can continue the entire day on one side of the pavement and move back
the next day to pull the lanes even, instead of moving back at mid-day.
The screed extension that lays the notched wedge is available from several
manufacturers whose operational procedures must be followed to pro-
duce a sound, durable longitudinal joint.

Related Paving Operations

8.6.1 Hand-Spreading Operations

When the major portion of a project will be placed manually without the
aid of a paving machine, mixes should be designed for good workability.
A good mix design technician has several options for making mixes that
are easier to place manually.

Placing and spreading by hand should be done very carefully, with the
material distributed uniformly to avoid segregation of the coarse aggre-
gate. When the HMA is dumped in piles, it should be placed in a man-
ner where the shovelers have to move the least amount of material the
least distance. Sufficient space should be available so that workers are not
required to stand in the HMA.

HMA should not be broadcast or spread from shovels because segregation
will result. Rather, it should be deposited from shovels into small piles and
spread with lutes. Any part of the HMA that has formed into lumps and does
not break down easily when struck with a shovel edge should be discarded.
After the material has been placed, and before rolling starts, the surface should
be checked with straightedges, and all irregularities should be corrected. On
cool days, careful attention must be paid to the HMA temperature. Rolling
should begin as soon as possible to ensure adequate compaction.

8.6.2 Supplemental Operations and
Appurtenances

Items such as roadway widening, shoulders, superelevated curves,
curbs, ditches and revetments are considered supplemental paving opera-
tions. In some cases, such as roadway widening and shoulder construc-
tion, specially designed pieces of equipment and attachments allow the
work to be done easily and efficiently. Certain roadway appurtenances,
such as asphalt curbs, dikes, ditches, spillways, and slopes, are increas-
ingly becoming part of paving contracts. For these jobs, special paving
equipment or special HMA mixtures (or both) may be required.

Roadway Widening and Shoulders

Two-lane pavements today should be 24~28 feet (7.3-7.9 meters) wide.
Many thousands of miles of primary routes are now inadequate in width
and thickness. Narrow roads should be widened before they are strength-
ened with overlays. Skillful planning can greatly improve the original
alignment. One technique is to place most or all widening at one side or
the other around curves. For example, sharp curves should be widened

When paved shoulders are part of the initial construction of a roadway,
procedures for paving travel lanes should be followed.

Superelevation

Paving curve sections with superelevated cross slopes on new construc-
tion does not generally pose a problem because the prepared foundation
material is usually superelevated or sloped the same as the new pavement.
This allows the same uniform thickness that is being placed on the tangent
or straight sections to be placed on the curved or superelevated sections.
As the paver proceeds through the transition to the superelevated section
and out again, the paver and the screed tilt accordingly on the prepared
foundation, and little if any adjustment in the screed controls is required.

If the superelevation is not built into the prepared base or subgrade, then it
must be built into the HMA pavement with variable thickness layers. Today’s
pavers are equipped with automatic slope controls, allowing the transverse
cross slope to be dialed in as the paver moves through the superelevated
curve. Sensors riding on an erected or traveling stringline maintain grade
control for the end of the screed nearest the sensor. The depth at the other
end of the screed depends on the transverse slope setting. When the paver
reaches the point where the superelevated slope begins to change, stakes or
other markers are placed on adjacent lanes with the required cross-slopes
settings so that the change is slowly dialed in as the paver proceeds through
the transition. The change in slope is gradual, and small errors in dialing do
not affect the riding quality of the pavement. Once the paver enters the full
superelevated cross slope, the slope setting will remain the same through-
out. Then, as the paver exits the fully superelevated section, the cross slope
is gradually dialed out using the same procedure, but in reverse.

Asphalt Curbs

Asphalt curbs (dikes) are accessories to new road and street paving.
On dual-lane turnpikes and interstate highways, asphalt curbs are often
constructed at the outer edge of the paved shoulders on elevated grades
to prevent slope erosion.

Asphalt Ditches and Spillways

Ditches lie parallel to the highway at the foot of large cut sections or
between dual-lane pavements having a depressed median strip. In many
locations in rolling terrain, a hard impervious surface is required to prevent
costly erosion. In deep-cut sections, it is often desired to construct ditches
over the tops of slopes and around the ends to prevent slope erosion. Spill-
ways to carry off water collected by curbs or dikes are subject to high water
velocities and may need to be paved. In all cases, such channels should be
sufficiently wide and deep so that the heaviest anticipated runoff is carried
away smoothly. In some instances, very large outfall ditches may be neces-
sary to carry runoff completely away from the right-of-way.

HMA makes an excellent ditch or spillway-lining material. As a rule,
highway-surface course HMA need only be modified by increasing the
binder and mineral filler contents as necessary to ensure a watertight and
durable lining. The resulting lower stability is not a concern; it actually adds
to the capacity of the lining to adjust to minor settlement without cracking.

Special slip-form paving machines are available for placing at least part of
the ditch linings. Spreader boxes, paving machines, and even hand-placing pro-
cedures may be used. Linings are made as thick as necessary for the use and
stresses involved, but practical experience indicates that a finished thickness of
1% inches (40 millimeters) of HMA should be the minimum thickness specified.
Compaction of HMA in ditches and spillways is accomplished by hand tamps,
weighted slip forms, small vibrating plate compactors, or small rollers.

Roadway Shoulder Rumblie Strips

Rumble strips were first used on New Jersey’s Garden State Parkway
in 1955. They provide a warning for drivers when their vehicle begins to
run off the road. Rumble strips are continuous bands of raised material or
indentations that are formed or grooved in the shoulders. They alert driv-
ers by transmitting sound and vibration through the vehicle.

The two most widely used methods for installing rumble strips in an
asphalt pavement are milled-in or rolled-in.

Rolled-in rumble strips are created by using a steel-wheel roller (see
Figure 8.30) to which half-sections of metal pipe or solid-steel bars are
welded. The compaction equipment presses the shape of the bar into the
HMA shoulder surface. The indentation is about 1 inch (25 millimeters)
deep, 2 to 2.5 inches (50 to 63 millimeters) wide and 18 to 35 inches (450 to
900 millimeters) long, transverse to the travel direction. The indentations are
usually set 8 inches (200 millimeters) on center and offset from the travel
lane edge by 6 to 12 inches (150 to 300 millimeters). See Figure 8.31 for a
picture of rolled-in rumble strips.

9.1 Introduction

Compaction is the process used to densify, or reduce the volume of, a
mass Of material. For hot mix asphalt (HMA), compaction locks aggregate
particles together to provide stability and resistance to deformation while
simultaneously reducing the permeability of the mixture and enhancing
its durability.

This chapter will discuss the mechanics of compaction, factors affecting
compaction, compaction equipment, rolling procedures, and troubleshoot-
ing compaction problems.

7.2.0 Virgin Aggregate and RAP
Cold-Feed Bins

Feed bins for virgin aggregate (see Figure 7.6) and RAP (see Figure 7.7)
are similar for both batch and drum-mix plants.

While vibratory pan feeders and plate-style feeders were used in the
past, virtually all plants, both batch and drum/continuous, now use con-
veyors with variable-speed motors located below feed bins to regulate the
flow of materials to the drying drum.

Variable-speed belt feeds are consistent and repeatable, and therefore
easy to calibrate. The ton-per-hour output can be determined at different
belt speeds, and then the required speed setting can be calculated for a
given recipe and given production rate. The feed-bin calibration process
is the act of measuring and recording output at different speed ranges for
a specific gate opening. This process is usually computerized on today’s
modern plants. Gate openings should be proportional to the amount of
ageregate being added to the plant to ensure uniform loading of the belt.

racy should be checked on a regular basis, regardless of requirements.

7.2.5 Aggregate Dryer

An aggregate dryer is needed for both batch and drum-mixer plants.

With some styles of drum-mixers, the dryer is very long because it is also
used as a mixing drum to mix the liquid asphalt binder with the aggre-
gate to produce the final mix. Some drum-mixers are two piece units—the
dryer and the mixing device are separate units.

It is the responsibility of the dryer to produce mix with a consistent
temperature. Wide variations in temperature in the final mix affect place-
ment technique and in-place density. A temperature swing greater than
10°-20°F (6°-11°C) typically affects field performance. Aggregate moisture
that varies widely in the stockpiles can make meeting this directive diffi-
cult for the plant operator. This is one reason why managing the stockpile
area for moisture control is so thoroughly discussed.

Aggregate dryers and drum-mixers come in both counterflow configu-
rations (aggregate and hot gases traveling in opposing directions) and
parallel-flow configurations (aggregate and hot gases traveling in the same
direction). Batch plants have counterflow dryers. The newer drum-mixers
discussed in this chapter also utilize counterflow dryers.

Dryers have a burner and fan fitted at one end to create a hot gas stream
that dries and heats the aggregate. Burner and dust control equipment
allow dryers with fossil-fuel burners to function very effectively from both
a particulate- and gaseous-emission standpoint.

Dryers are inclined and rotate. They function by picking up and drop-
ping aggregate material into the hot gas stream. Inside the dryer are
steel flights, or lifters. These flights pick up the aggregate material and
drop it through the hot gas stream as the dryer rotates (see Figure 7.9).
To prevent incomplete combustion and hydrocarbon contamination, the
aggregate material never passes through the burner flame. Special burner
flighting is installed in the dryer for the length of the flame. These special
flights pick up the aggregate, trap it against the outside wall of the dryer,
and carry the material over top of the flame. Gravity causes the material
to advance slightly with each rotation because the dryer is installed on
a slight incline. Retention time typically is three to five minutes but can
be longer, depending on the dryer’s size, length, and flight configura-
tion. Retention time can be lengthened for extremely absorptive, wet, or

otherwise difficult-to-dry material by installing dams in the dryer between
the rows of flights or by changing the angle of incline of the dryer if the
unit is adjustable.

Flights can be configured for different material and local conditions.
Small aggregate particles heat quickly and easily, although they are typi-
cally wetter and require a higher burner position and exhaust fan damper
position to remove the additional water from the rock. Coarse mixes
with large quantities of stone have more mass for each particle and often
require a more aggressive flight pattern for maximum heat transfer into
the stone. The equipment manufacturer can provide advice for adjusting
flight configurations.

Flights also wear as they dig through the aggregate material on the bot-
tom of the dryer. As their leading edges wear down, drying efficiency is
reduced. If moisture is retained in the mix, quality and placement will be
compromised and the long-term serviceable life of the pavement may be
affected.

Moisture retention in the mix can be monitored simply by weighing
produced mix, drying it, and then weighing it again. Any weight loss can
be attributed to moisture in the aggregate material. Reducing the feed rate
will usually solve the problem.

Elevated exit gas temperature is another indication that the flights are
either worn or that the configuration is wrong for local conditions. Flights
can easily be replaced if they are worn. The pattern of the flight can also
be changed for more drying efficiency.

Another indication that the dryer is functioning inefficiently for project
conditions is a significant drop in material temperature after production. If
the aggregate in a batch plant—or the mix in a drum-mixer plant—loses
temperature significantly between the time it leaves the dryer and is loaded
into the truck during normal production Gmore than 25°-30°F/14°-17°C),
the aggregate is either not completely dry or not heated all the way through.
Mixes with a high percentage of large aggregate often require elevated
dryer exit temperatures to compensate for the longer time it takes for the
colder center of the stone to heat-stabilize, but a temperature drop might
also indicate that the stone is not completely dry. When significant tem-
perature drops are encountered, moisture retention tests are needed. If no
moisture is present, the uncoated stone can be overheated to compensate
for the eventual temperature drop.

Drying effectiveness should be monitored during production and over
the life of the equipment. The flights in the dryer are subject to wear
because they constantly dig through and handle aggregate materials. These
flights will wear and must be maintained. Their maintenance is part of the
inspection/monitoring process for plant operating efficiency. Recording
the surface temperature taken with an infrared temperature gun along
the length of the dryer shell is an excellent way to monitor the flighting
arrangement and drying efficiency. By plotting the temperature readings
at 2-foot (0.7-meter) intervals, hot and cold spots along the length of the

7.2.6 Dust Collection Equipment

The gas velocity in an aggregate dryer is quite significant and will
remove fine aggregate fractions from the dried aggregate mixture. Dust
collectors are installed on hot mix plants primarily to collect the dust for
environmental reasons, but captured dust is often reused, depending on
the type of equipment installed on the plant.

There are two stages of dust collection on plant systems—primary
collectors and secondary collectors. Primary collectors gather the large
dust/fine aggregate particles. Secondary collectors gather the smaller
particles.

Most primary collectors are either the knockout-box type or the cyclone
type. Figures 7.10 and 7.11 show these types of collectors installed on
plant systems. The fines collected in primary collectors are returned 100
percent to the plant process, without any contemplation of partial return.
Most people consider this dust to be sand-size particles, but in reality they
also have a fairly high percentage of material passing the 200-mesh or
75-micron sieve. The same style of primary collectors are found on both
batch and drum-mix facilities.

Secondary dust collectors fall into two categories—wet washers (or wet
scrubbers) and baghouses (or fabric filter collectors). This equipment is
positioned immediately upstream of the exhaust fan and is connected to
the fan with ductwork. Figures 7.11 and 7.12 show these types of systems
installed on hot mix plants.

Wet washers clean the dust from the gas stream by injecting additional
water into the system. This makes the dust particles heavier, allowing
them to fall out of the gas stream. Collected dust is very wet—basically a
wet sludge—and so cannot be returned for use in the mix process. This
effluent is pumped to a settling pond where the dust settles to the bottom.
The particle-free water is then recycled from the surface of the pond and
reused for dust collection.

A baghouse cleans with a dry process, allowing the gas to pass through
a fabric filter supported by a wire cage. Dust is collected on the outside
of the fabric filter while the dryer is running, and cleaning air periodically
passes through the bag from the opposite direction, causing the collected
dust to fall into the hopper of the baghouse. This collected dust can either
be returned to the mix-production process or removed from the system.
Figure 7.13 shows the internal components of a baghouse.

When dust is returned to the plant process, certain operational practices
are important in order to keep the dust return consistent.

7.2./ Dust-Return Equipment

Most plants are fitted with fabric filter collectors, allowing operating
personnel to return the captured dust to the mix-production process. As
mentioned above, fines collected from primary-type collectors are returned
100 percent to the process without any contemplation of partial return
(see Figure 7.14).

Dust from a baghouse secondary collector, however, can be returned
entirely or in part, depending on the requirements of the job-mix formula.
Fines return is accomplished mechanically with screw conveyors or pneu-
matically with compressed air or blower systems.

The general practice is to design mixes so all the fines collected in the
baghouse are reused in the mix or so all the fines are wasted/removed from
the process. This keeps the equipment options and operating principles
simple.

When 100 percent of the dust collected in the baghouse is to be reused,
the following operational best practices are important:

¢ The baghouse cleaning and fines return must stop when the dryer
stops. This keeps large quantities of dust from being transferred to
the mixing area of the drum or deposited in the fines bin on the batch
plant when the aggregate feed stops.

* Large and rapid increases in production rate should be avoided. It
takes time for the dust to be collected on the bags, cleaned off the
bags, and then returned to the system. Large jumps in production
rate can be immediately accomplished by resetting the cold feed bins,
but it takes time for the dust corresponding to the production-rate
increase to be collected on the bags and returned to the mix. The mix
produced during that time will be short of -200 mesh (75 micron)
material and high in voids. Large, rapid decreases in production have
the opposite effect. Most modern control systems ramp production up
and down in small intervals because of this.

¢ Fines-return equipment inspection should be done regularly. When
the fines-return equipment wears, it may only be able to return a
certain quantity of fines. Test results are characterized by a shortage
of fines and an increase in voids above a certain rate on the plant
system.

When partial dust return is required, several equipment options exist
for both batch plants and drum-mix plants. Most plant owners purchase a
storage silo for transferring the collected fines for reuse or for storage for
later use. If the silo fills to capacity, accumulated fines are usually trucked
off and wasted or used for other purposes, such as in the manufacture of
slurry seals. Figures 7.14 and 7.15 show this type of equipment.

Baghouse dust stored in silos can either be reintroduced into the process
volumetrically with a calibrated feeder valve (with output charted to the
speed of the feeder valve, as similarly done in the calibration process of
cold feed bins), or it can be weighed back into the process.

With a batch plant, dust can be returned with a calibrated feeder to the
hot aggregate elevator, be weighed back through the aggregate weigh
hopper as an additional hot bin draw, or be weighed in a separate scale
and then deposited into the mixer. All these options are common.

In a drum-mix plant, material can similarly be returned through a
calibrated valve, or a negative-weighing pod can be used to continu-
ously weigh dust back. Figure 7.16 shows this type of negative weigh pod
system.

Local specifications often determine which method of dust reentry is
desirable or required. The important point is that many options exist for
returning captured dust to the mix production process. Mix requirements
and local specifications dictate the required equipment options.

7.2.10 Fiber Additive Equipment

Fiber additives are specified for some hot mix formulas to help ensure
that a uniform film thickness is maintained between the aggregate par-
ticles and to reduce the possibility of draindown of the binder in the mix.
They are produced from both mineral fiber and cellulose fiber materials.

Fiber additives are popular with mixes that contain a high percentage
of large-size or same-size aggregate and a low percentage of fine aggre-
gates to fill the voids between these large aggregate particles—like Open-
Graded Friction Course (OGFC) and stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes.

Fibers are typically added pneumatically with a machine that shreds
and blows the fibers into the mixing area of the plant. This machine uses
a negative-weighing principle where the bundle of fibers deposited in the
machine is continuously weighed, and the declining weight is calculated
and displayed on the control system as it is separated and blown into the
plant.

In a drum-mixer application, this machine runs continuously and there
is a continuous weight display of the fiber additives. The flow rate of the
machine is typically tied into the plant master controls and the aggregate
belt scales.

In a batch-plant application, the machine starts and stops based on the
target weight of fibers for each batch. With a batch plant, fibers can also
be added by hand in bag form by dropping a pre-packaged bag of fibers
down a chute directly into the pugmill with each batch.

7.2.11 Finished Product Storage Silos and
Load-Out Equipment .

Finished product storage silos allow the plant to run continuously whether
or not trucks are present. Silos are required for drum-mix plants, because the
mix flows continuously from the mixing area when the plant is running and
must be elevated for dispensing into trucks. Silos on batch plants allow the
plant to run continuously, thereby increasing daily output.

Silos can be either the surge or the storage type. Surge silos are typically
not insulated and are often open-topped bins. This type of storage unit is
lighter and can be made portable so that it works well with portable plants.
Storage silos are fully enclosed and heavily insulated. They generally have
heated cones, and some have sidewall heat. Top and bottom seals are com-
mon on storage silos. Storage silos allow longer storage times for mixes,
without significant heat loss or oxidation to the mix. Storage silos are mas-
sive units requiring large foundations, making them semi-permanent installa-
tions. Regulatory agencies sometimes rate storage silos for storage periods or
limit maximum storage times. Allowable storage times are sometimes based
on the absorption characteristics of the aggregate, rather than on the features
of the plant equipment.

7.3.1 Aggregate Gradation Control with a
Batch Plant

Aggregate gradation is controlled in two places in a modern batch
plant—in the cold feed and in the screening unit/hot bins (see Figure
7.19). Changes in the past few years have complicated the gradation
control process in a batch plant.

Batch plants were originally designed to accept a crushed aggregate
product and a sand. The screens at the top of the batch tower were used
to separate the aggregate into fractions so that different mixes could be
constructed, some with larger rocks and a larger percentage of stone for
base or binder lifts and some with more sand and smaller stones for wear-
ing surfacees. When batch plants were first designed, purchasing presized
aggregates for hot mix production was not an option. The batch tower's
first job was to size the aggregates.

In today’s world, sized aggregates are readily available. Mixes can be
constructed with different percentages of sized aggregates at a ratio
blended right from the cold-feed bins. This is one reason why drum-mixers
came into being in the 1970s. It also eliminates some of the requirements
for a batch tower. In fact, it is not uncommon today to find five or six
different aggregates being blended at the cold bins to feed a batch plant.
With an overwhelming majority (over 95 percent) of today’s batch plants
having only four hot bins, ratio blending at the cold feed affects the hot-
bin gradations dramatically in a batch-plant operation.

Therefore, in modern batch-plant production, the blend formula is usu-
ally first established for the cold-feed bins to arrive at the target job-mix
formula just like a drum-mix plant, and then the hot bins are sampled for
their individual gradations and a new blend recipe/ratio is constructed
with these hot-bin gradations to re-arrive at the job-mix recipe. The ratios
of the individual hot bins become the recipe that is used to construct the
final mix with the batching plant.

If the HMA producer has good quality control on the cold-feed bins,
mixes can be proportioned completely with the cold feeds, eliminating the
need to screen the material in the batch plant. This is often referred to as
“screenless mixing.” Screenless mixing is done by opening a bypass chute,
allowing the heated aggregate stream to drop immediately into the No. 1
bin without passing over the screen deck. This requires drawing aggregate
from only one hot bin when weighing an individual batch, which speeds
up production. Bypassing the screen deck also eliminates wear and tear
on the screens and the screening mechanisms, which are both expensive
and time consuming to repair and maintain.

See Chapter 3, “Aggregates and Aggregate Additives,” to review the math-
ematical procedure for establishing these blend ratios. This procedure is
first used in the design process to establish the required percentages of
each material for the job-mix formula; it is then used against the individual
gradations of the cold-feed materials to reconfirm the blend ratios from
the cold-feed bins. It is used again with the individual hot-bin gradations
to establish the hot-bin percentages required to re-arrive at the target com-
posite gradation.

Once these individual hot-bin percentages are determined, establishing the
actual weights of each hot bin is relatively simple. The percentage for that
hot bin is multiplied by the total quantity of aggregate desired in the batch
and this becomes the target weight for that hot bin for that batch size.

Several things can negatively affect the gradations in the hot bins, which
will therefore negatively affect the gradation in the final mix:

* A blocked or plugged cold-feed bin, or feeder, will reduce that material

from the composite aggregate blend going to the dryer.

° If the cold-feed bins’ blend ratios are set incorrectly, the gradations in
the individual hot bins’ will be negatively affected (too many coarse
fractions in the bin or too many fine fractions in the bin).

* Too much material of any given size may exceed the screening
and separating capability of the screens on the tower, causing that

When sampling hot bins, it is important to take a full cross section of the
flow from the gate because most hot-bin walls are sloped and materials
can segregate slightly. Also, the flow of material across the screen lends
itself to segregation. This does not cause any problems with the final mix
because the materials are being mixed in the pugmill, but it is important
to obtain an accurate gradation of each hot bin. Therefore, it is important
to sample each bin across the full width.

After the sampling is done and the gradations from each hot bin are
established, the ratio of each hot bin can be calculated to arrive at the
job-mix formula.

Since material from each hot bin is weighed, the percentages of the hot
bins need to be converted into weight values. Most batch computers make
this job easy by allowing you to enter the formula in weights that total
1000 pounds (453 kilograms). For example, if hot bin No. 2 is 33.5 percent
of the total mix, then the weight target in the batch formula will be 335
pounds (152 kilograms). The operator then selects the batch size—with
the batch computer doing the calculations for the operator—to establish
the weights for the entire batch. This procedure also allows the operator
to change the batch sizes without changing the formula. It is common for
batch plants to change batch size. This usually is close to the size typically
run. Most computers make this adjustment. For example, if a plant is a 4-
ton (3.6 metric tonnes) plant, but the desired load is 14 tons (12.7 metric
tonnes), the batch computer will produce four drops at 3.5 tons (3.2 met-
ric tonnes) to arrive at the target quantity.

The gradations in the final mix will only be accurate if the operator
or the computer batches the proper quantity of material from each hot
bin. The accuracy of the scale is critically important. Frequent calibration
checks are advisable. ;

Each activity is required for gradation accuracy in the final mix produced
with a batch plant. More aggregates in the job-mix formula and tighter mix
tolerances require stringent management of the various factors that can
affect final gradations.

7.3.2 Asphalt Content Control with a
Batch Plant

Asphalt content control in a batch is simpler than gradation control.
Asphalt is simply weighed to the target weight in each batch in a separate
container called the asphalt weigh bucket. A fill valve opens, the computer
watches the weight accumulate in the asphalt scale, and the computer stops
the scale just before the target weight is reached, allowing some material to
still drain from the valve and pipe until the target weight is reached. This
early shutoff function is referred to as a free-fall compensation value, and
is automatically adjusted by the computer so the exact target is reached
with each batch. This same free-fall compensation feature is also used on

7.3.0 Mixing/Coating with a Batch Plant

Aggregates and asphalt are mixed in a pugmill in a batch plant. The
pugmill is a twin-shaft design, with adjustable hardened steel tips and
hardened steel liners. Tips can be configured for different styles of mixing.
Preferences vary, but all styles seem to be effective. The manufacturer’s
manual provides suggestions for setting the tip configuration. The pattern
shown in Figure 7.22 is referred to as an around-the-box pattern. Tips
adjust outward so that the clearance between the tip and the liner is not
excessive. The tips are usually adjusted to slightly less than the size of the

largest aggregate mixed and are then restored to target tolerance when the
gap between the tip and the liner becomes excessive.

Missing tips, excessive clearance between the tips and the liner, and
improper pattern to the tips all negatively affect the mixing efficiency of
the pugmill.

Overfilling or underfilling the pugmill also causes inadequate mixing.
When the pugmill is overfilled, the asphalt binder and the aggregates that
were drawn last do not have a chance to be pulled down into the rest
of the materials. Both asphalt content and gradation will be negatively
affected and inconsistent throughout the batch. Significantly underfilling a
mixer may also result in less than desirable results and cause segregation
in large-rock mixes. A reasonable batch size should be respected.

Batch mixing time should be long enough to produce a homogenous
mixture of evenly distributed and uniformly coated particles. Mixing time
can be established for a particular mix by the procedure noted in AASHTO
T 195 (ASTM D 2489). This method bases the degree of mixing on the
percentage of coarse particles that are completely coated with asphalt. The
method is correlated with mixing time.

The test involves separating coarse aggregate particles from the mix on
a selected sieve size and examining 200 to 300 particles under a strong
light. Any speck of uncoated aggregate particle visible to the naked eye is
classified as uncoated. Usually particles coated at 90 and 95 percent are
minimums for base and surface mixes, respectively. The least time needed
to achieve these minimum coating levels is the most desirable mixing
time. Most mixes will undergo additional mixing and coating as they are
handled through slat conveyors, storage silos, and paving equipment. The
common total batch mixing time is 25-35 seconds.

7.3.4 Recycled Mix Production with a
Batch Plant

With a batch-type plant, two basic systems for recycled mix production
are popular: the weigh-hopper method and the bucket-elevator method.
With both systems, virgin aggregate is super-heated in the dryer and then
the RAP is introduced later in the plant process. The super-heated virgin
aggregates heat and dry the wet ambient RAP. The steam and dust released
from the RAP has to be ducted to the exhaust system and the dust col-
lection equipment. Because the RAP is not introduced into the aggregate
dryer (as it is in a drum-mixer plant), additional ducting has to be added
to the plant to accomplish this goal.

With the weigh-hopper method, RAP is either introduced into the
aggregate-weigh hopper in the batch tower as an additional ingredient, or
an additional weigh hopper is added specifically for the RAP. The control
system weighs the RAP. One must adjust the binder content in the final
mix recipe for the usable binder in the RAP (see Chapter 4, “Hot Mix
Asphalt GHMA) Design”).

With the bucket-elevator method, RAP is introduced with the virgin
aggregate into the bucket elevator. The steam release starts in the bucket
elevator, which results in a continuous steam release that is less immediate
and less rapid than in the weigh-hopper method. This makes the steam
ducting easier to design, install, and manage—explaining why this type of
system is popular. With this method, however, the RAP is fractionated and
separated into the different hot bins of the batch tower. This means that
the asphalt content and the gradation of each hot bin must be analyzed
so the proper weights for each hot bin and the new liquid binder can be
properly determined. It is common to find belt scales fitted to both the
virgin-aggregate conveyor feeding the dryer and the RAP conveyor feeding
the bucket elevator. With these scales, the ratio of RAP to virgin aggregate
can be maintained.

7.4.1 The Development of the
Drum-Mix Process

Drum-mixers started appearing during the early 1970s. The first drum-
mixers used elongated drying drums not only to dry and heat the aggregate
but also to mix the liquid asphalt with the heated aggregate to produce the
final hot mix all in the same drying cylinder.

These first drums were parallel-flow dryers, meaning that the aggre-
gate and the process gas flowed in the same direction. The wet, ambient
aggregates were introduced to the end of the dryer where the burner was
located, and the aggregate was dried as it moved away from the burner.
In this fashion, the liquid-asphalt binder could be introduced into the exit
end/cooler part of the drum.

This process required less capital expenditure for initial equipment,
greater mobility for relocation to remote projects, less installation time,
less maintenance, and higher RAP capability.

This process could not have been developed without the following:

> The availability of uniformly produced, consistently sized aggregate

° The development of large, inexpensive variable-speed motors that

can be fitted to the cold-feed bins to easily proportion aggregates to
the desired final mix gradations

e The development of belt scales and flow meters for measuring the

flow of materials continuously

° The development of finished mix storage silos to elevate the material

so it can be dispensed into trucks

Each equipment development occurred independently and it became
obvious that the need for sizing and fractioning material in a batch plant
was no longer necessary.

Drum-mix plants were lower to the ground, easier to move, and less
expensive to buy and maintain than batch facilities. As a result, they
became the process of choice for hot mix asphalt producers starting in the
late 1970s, and today over 80 percent of the mix produced in the United
States is with drum-mix plants.

10.3.4 Maximum Theoretical Specific
Gravity

Testing for maximum theoretical specific gravity of HMA mixtures
(sometimes called the Rice test) is accomplished by using a vacuum to
saturate the test specimen in water. The vacuum causes the water to boil
and drives water into any available pore spaces that exist between coated
particles. The saturated specimen is then weighed in air and in water and
specific gravity values are calculated. The result is the specific gravity of
the mix at zero air voids.

Recent changes to the test procedure have added more restrictions on
the vacuum levels applied to the specimen and have introduced vibrating
frames to produce consistent levels of agitation for each specimen. Use of
these modern procedures will reduce the variability between laboratories
on a given project.

‘Test procedures for maximum theoretical specific gravity are AASHTO
T 209 or ASTM D 2041.

The test values of maximum theoretical specific gravity are used in con--
junction with other tests. It is the basis of the determination of air voids when
compared with laboratory specific gravities and can be a basis for compaction
values when compared with cores sampled from completed pavements.

10.3.5 Bulk Specific Gravity (Laboratory
Density)

Testing for bulk specific gravity is done by measuring the bulk specific grav-
ity of laboratory compacted or cored asphalt specimens. Implementation of

the Superpave system has led to increased usage of the Superpave Gyratory
Compactor (SGC) for laboratory compaction of bulk specific gravity speci-
mens of HMA. The Marshall method and the Hveem method of compacting
test specimens are also used in some jurisdictions.

The bulk specific gravity tests are typically used for two reasons: to
establish a laboratory density for comparison to in-place density to deter-
mine compaction, and to calculate volumetric properties of the mix-
ture. Regardless of the method used to prepare the test specimens (SGC,
Marshall, or Hveem), the intent is to compact test specimens within a
specified temperature range with a standard amount of compactive energy
in the laboratory. The specimens are first weighed in air, and then weighed
again while submerged in water. This testing yields specific gravity test
results that can be converted into unit weight values.

The laboratory compacted density can then be used to establish a target
value for compaction in the field. A typical compaction specification, for
example, would require the contractor to compact the HMA in the field
to 95 percent of the laboratory compacted specimens. The bulk specific
gravity test results are also used to calculate air voids.

10.3.6 Air Voids

Air voids are calculated using the bulk specific gravity of compacted
specimens and the maximum theoretical specific gravity. Specifications
typically require the laboratory compacted air voids to be within a speci-
fied range of the air voids from the JMF Gmix design). A typical range would
be plus or minus one percent from the mix design value. Refer to AASHTO
T 269 or ASTM D 3203 Percent Air Voids in Compacted Dense and Open
Bituminous Paving Mixtures for calculating the mixture air voids.

10.3.7 Temperature

Temperature control is critical to many HMA operations including
production, placement and compaction. The use and importance of tem-
perature measurements are discussed in more detail in Chapter 7, “Manu-
facture of Hot Mix Asphalt,” Chapter 8, “Hot Mix Asphalt Paving,” and
Chapter 9, “Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction.” Temperature is generally mea-
sured (Figure 10.9) at many different points during production and place-
ment of hot mix asphalt. It is usually measured with simple thermometers,
but several adaptations to the equipment are useful, especially for quality-
control. Traditionally, large-face dial thermometers, some with long stems,
have been used for testing temperatures at the various stages of asphalt
production and paving. Digital thermometers with replaceable probes are
being used more because the costs have come down. These thermometers
can be very accurate across a broad range of temperatures, so they are
very versatile. Infrared thermometers are also gaining in popularity; the

10.4 Sampling and Testing
Plan for Quality Control

Some form of quality-control (or process control) is provided during
production of all HMA. As an example, consider a plant operator who
is using a temperature gauge to measure the temperature of the heated
aggregate before batching. The batch-plant operator will discard the initial
aggregate that is not up to temperature, based on the results of these tem-
perature readings. When the aggregate reaches the target temperature, the
operator will proceed with preparing the first batch of HMA. This example
demonstrates how the operator uses data to control and adjust the batch-
ing process. The temperature reading is a process control test.

There are two key aspects of true quality-control testing. The first is mea-
suring specific characteristics and inspecting appropriate activities that
affect the quality of the finished product. The second aspect is providing
the results of those measurements and inspections in a meaningful way and
within a time frame that allows adjustments to the production process. The
subsequent adjustments or corrective action can thus prevent appreciable
non-complying materials from being incorporated into the finished work.

The quantity of quality-control data and the level of control vary greatly
from one project to another. Some project specifications require the con-
tractor to exercise extensive control and mandate specific tests and test
frequencies. Other specifications allow the contractor or the supplier to
determine the level of testing based on their own judgment of what data
is needed to adequately control the production operation.

10.4.1 Selection of Sampling Methods and
Sample Frequency

Sampling methods and sample frequencies used for quality-control
are sometimes established by the project specifications and sometimes
selected by the contractor/supplier. Many pavement owners or agencies
dictate sampling methods and give minimum sample frequencies. The
contractor, of course, is free to perform any additional testing he deems
appropriate.

Methods and locations of sampling can have significant influence on the
outcome of the parameters being measured for quality-control. Sampling,
therefore, is an important aspect of the overall quality-control program.

The methods and frequencies selected for sampling are not always the
same for quality-control as they are for acceptance. An example is the sam-
pling methods used to determine the aggregate gradation. Some agencies
use cold-feed belt sampling methods for testing aggregate gradation for

quality-control purposes. These samples are taken at the asphalt mixing
plant and can provide test results within hours of sampling. The results,
therefore, do not accommodate “real time” adjustments but do provide
opportunities for production to be adjusted at many times during a given
day. In contrast to these quality-control sampling methods, the acceptance
samples for gradation are often taken from the paving laydown process,
and gradations are run after the asphalt binder has been extracted from
the HMA samples. The results may be more representative of the mix that
was delivered to the project, but they may not be received until days after
placement. The amount of time needed to get results is an important factor
in the selection of sampling methods for HMA production.

10.4.2 Selection of Testing Methods

In the past, control of HMA production was almost exclusively driven
by asphalt content and gradation. As production capabilities become
more refined, and as the technical understanding of the factors affecting
longevity of asphalt pavements progresses, there is an increased emphasis
on controlling air voids and other volumetric properties of the mix. Many
agencies have now added mixture volumetric tests to their requirements for
both quality-control and acceptance for production of HMA.

Based on NCHRP Synthesis report 346 “State Construction Quality Assur-
ance Programs,” most state highway agencies in the U.S. select the sampling
and testing methods that are used for quality-control on their projects. These
testing requirements are typically identified in the project specifications.
Many agencies include quality-control line items in bids to reimburse con-
tractors for costs associated with providing the tests. The synthesis report
identified the following parameters that are normally required for quality-
control testing programs.

° Asphalt content

° Aggregate gradation

° Compaction

* Volumetric properties (air voids, VMA, VFA)

° Ride quality

° Aggregate characteristics (CAA, FAA, F&E, SE, etc.)

° Pavement thickness

Many of the test values will be compared with the JME When that is the
case, the same tests should be used for quality-control as were used in devel-
opment of the job-mix formula. It would not be advisable, for example, to
use Marshall density values to evaluate air voids on mixes that were designed
using the SGC. Portable SGC equipment is available and being utilized for
quality-control and acceptance on many projects. For more information on
the Superpave system or the SGC, refer to the Asphalt Institute’s manual
SP-2 “Superpave Mix Design” and Chapter 4, Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.

For tests that are not compared with the JMF, the test methods selected
for quality-control purposes may be different from the test methods used for

acceptance of the same parameter. Quality-control testing for compaction of
asphalt pavements, for example, quite often uses nuclear density test meth-
ods, whereas the acceptance testing for compaction may be performed using
bulk specific gravity test methods on cores cut from the finished pavement.
Test results from cores come too late to be used for adjusting the compaction
operation because the asphalt will have already cooled. Nuclear density tests
can be taken while the HMA is still hot and the compaction operation is ongo-
ing. Thus, nuclear density test results can be an important part of the overall
quality-control program for HMA compaction.

Since quality-control methods must emphasize timeliness and convenience,
the results can be less accurate and impart more variation in the data. If the
QC test method is different than the acceptance method, a minor shift in the
data should be anticipated. It would be unreasonable, for example, to stop
compaction operations when a reading of 95 percent compaction was read
on a nuclear density gauge, if the minimum required compaction was 95
percent using core densities. A higher target could be established to account
for the likely variation and the possible shift of compaction values from
the nuclear density method to the core density method. It is also a prudent
quality-control measure to correlate quality-control test methods and labo-
ratories to the acceptance methods performed by the acceptance laboratory.
This can be accomplished with several “split” samples from a test strip or at
the beginning of HMA production.

If a nuclear gauge is being used to control density and if an accurate corre-
lation to core densities has not been made, a “maximum density” method can
be used to overcome the variation from nuclear density test results and core
density test results. For this maximum density method, nuclear density read-
ings are taken in specifically marked locations between roller passes so that
the relative increases in compaction can be tracked throughout the compaction
operation. When the pavement reaches the highest achievable density (that is,
other rolling patterns yield lower density), then the pavement has achieved
a relative maximum density. Compaction should continue with the same pat-
tern until this relative maximum is achieved in other locations for that day’s
placement as well. Establishing rolling patterns is a trial-and-error process. It is
important to recognize that the number of vibratory passes in the breakdown
rolling will typically have a greater effect on the final compaction values than
the number of passes with a finish roller. Therefore, rolling patterns with vary-
ing initial breakdown passes should be included in the trial-and-error process.
A variety of rolling patterns may need to be tried before settling on the final
pattern. For more information on compaction of hot mix asphalt, refer to
Chapter 9, “Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction.”

10.4.4 Test Strip

Every project involves a unique set of circumstances and challenges
relative to producing a quality pavement. Even the best pre-paving plan-
ning leaves the contractor with methods and materials that work in theory
but have not been proven in actual production. A job-mix formula, for

=== ORIGINAL PDF PAGE 9 ===
=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 509 ===
example, is established based on mix design analyses from samples that
have been prepared and mixed in the laboratory. The results of these tests
do not exactly match the results of similar materials that are produced in
an industrial asphalt mixing plant. Placement and compaction operations
usually have to be adjusted from one project to another because of differ-
ences in the environment and the equipment used. Compacting asphalt
on compacted subgrade, for example, may require different rolling pat-
terns from that for an overlay of an existing HMA roadway. Test strips to
validate the means, methods, and materials to be used under the specific
project conditions are becoming widely used by owner/agencies and pav-
ing contractors.

Test strips or “test sections” are constructed before beginning regular
production paving for a given asphalt mixture. They are typically consid-
ered part of the regular pavement if the test results comply with project
specifications. They are comprised of shorter lengths of pavement (typi-
cally 500 to 1,000 feet or 154 to 308 meters) and should be constructed
with the same equipment and materials that will be used for the rest of the
project. Projects with cold joint density specifications will often require
a cold joint to be constructed as part of the test strip. Two or three days
of time may be required to evaluate the test strip data before beginning
regular production paving. Testing for test strips is typically the same as
that required for quality-control and acceptance for the rest of the proj-
ect. Comparing test results from different laboratories and confirming the
details of the methods used can be a side benefit to the test strip process.
This is a good time to verify that any variations in laboratory values are a
function of normal deviation in testing and differing methods and are not
because incorrect or different procedures or equipment are being used by
one of the laboratories.

If the test results of the test strip indicate the job-mix formula is suit-
able for producing complying materials, and the appropriate spreading,
placing, and compaction can be achieved, then production paving can
proceed. If the results of the test strip do not meet project specifications,
then adjustments can be made and a new test strip produced.

10.5 Plant Production

Quality-Control Data Analysis

Quality control of plant production involves producing, analyzing, and
reporting a tremendous amount of data. The organization of this data
is generally established in the quality-control plan. There are three key
areas for which the data must be properly analyzed, documented, and
understood. The first of these is in verifying the job-mix formula. Because
the formula is developed in the laboratory and made from samples taken
weeks before production begins, it is always prudent to verify that the
job-mix formula will produce a quality mix and that the parameters estab-
lished in the mix design can be achieved. The second key area is organiz-
ing and documenting the daily quality-control data. This is necessary so
that the data is meaningful to the users and readily available if problems
occur or if adjustments need to be made. The third key area is in adjust-
ing the production process. A good understanding of the test results and
reasons for the data are the foundation of the good judgment necessary to
make appropriate adjustments. More detailed descriptions of these three
key areas of quality-control data analysis are presented below.

10.5.1 Job-Mix Formula Verification

Field verification of the HMA mix design is the first step in implement-
ing the overall quality-control plan. It involves testing and analyzing the
field-produced mixture to verify that the criteria established by the specifi-
cations are being met. Verification is necessary at the beginning of produc-
tion of each mixture or job mix formula to determine what differences exist
between design and production and if adjustments need to be made.

HMA mixture designs are developed in the laboratory, usually with
aggregates sampled from the project and before production begins. Test-
ing and analysis of the anticipated performance of that mix is generally
accomplished as part of the design.

It has been documented in many cases that even though a mix is designed
for a certain percent of air voids in the laboratory, a plant-mixed sample
containing the proper asphalt content, aggregate gradation, and compaction
by the same laboratory method may show different volumetric properties.
In their study of field testing and field laboratory test results titled FHWA
Demonstration Project 74, “Field Management of Asphalt Mixes,” FHWA
found substantial differences between the laboratory mix design and the
volumetric properties of the field-produced mixture. Based on the study,
these changes tend to result, on average, in slightly lower air void and VMA
values than the targets established by the laboratory-prepared JMFs.

Significant equipment and material differences exist between the small-scale
operation of the laboratory mixing bowl and an asphalt mixing facility, which
could lead to the mixture property changes noted. Many causes for volumetric
changes in field-produced mixture have been proposed:

¢ Moisture removed more effectively in the laboratory than in the field

° An excessive amount of baghouse fines returned to the mixing process

° Highly absorptive aggregates used. (Field absorption may differ from

laboratory absorption.)

* Poor sampling used to obtain aggregate samples for the laboratory

design

* Nonstandard mix design procedures used to develop the JMF

° Material sources (location and/or strata) changed between the mix

design and field production

° Aggregate degradation and shape changes during plant mixing

* Non-uniform stockpiling of aggregates

The changes that occur in the field-produced mixture are likely due to
a combination of some or all of the listed causes in any particular case.
Attention to detail in material production and mix design development can
reduce the potential effects of many of the factors shown.

Some form of verification that the proposed job-mix formula will produce
materials meeting the project specifications will occur on every project.
Some agencies require that verifications be performed by the agency labora-
tory or by a laboratory different from the one that did the mix design. Other
agencies will utilize test strips to validate the job-mix formula. Additional
discussion of test strips can be found in Section 10.4.4. Some agencies allow
the contractor to make minor changes in the job-mix formula without a new
mix design if the changes are made to improve the volumetric properties
of the mix. Improvements to volumetric properties in such cases generally
mean getting the laboratory air voids closer to the target value.

If there are no specified means to verify the job-mix formula, then it
will be done during the initial paving. This creates a higher risk that non-
complying or marginal HMA will be produced for a longer period of time
before adjustments are made.

10.5.2 Mixture Control

Quality-control test data must be quickly reported and analyzed so that
it can be used to improve the consistency and overall quality of the HMA
product. The goal is to understand the data and to evaluate whether any
adjustments to the plant process or materials processes are warranted. It
is necessary to keep thorough records so the quality-control manager can
understand the overall production and observe trends that may need fur-
ther action. Good mixture control is accomplished by first organizing the
data in a meaningful way. Control charts are often used for this. Then the
data must be analyzed and understood. Finally, appropriate adjustments to
the production operations need to be made.

Control Charts

One way that quality-control data is presented is on control charts
(Figure 10.10). Control charts are graphed plots of quality-control data
over time. The specification limits may also be plotted. Control charts are
useful because they provide a quick visual reference of how close to the
specification limits the test data are. They can also be invaluable in spot-
ting trends in the data.

Data Analysis

Some data collected for quality-control is straightforward, and corrective
action is relatively automatic after receiving the information. An example
of this would be measurements of temperature at the batch plant. If the
target temperature for mixture leaving the plant is 320°F (160°C), and the
quality-control check determines that the temperature is 300°F (149°C),
the plant operator can begin adjustments immediately to increase the mix-
ing temperature to the target.

sideration before corrective action is taken. An example of this would be
measurements of laboratory air voids in the mix. If the target air voids are
4 percent (assume the specifications limit is +1 percent) and if the quality-
control test indicates the mixture is at 2.9 percent, there are several things

provided during the project. The use of computer spreadsheets, e-mail,
and the Internet mean these reports can contain large amounts of data,
and the information can be distributed to the appropriate parties in a
timely way. Because of the tremendous amount of data generated for
quality-control of HMA production, summaries of the daily average values
can be useful in analyzing and understanding how the materials are chang-

It is impossible to plot all the relationships in the quality-control data that
may become useful. Storage of quality-control data in spreadsheet applica-
tions can be helpful so that specific plots can be rapidly developed while
investigating noncompliant items. An example of this is shown in Figure

the data presented in the quality-control summary (Figure 10.11). A plot of

two parameters. As the minus No. 200’s decreased, the air voids decrease

ing and suggests that better control of the minus No. 200’s would affect
better control of the air voids. Further observation of the data reveals that
the mixture is essentially achieving the design air voids if the mix design
minus No. 200’s is achieved. In this example, changes in the job-mix for-
mula would not contribute to correcting the high air voids.

10.5.3 Plant Adjustments

The purpose of quality-control testing for HMA production is to provide
plant managers and operators with valuable information they need to control
the plant. If the data show that the produced material is deviating from the
job-mix formula, the plant personnel should decide what adjustments are
needed to bring the material closer to the job-mix formula.

It is important to realize that all HMA plants (in this discussion,
aggregate crushing and production equipment are considered part of the
plant) will produce material that has a given amount of variation. Some
plants will produce materials that are more consistent than others. Under-
standing this is important because adjusting the production will not cor-
rect normal plant variations.

As an example of this, consider a plant that is operating under an allowable
tolerance for the percent passing the No. 200 screen of 3 percent to 7 percent.
Assume the average result from quality-control tests is 6 percent passing the
No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) and the QC test results range from 4.5 percent to
7.5 percent. No adjustments have been made to the plant up to this point. In
this example, the job-mix formula for minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) was
5 percent and the average QC results were 6 percent. Based on this infor-
mation, the plant operator should be considering an adjustment that could
slightly reduce the minus No. 200, preferably by 1 percent. All the other
quality-control data, of course, would have to be reviewed before any adjust-
ments are made to be sure that it would not be detrimental to other quality-
control test parameters.

Assume in the above example, that the first quality-control test result
received was the 4.5 percent minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeter). An immediate

10.6 Placement Quality-Control
Data Analysis

The primary quality factors for placement of HMA are compaction,
thickness, and smoothness. Placement quality-control, therefore, focuses
on these same factors. These three factors are discussed below. Tempera-
ture is also discussed because it plays such a vital role in controlling and
achieving compaction.

Similar to quality-contro] data for hot mix production at the plant, it can
be very useful to summarize the placement quality-control data to get a
better overall picture of what is happening during placement and compac-
tion. The two key factors measured—smoothness and compaction—are
critical to the performance of an asphalt pavement, so understanding and
controlling these aspects of the construction operation are very important.
Tables 10.7A and 10.7B (metric) show examples of a quality-control data
summary for HMA placement. For complete quality-control documenta-
tion, summaries should be compiled and given to the quality-control man-
ager on a regular basis. For interpretation and analysis of the data, it is
useful to get an overall understanding of what is happening on the project
and then to evaluate the details of particular issues more specifically.

10.6.1 Temperature

Temperature is perhaps the most critical variable in HMA compaction.
The temperature of the mix is, therefore, very important to those respon-
sible for compaction. One difficulty that the compaction crews have is that
the HMA goes through so many other operations before it is ready to be
compacted. The temperature is affected by each operation, and the com-
paction equipment operators and foremen have little influence on them.
Good quality-control, which includes temperature readings at all phases
of production and delivery, will help provide HMA at the appropriate
compaction temperatures to the rollers. Some quality-control specifica-
tions require temperature readings to be recorded at many points along
the way and reported each day. Specifications for a project using a batch
plant, for example, may require that temperature readings be recorded
for the asphalt cement prior to mixing, the aggregate after heating, the
mixture immediately after mixing, and at delivery to the paving machine.
With this kind of data stored for each day’s production, it would be easy
to determine the root cause of temperature problems if they occurred on
a particular day. More importantly, it would be easy to establish a correc-
tive action plan to get the temperatures where they need to be for proper
compaction.

10.6.2 Compaction Control Methods

The goal of compacting a hot mix asphalt pavement is to achieve an
optimum air void content and provide a smooth, uniform surface. The
resulting in-place air void content of the HMA is in many ways the single
most important factor that affects performance of the mixture throughout
the life of the pavement. The activities involved in the proper design, pro-
duction, placement, and compaction of the asphalt mixture are combined
to achieve the in-place density of the HMA pavement and to ultimately
determine whether the pavement will perform as expected.

The key points here are that controlling compaction is very important
and that compaction is affected by many factors other than those spe-
cifically measured during placement of the mixture. This means that low
compaction problems on a given project may not be resolved simply by
adjusting the rolling pattern or the temperatures. The quality-control] man-
ager and project superintendent may need to analyze all the quality-control
data, including that from the mixture production, to address compaction
problems.

Of course, in addressing a compaction problem, the paving crew should
first review their own operations and procedures for the placement and
compaction operation, paying particular attention to temperatures, disci-
plined adherence to good rolling technique, and consistent and tested roll-
ing patterns. The compactability of the asphalt mixture provided should
also be evaluated.

A variety of methods are available to control the quality of the com-
paction for HMA, and those methods will be discussed in more detail
below. These methods are usually driven by specification, however, so the
quality-control will be based on the specified methods for acceptance of
compaction. The quality-control efforts should be centered on establishing
and documenting compliance with the actual specifications.

in-Place Density/Compaction Control Measurements

A common control method for compaction is to simply measure the in-
place density. These measured densities will be compared with a laboratory
density (the specified reference density), and a percentage of the labora-
tory density will be calculated. Nuclear density measurements are the most
common form of quality-control density testing. Non-nuclear devices using
electromagnetic waves to measure the dielectric constant in the pavement
materials have been developed and are gaining popularity for quality-control
density testing. Information on comparisons of nuclear and these non-nuclear
devices can be found in the reference list. In some instances, quality-control
testing for density is done using cores; however, the use of cores imparts
a delay in the results. Cores should always be used, however, to correlate
and/or calibrate the quality-control test methods selected, especially if cores
are to be used for acceptance.

Rolling Patterns

Most paving contractors will create defined “rolling patterns” for a par-
ticular placement on the first day of paving. Nuclear density test data can
play a key role in developing these rolling patterns. A rolling pattern is
defined as the number and sequence of successive passes of compac-
tion equipment over an asphalt pavement. The rolling pattern is generally
adjusted until the desired compaction is achieved. It is a trial and error
process. Details of compaction principles and rolling procedures can be
found in Chapter 9, “Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction.”

In order to provide meaningful information to the quality-control man-
ager, the quality-control density testing must be done at many points in
the rolling operation. Rolling patterns are established on the first day of
paving and ideally done during construction of a test strip. The test strip
will provide calibration of the nuclear gauge with cores cut from the pave-
ment, and the rolling pattern may need to be adjusted. The following
example describes how the quality-control testing is used in the develop-
ment of a compaction control-rolling pattern.

Rolling Pattern Example

The equipment that will be used for the rolling pattern must be the
same as will be used during construction. For this example, the compac-
tion equipment consists of a double-drum vibratory compactor for the
breakdown rolling, a heavy pneumatic compactor for the intermediate
rolling, and a static steel-wheel compactor for the finish rolling.

During breakdown rolling, the operator may make a vibratory pass for-
ward and back for the first coverage. After that coverage, the quality-
control technician takes a nuclear density test and marks the location
of the test on the pavement. This is done by drawing a rectangle outlining
the bottom of the gauge on the pavement so the gauge can be returned
to the same location and orientation for subsequent tests. It is good practice
to mark and test multiple locations so that average density changes can be

pattern may include a different number of passes, a different amplitude on

pneumatic rollers, heavier compactors, insertion of another vibratory roller
into the intermediate compaction process, and so on. Numerous options are
available for compaction rolling patterns.

that nuclear density gauges can have a large bias and wide variation from the
actual density of the pavement. Good quality-control plans improve the accu-
racy of these instruments by calibrating the gauge with cores from the project.

10.6.3 Mixture Compactability

Some mixtures are easier to compact than others. It can be useful when
controlling the paving operation to understand the effect of the mixture and
the variables that affect compactability.

Experience says that coarse-graded mixtures with low fines tend to require
significant effort to compact. Success with such mixtures can be achieved
with excellent compaction methods and good quality-control measures.

Two other mixture parameters that should be of particular interest to the
paving crew or paving subcontractor are air voids and lift thickness. The
following sections describe these two mixture properties and how they can
affect compaction. These two items should be carefully planned and moni-
tored with respect to their impact on the compactability of HMA.

Laboratory Air Voids

If a mixture is produced with laboratory compacted air voids that are higher
than the target, additional compactive effort in the field will be required to
achieve density. Suppose, for example, that a project has an established roll-
ing pattern that achieves about 94 percent compaction when the mixture
is produced at 4 percent laboratory voids. If the laboratory air voids of the
mixture suddenly increased by 1.5 percent (to 5.5 percent), then the same
rolling pattern in the field would yield something on the order of 1.5 percent
less (about 92 to 93 percent).

When changes in laboratory air voids create a need for additional com-
pactive effort, the establishment of a new or revised rolling pattern may be
needed. Even if no air void limits are specified for production, the laboratory
compacted voids must be controlled to produce a compactable mix.

Compaction problems can occur as well if the air voids are low, espe-
cially in projects with statistical acceptance requirements. In those cases,
fluctuations in air voids will cause fluctuations in compaction and can
affect pay factors that include standard deviations. Good consistency in
laboratory air voids of the produced mix is essential for maintaining good
control of compaction.

Lift Thickness

Lift thicknesses must be well planned, and compactability should be the
driving factor in that decision. If the desired lift thickness is less than the rec-
ommended values, then a mixture with a smaller nominal maximum aggre-
gate size should be used so that proper compaction can be achieved. See
Chapter 9 for recommended lift thicknesses based on nominal maximum
ageregate size.

It is also important to realize that adjustments to compaction opera-

10.6.4 Thickness and Spread Rates

Quality-control testing for pavement thickness is accomplished using
depth gauges (Figure 10.13) that are pushed into the HMA after the paver
has placed the hot mix but before the compaction equipment has rolled it
down. For most dense-graded mixes, a rule of thumb is that the thickness of
the uncompacted hot mix should be 1.25 times the final thickness. The actual
decrease in thickness could be more accurately determined for a given mix
design using core thicknesses measured and compared with the initial depth
gauge thicknesses.

Thickness is also measured by cutting cores from the completed pave-
ment surface. Core thicknesses are considered the most accurate method,
but because they are taken after the work is complete, they do not lend
themselves well to quality-control purposes. These values could be used
to adjust thicknesses for subsequent paving days, but the paving equip-
ment is usually controlled with thickness readings behind the paver with
a depth gauge.

In measuring and adjusting lift thicknesses, it is important to consider that
paver screeds are self-leveling. They are designed to fill in the low spots and cut
down the high spots, making it impossible to lay a uniform lift thickness on an
uneven surface. If the depth checks were exactly equal all of the time, the new
surface would have the same rough profile as the underlying pavement or base.
In reality, many quality-control “depth checks” will be made behind the paver,
but these checks should lead to very few adjustments to the paving machine.
Consideration of this natural variation in lift thickness, especially for overlays
on rough pavements, is necessary for evaluating core thicknesses for accep-
tance purposes as well.

Some agencies specify spread rates (yield) in lieu of thickness. Spread rates
can be checked as a quality-control measure by calculating the weight of mate-
rial placed over a measured area. If measurement of spread rates is required as
part of the quality-control plan, a technician can keep a log of beginning and
ending stations for each truck delivered, along with the weight of each load.
On this log, running values of spread rates can be calculated and reported to
the paving superintendent on a periodic basis throughout the day.

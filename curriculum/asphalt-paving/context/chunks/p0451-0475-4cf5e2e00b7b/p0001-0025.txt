9.2.2 Need for Compaction

During the compaction of HMA, three very important factors that affect
pavement performance take place. The asphalt-coated aggregate particles
are pressed together, air voids are reduced, and mix density (weight-to-
volume ratio) increases. This squeezing together of the aggregates increases
their surface-to-surface contact and inter-particle friction, resulting in
higher mix stability and pavement strength.

The reduction of air voids to the optimum level in the mix produces
a pavement that is nearly impermeable. In an undercompacted mix, the
voids tend to be interconnected, thereby permitting the intrusion of air
and water into the pavement structure. Premature pavement distress can
then occur. The air oxidizes the asphalt binder, making it brittle and caus-
ing the mix to crack under the repeated deflections from traffic loads.
Premature aging can also result in premature thermal cracking. Water per-
meating into and through the pavement can lead to the stripping of the
asphalt binder from the aggregate, the weakening of the base and sub-
grade soils, or freeze-thaw damage in colder climates. Finally, if an optimal
level of compaction is not achieved during construction, subsequent traffic
will further densify the mix. This will occur principally in the wheelpaths,
and the resulting ruts may be a safety hazard for traffic, especially during
wet weather.

9.2.3 Mechanics of Compaction

The goal of compacting HMA is to achieve the optimum air-voids content.
Behind the paver, the HMA typically has 15 to 20 percent in-place air voids.
It is the task of the rollers to reduce that void content to 8 percent or less for
dense-graded HMA mixes. Typically, stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes should
be compacted to 6 percent or less air voids. At these levels, the voids are usu-
ally not interconnected and the detrimental effects of air and water (primarily
raveling and stripping) are avoided, as illustrated in Figure 9.2. If a mix is
compacted to a very low air-voids level (less than 2 percent), it is an indicator
that a problem exists in the mix design or mix production itself—for example,
too much asphalt binder or dust.

Even with a properly designed mix, compaction of the mat is possible
only if two conditions exist: correct mix temperature and mix confinement.
At high temperatures, the asphalt binder is in a fluid state, allowing the
aggregate particles to readily move past one another and rearrange into
a tight mass. As it cools, the asphalt binder stiffens and resists further mix
densification. Therefore, compaction must be completed while the mix is hot
and workable. Further, no one specific set of temperature ranges applies to
all asphalt binders or mixes. For example, mixtures using polymer-modified
asphalt binders stiffen at higher temperatures, allowing less time for compac-
tion than mixtures using an unmodified-asphalt binder.

Even when the mat is the correct temperature, the target density can only
be obtained when the mix being compacted is adequately confined. This
confinement is controlled by three forces: the compressive force of the roller,
the support provided by the surface beneath the mat, and the cohesion of
the mix. The compressive force of the rollers comes either from their weight
or from a combination of weight and dynamic energy due to vibration.
The supporting force of the subgrade results from its stability and firmness.

9.2.4 Factors Affecting Compaction

Factors affecting compaction can be categorized into several classes:
mix properties, environmental effects, layer (lift) thickness, subgrade and
bases, and time available for compaction.

Mix Properties

Properties of various aggregates and asphalt binders have a pronounced
effect on the workability of mixes. In addition, the specific mix types cho-
sen for a project may have different target density requirements, which
will affect the types of equipment and procedures used. For example, a
gap-graded mixture that uses a polymer-modified asphalt binder such as
SMA requires a different approach for compaction than does a fine-graded
mixture designed for light-duty traffic. These properties and the tempera-
ture of the mix at the time of compaction must be considered when decid-
ing on a compaction procedure.

Aggregate

Gradation, surface texture, angularity, and characteristics of the filler/dust
affect the workability of the mix. As the maximum aggregate size or the per-
centage of coarse aggregate in the mix increases, the workability decreases
and greater compactive effort is required to achieve target density.

Similarly, mixtures using aggregates with a rough surface texture require
greater compactive effort, as opposed to a smooth-aggregate surface. Many

mixtures used today on high-volume and/or heavy-load facilities, such as
interstate highways and major airports, have specifications that require
high percentages of crushed aggregate. Mixtures designed to resist rutting
under heavy traffic often require more compactive effort applied at higher
temperatures than those designed for light-duty traffic.

Mixtures produced from gravels typically have more rounded particles
than quarried stone and are thus more workable and easier to compact. Nat-
ural sands, which commonly have rounded particle shapes, are often added
to lower-traffic volume mixes in the interests of economy and to improve
workability. Too much sand, particularly in the middle particle sizes—around
the No. 30 (0.60 millimeter) mesh sieve—will result in tender mixes (mixes
with high workability but low stability). Tender mixes are easily displaced
by heavy rollers and too much rolling. They are often susceptible to scuffing
and shear deformation by traffic after several weeks in place.

The fines or filler content in the mix will also affect the compaction
process. The combination of filler and asphalt binder is what glues the
aggregates together in HMA pavements; therefore, the mix should contain
sufficient fines to combine with the asphalt binder to produce the neces-
sary cohesion when the mix cools. The addition of mineral filler will help
offset the tenderness or slow-setting properties of mixes containing too
much sand. Conversely, if a mix contains too many fines it will become
“summy” and very difficult to compact.

Asphalt Binder

At room temperatures, asphalt binder is virtually a solid, whereas at
250°-300°F (121°-149°C), it is a fluid. As previously mentioned, for a mix
to be properly compacted, the asphalt binder must be fluid enough to
permit the aggregate particles to move past one another. In effect then,
the asphalt binder acts as a lubricant during compaction. As the mix cools,
the asphalt binder solidifies and resists compaction. In general, once mix-
tures made with an unmodified asphalt binder fall below 175°F (80°C), the
asphalt binder, in combination with the fines in the mix, begins to bind
the aggregate particles firmly in place. Consequently, productive compac-
tion of the mix without damaging it is extremely difficult once the mix has
cooled below that point.

The grade of asphalt binder used and the temperature at which the
mix is produced determine its viscosity. Other factors being equal, higher-
viscosity asphalt binders in the mix may require a slightly higher compac-
tion temperature and/or greater compactive effort. Generally, Performance
Graded (PG) binders with a high temperature grade difference of 92°C or
more require polymer modification. The use of polymer-modified asphalt
binders in HMA has increased steadily since the mid-1990s. These bind-
ers must be heated to higher temperatures to achieve the same fluidity as
unmodified binders for mixing and compaction, and compaction must be
completed more quickly—before the mixture temperature drops below
240°F (115°C). Care must be taken not to overheat any asphalt binder, or
damage may occur. Consult the binder manufacturer for recommended
temperatures and handling practices.

The effective asphalt binder content of the mix also affects workability. As
the asphalt content increases, the film thickness of the asphalt binder on the
aggregate particles increases. At compaction temperatures, this increased
film thickness increases the lubricating effect of the asphalt binder and up
to a certain point makes compaction easier.

Mix Types

Three main types of mixes are used in HMA construction: dense-graded,
open-graded, and SMA. These different mix types are discussed in Chap-
ter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt Design.” Each has specific characteristics that will
affect its ability to be compacted. In general, the more compactive effort
required in the laboratory for the mix to achieve target air-voids require-
ments, the tougher it will be to compact in the field. Open-graded mixtures
generally do not require a great amount of compactive effort in the field.

Mix Temperature

The temperature at which an HMA mixture is produced affects both the
ease of compaction and the time available for compaction. The time available
for compaction increases with mix temperature, but there are limits as to how
high the production temperature should be to avoid damaging the asphalt
binder. Upper limits for mix temperature are discussed in Chapter 2, “Petro-
leum Asphalts,” and more specifically in Asphalt Institute’s (EC-101), Best
Management Practices to Minimize Emissions During HMA Construction,
which covers best practices for storage and handling of asphalt binders. The
upper temperature limits increase with higher PG grades and are generally
higher for modified binders. The best temperature to begin rolling is the max-
imum temperature at which the mix will support the roller without moving
horizontally.

At the time of placement, the mix temperature is essentially uniform
throughout the thickness of the lift. However, the top and bottom surfaces
cool more rapidly than the interior portion of the mat. Extreme tempera-
ture differences can lead to “heat checking” or other surface deficiencies,
or to honeycombing near the bottom of a thick lift placed during cold
weather. Careful surface inspection during compaction and observation of
the condition of core specimens removed from the pavement is necessary
to judge whether these problems exist.

Environmental Effects

The rate at which the mix cools affects the length of time during which
density can and must be achieved. The following conditions all affect the rate
of cooling of the HMA: ambient (air) temperature, the temperature of the sur-
face upon which the mix is being placed, the wind speed, and the time of day.
Cool air temperatures, strong winds, and cool surfaces all shorten the time for
compaction. This may make compaction more difficult. Paving during the day
will usually allow for a longer paving time because of the sun’s effect on the
air temperature and on the underlying surface temperature. Conversely, night
paving tends to allow less time for compaction.

Layer (Lift) Thickness

Generally, it is easier to achieve target density in thicker lifts of HMA
than in thin ones. The thicker the lift, the longer it retains its heat and the
longer the time for achieving compaction. This is beneficial when rolling
lifts of highly stable mixes that are difficult to compact, or when paving
in cool weather.

A generai rule of thumb for mixes above the maximum density line (see
Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt Design”) would be to use at least three times the
nominal maximum aggregate size for the minimum lift thickness. For example,
a %-inch (12.5 millimeters) fine-graded mix would have a minimum compacted
lift thickness of 1% inches (37.5 millimeters). A coarse-graded mix (having a
gradation that passes below the maximum density line) requires a 4-to-1 rule
or minimum lift thickness of four times the nominal maximum aggregate size.
For example, a %-inch (12.5 millimeters) coarse-graded mix would have a
minimum compacted lift thickness of 2 inches (50 millimeters). The reason for
the different criteria is to provide sufficient room for the particles to realign
and pack during compaction.

Increased layer thickness can permit lower mix temperatures to be used
because of the reduced rate of cooling. For example, paving thick layers
in the middle of the summer may lead to an excessively long wait for the
mix to cool before the finish rolling can be completed. Additional rolling
during this time may actually damage the mat.

Uniform lift thickness is important as well. In variable thickness layers
(such as those used to correct cross-slope or to level the profile), it is best
to use a prescribed rolling pattern rather than a density target, to ensure
that the best possible uniform density is achieved. Use of a pneumatic-tire
roller on uneven surfaces will also help. If the thickness of the layer is less
than two and a half to three times the nominal aggregate size, it may be
impossible to achieve the density requirements in the specifications.

Subgrade and Bases

The condition of the subgrade and/or base that is being paved upon
will have a significant impact on the final density achieved. The stiffness
of the underlying layer plays an important part in helping to provide the
confinement necessary for compaction. Attempting to bridge a soft base
or subgrade with HMA mix is not recommended. In addition, the tempera-
ture of the base and the moisture condition can either help or hurt the
compaction process. Cold, wet bases tend to pull heat out of the HMA mix,
causing it to cool rapidly, thus making compaction more difficult.

The base and subgrade layers must be level and true to line and grade
so that the HMA layers are uniform in thickness and section. As previ-
ously mentioned, a soft or weak base or subgrade will create difficulties in
obtaining the desired uniform density.

Time Available for Compaction

All these factors (mix temperature, air temperature, wind, time of day,
mat thickness, and base condition and temperature) will affect the rate
of cooling and thus the time available for compaction. Several computer

9.2.5 Cool Weather Paving

Placing and compacting HMA is difficult in cool weather and poses
more challenges than just the reduced time available for compaction. It
is particularly important to ensure that the equipment is properly main-
tained and that the sequence of delivery, placement, and rolling is well
coordinated. Special attention should be given to pneumatic-tire rollers to
keep the tires warm to avoid pickup, which will be discussed later.

Also in cool weather, the temperature differential between the air and
the mix is substantial, causing the surface to cool much more rapidly than
the internal portion of the mix. This differential cooling may cause a thin
crust to form at the surface. This crust might develop microcracks (called
checking) that later may cause the pavement surface to ravel prematurely.
Cold surfaces may also cause immediate solidification of the mixture that
is in contact with the surface, resulting in “honeycombing” in thick mats
and possibly poor adhesion or slippage in thin mats.

In summary, materials, mix types, and environmental conditions can
have a profound impact on the compatibility of the mix. These effects are
summarized in Table 9.1.

9.3 Compaction Equipment

Compaction is performed using any of several types of rollers that apply
force (either static or dynamic) to the mat, reorienting aggregate particles
to reduce the volume occupied by the mixture.

Self-propelled rollers are by far the most common type of compaction
equipment used for HMA construction. Towed-type rollers should not be
used for HMA compaction, but hand-operated walk-behind rollers, vibrat-
ing plates, or even hand tamps are often used in small areas inaccessible
to larger rollers. Typical self-propelled compaction rollers include these
three types:

9.3.1 Static Steel-Wheel Rollers

Steel-wheel rollers by configuration are two-axle tandem rollers or
three-wheel rollers, as shown in Figure 9.5. Usually, the drums are solid
across the width, though models with split drums are available. The split
drum allows the rotation speed of the drum to change (from one side to
the other) when turning, minimizing shear stresses on the surface of the

lift that may cause tearing or cracking. Steel-wheel rollers used for rolling
HMA include a water-spray system and scrapers to moisten the drums to
help prevent mix from sticking to the drums.

Steel-wheel rollers should be checked for wear on the surface of the drum.
A sharp metal straightedge may be used for this check. The roller should not
be used if grooves or pits have worn into the rolling drum. Scrapers should
be routinely inspected and replaced if they are excessively worn.

Weight

Steel-wheel rollers can vary in weight from 3 to 14 tons (2.7 to 12.7 tonnes)
or more. Most can be ballasted to increase the weight. Roller widths range
from 40 inches to 54 inches (102 centimeters to 137 centimeters). A typical
steel-wheel roller would have an unballasted weight of 8 tons (7.9 tonnes)
and a ballasted weight of 10 tons (9.8 tonnes) with 50-inch (127 centime-
ters) wide drums. Wider and heavier rollers are available from a variety of
manufacturers.

Pounds per Linear Inch

Because steel-wheel rollers vary in width and weight, a simple means
was developed to help quantify the compactive effort applied by static
rollers. By dividing the weight of the roller by the width of both drums,
the overall static linear load, expressed in pounds per linear inch (PLD
or kilograms per millimeter of roller, can be determined. For example, an
8-ton roller with a 50-inch drum width would have an overall PLI of 160
PLI. The PLI can be used to match up rollers of different sizes and manu-
facturers when establishing a rolling pattern.

Adjusting Compaction Force

The two ways to adjust the compactive force of a steel-wheel roller are
to adjust the ballast weight) of the roller and the speed. Adding weight
will increase the static linear load and apply greater force on the surface
with each pass or coverage the roller makes. Adjusting the speed of the
roller affects the dwell time that the drum has on the surface of the HMA.
Speeding up a roller decreases the dwell time and lowers density.

9.3.2 Pneumatic-Tire Rollers

Pneumatic-tire rollers are also called rubber-tire rollers, or seal rollers.
Self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers have two to eight wheels in front and
four to eight wheels in the rear, though most will fall into the seven- or
nine-total-wheels classes. The wheels on these rollers generally oscillate
(axle moves up and down). An example of a pneumatic roller is shown in
Figure 9.6.

Unlike a steel-wheel roller, the surface of a pneumatic-tire roller conforms
to the shape of the underlying surface. This allows the roller to contact all
points on the surface and also to manipulate particles differently than a
steel-wheel roller.

Pneumatic-tire rollers have several advantages:

° They provide a more uniform degree of compaction than steel-wheel
rollers.

They improve the seal near the surface, thus decreasing the perme-

° They orient the aggregate particles for greatest stability, as high-pressure

The main disadvantage of a pneumatic-tire roller is the tendency for the
tires to pick up fines from the surface of the HMA mix—unless the tires
are kept clean and warm. This can be a serious problem with mixtures
using polymer-modified asphalt binder and especially for SMA. Although
pneumatic-tire rollers have been used successfully to compact SMA, their
use is discouraged with this mixture classification.

Several self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers have a device to change tire
inflation while the roller is operating. This inflation-on-the-run system auto-
matically maintains any preset tire pressure or can raise or lower the infla-
tion pressure while the roller is operating. Some compacting conditions and.
requirements require different inflation pressures. However, tire inflation pres-
sures should all be very close between tires and checked at regular intervals.

Weight

Self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers vary in weight from 10.5 to 30 tons
(9.5 to 27.2 tonnes). Ballast can be added to the machines to increase the
weight. Widths of these rollers range from 60 to 90 inches (524 to 2286
millimeters).

Contact Pressure

The contact pressure of a pneumatic-tire roller is directly related to
the wheel load divided by the contact area (footprint) that the tire has in
contact with the pavement surface. The ply rating of the tire determines
the maximum and minimum inflation pressures, thereby affecting the con-
tact area and resulting contact pressure. Running the tires at the mid to
lower end of the inflation pressure range for the particular tire will help
improve the surface texture by sealing the surface. Running the tire infla-
tion pressure higher increases the compactive effort of the roller. All the
tires should have the same ply rating and the same inflation pressure.

Adjusting Compactive Force

There are several ways to adjust the compactive force of a pneumatic-
tire roller. The first is to add ballast, which will increase the mass of the
roller. The second means of adjusting the compactive force is to increase
the air pressure in each of the tires per the tire inflation pressure recom-
mendations. Increasing air pressure reduces the contact area (footprint)
and increases the contact pressure of the roller. The third means of adjust-
ing the compactive effort is to adjust the speed of the roller Gust like the
steel-wheel roller). Adjusting the speed of the roller affects the dwell time
the tires have on the surface of the HMA.

Tire Pickup

When using pneumatic rollers, keeping the tires hot is the most effective
means of preventing pickup. Running the roller back and forth on a pre-
viously placed mat for at least 10 minutes will help warm the tires; once
paving has started, the roller can slowly work into the hot mix. If needed,
applying a small amount of nonfoaming detergent or water soluble release
agent on the wetting mat of a pneumatic-tire roller at the beginning of
rolling operations helps prevent the HMA from sticking to the tires before
they warm up. Do not use diesel or other petroleum fuels/solvents as a
release agent. Keeping pads and scrapers on each tire will prevent pickup
from getting worse if a tire does pick up in an area. If tires start to pick
up, move the roller off the mat, clean the tires carefully, then start again.
Skirting around tires helps minimize heat loss due to wind.

9.3.3 Vibratory Rollers

ing HMA. They may include one or two smooth-surface steel drums, as
shown in Figure 9.7, that have eccentric weights spinning around a shaft

HMA mixture but generally are not used in the vibratory mode when the
mat thickness is 1.5 inches (37.5 millimeters) or less to avoid fracturing the
aggregate and damaging the mixture. The compactive effort of vibratory
rollers can be adjusted for the mix type and base conditions on the project by
varying the frequency and amplitude. Spilt-drum vibratory rollers can also

lifts of HMA, but the use of dynamic force is what makes them so effective
when compared with static rollers. Frequency is expressed in vibrations or
impacts per minute, or hertz (Hz), and is a measure of the rotational speed
of the eccentric weight inside the drum of the roller. Typical frequencies of
rollers used on an HMA pavement range from 2,500 to over 4,000 vibrations
per minute (vpm) (42-67 hertz). The high-frequency drum movement puts
the aggregate particles in motion, allowing them to slide past one another
more easily under the weight of the drum. Mixes with high percentages
of aggregate with crushed faces benefit the most with vibratory rollers.
The general rule of thumb is to use highest-frequency setting available.
High frequency also allows the roller to be operated at a greater forward

speed, increasing the efficiency of the roller. The frequency of each drum
should be routinely checked using a reed tachometer.

Amplitude

Simply explained, amplitude is the up-and-down motion of the drum
that is caused as the eccentric weight spins inside the drum. The higher the
amplitude, the greater the vertical movement generated, and the greater the
force exerted on the HMA mix. The general approach is to use the lowest-
amplitude setting that will achieve density. However, amplitude is tied closely
to the thickness of the layer being placed. Higher-amplitude settings are
appropriate when lift thicknesses are 3 inches (75 millimeters) or greater.
Thicker layers can handle a higher amplitude. Care must be taken not to roll
too much with a vibratory roller because it is possible to overcompact. Too
much force can damage the HMA surface, crushing aggregate particles.

Impact Spacing

The vibratory roller should always be moving when the vibration system
is enabled. As the drum vibrates and the roller is moving, the drum is lifting
and dropping on the mat, creating small impacts in a uniform pattern. This
pattern is difficult to see except at night when the light angles are low. The
general rule of thumb for impact spacing is 10-14 impacts per foot (33-46
impacts per meter). This allows the entire surface to be impacted as the
roller travels in vibratory mode. A number of manufacturers have indicators
at the operator’s station to indicate whether the roller is being operated
properly. If the frequency of the roller is known, it can be divided by the
desired impact spacing to establish the speed of the roller. For example, if
a roller can develop 3500 vibrations per minute and a 10 impact-per-foot
impact spacing is desired, the optimum speed would be 3500/10 = 350 feet
per minute (107 meters per minute). In summary, impact spacing, vibration,
and amplitude can be used to adjust the compactive effort of a vibratory
roller. Slowing the forward speed of the roller decreases the impact spacing,
causing the number of impacts per foot to increase as shown in Figure 9.8.

9.3.4 Guidelines for Choosing a Roller

Any type or combination of rollers can be used to compact HMA mixes.
The key to roller selection is efficiency and effectiveness. A general rule of
thumb is that lighter rollers are better suited to more tender mixes placed
in thin layers, and heavier rollers are better suited to thicker, stiffer layers.
A light roller on an interstate highway would be neither efficient nor effec-
tive. Wider rollers can cover the mat quickly and are more efficient and
effective on mainline highways, but their use in a parking lot with many
turns and slope changes would be ineffective.

Thin Lifts (<1.5 inches compacted)
On thin lifts, most rolling operations can be completed with static steel
and/or vibratory rollers operating in the static mode. A rolling pattern

9.3.5 New Technology

Perhaps the most significant development in recent years has been
the increase in frequency available for “highway class” vibratory rollers.
Since 2000, most roller manufacturers have developed large tandem vibra-
tory rollers capable of operating at frequencies of over 3000 vibrations
per minute (50 hertz); some can operate at 4000 vibrations per minute
(67 hertz) or higher. This permits a single roller to cover a much larger
area while maintaining the minimum recommended impact spacing.

There have been substantial advances in equipment technology in the
compaction area, and this trend appears to be growing. Advances in sen-
sory and vibratory systems have resulted in the development of rollers
capable of automatically adjusting the amplitude based on the stiffness of
the HMA pavement. As the mix becomes denser (also stiffer) through the
compaction process, several manufacturers have equipment that adjusts
the intensity and direction of the amplitude to maximize efficiency. These
concepts and equipment were first developed for soil and embankment
compaction, and are often called Intelligent Compaction (C).

Rollers may have built-in temperature sensors to help the operator better
understand the mix as it cools. Technology exists to monitor and record the
stiffness of the pavement in relation to the location of the roller and to provide
printouts or color displays of areas needing additional coverage. Advances in
pneumiatic-tire technology have yielded tires that HMA doesn’t stick to and
stiffer tires that help focus the energy into the compaction process.

Vibratory pneumatic rollers have been developed to harness the advan-
tages of both types of vibration and pneumatic-tire compaction technology.
Continuing advances in compaction equipment seem certain.

4 Rolling Procedures

The goal of the compaction process is to achieve the required density in
as few coverages as necessary while preserving the smoothness and tex-
ture created by the paver. Rolling should start as soon as possible after the
HMA has been spread and can support the weight of the rollers without
lateral distortion.

9.4.1 Test Strip

A test strip should be built at the beginning of every project for each
mix or thickness to determine the proper roller pattern. The test strip
should be built at the same paving speed at which normal operation is
anticipated. The in-place density is checked after each coverage, using a
nondestructive gauge (nuclear or non-nuclear). A relationship between
number of coverages and density is determined while monitoring the
temperature of the mixture. This process helps determine the optimum
number of coverages for each roller in the rolling train and estimate the
dimensions of rolling zones for each roller. As shown in Figure 9.9, density
increases with each coverage, then peaks. The peak is relatively flat with
static steel-wheel rollers but very sharp with vibratory rollers. Care must
be taken to not go beyond the peak with vibratory rollers because the
density will decrease due to the mix being damaged.

Subsequently, during the compaction process, those involved must be
particularly observant and knowledgeable. A qualified quality-control
technician using a calibrated density gauge is invaluable in determining
the appropriate rolling pattern and adjusting the pattern as needed to
ensure that the target density level is achieved.

9.4.2 Determining Roller Requirements

The width of paving should be considered when selecting rollers. If a
wide pavement layer is constructed using narrow rollers, the rollers will
need to make many passes to completely cover the surface. This takes
time during which the mix will cool and differential density can result.
Generally, the width of the pavement is no more than three times the
width of the roller. Often the contractor selects two wide rollers operating
slightly offset from each other so that they effectively cover the width of
the mat each time they make a pass over the mat.

9.4.3 Lift Thickness

The thickness of the lift being placed will have an effect on the time
available for compaction and on the types of equipment used to compact
the HMA pavement. If the lift is less than 1.5 inches (30 millimeters) after
rolldown (compaction), it can be compacted with combinations of static
steel-wheel rollers, vibratory rollers operating in static mode, and/or pneu-
matic rollers. Thin layers tend to cool quickly, requiring attention to the
compaction process so that the desired level of density is achieved. Thicker
lifts of HMA tend to hold heat longer, allowing more time for compaction,
and may require more effort to compact due to the thickness. More rollers
may be needed, including vibratory rollers operating at higher amplitude
settings, and combinations of vibratory, pneumatic, and static rollers.

9.4.5 Sequence of Roller Operations

The number of rollers required on a project is related to the type of
mix, the production rates, and the desired density levels. It is impossible
to establish one roller pattern that will achieve density on all mixes. How-
ever, the generally accepted sequence has three phases: breakdown or
initial rolling, intermediate rolling, and finish rolling. Breakdown rolling
provides most of the increase in density during the compaction operation.
Intermediate rolling further densifies and seals the surface. Finish rolling
removes roller marks and other blemishes left from previous rolling and
minimally increases density.

Breakdown Rolling

Breakdown rolling is usually accomplished with steel-wheel rollers.
Vibratory or static-weight tandem rollers are recommended. Pneumatic-tire
rollers, however, have also been used successfully for breakdown rolling,
but normally should be limited to base courses.

The weight of the roller used for breakdown rolling depends to a large
degree on the temperature, thickness, and stability of the mix being placed.
Generally, a roller weighing 8-12 tonnes (7300-10,900 kilograms) is used
for this operation.

In general, it’s best to start the rolling operation on the low side of the
mat, which is usually the outside of the lane being paved, and progress
toward the high side. The reason is that these HMA mixtures, when hot,
are not as stable as current mix types and tend to migrate toward the
low side of the mat under the action of the roller. If rolling is started on
the high side, this migration is much more pronounced than if rolling
progresses from the low side. When adjoining lanes are placed, the same

the target values. A simple pattern will be more consistently followed than

Intermediate Rolling

Secondary or intermediate rolling should closely follow breakdown roll-
ing while the HMA mix is still hot and compactable.

Pneumatic-tire or vibratory tandem rollers may be used for intermediate
rolling. Tire contact pressures should be as high as possible without caus-
ing displacement of mix that cannot be remedied in the final rolling.

Pneumatic-tire rolling should be continuous after breakdown rolling until
all the mix placed has been thoroughly compacted. Turning of pneumatic-
tire rollers on the fresh mix should only be permitted where it does not
cause scuffing or undue displacement.

Some mixes exhibit what has been termed a tender zone at an interme-
diate temperature range of 230°-190°F (110°-88°C). This is most common
with coarse-grade mixtures that may retain a slight amount of moisture
after production. Rolling on the mix in this temperature zone with a steel
drum will cause the mix to laterally displace or be shoved to the point of
tearing the mat. If this phenomenon occurs, compaction with steel-wheel
rollers should cease until the mixture is sufficiently stable to support the
roller without shoving. Pneumatic-tire rollers, which apply less contact
pressure than steel-wheel rollers, are less likely to seriously deform the sur-
face under these conditions. Additional densification can also be achieved
with the finish roller if it is sufficiently heavy and operated slowly.

Vibratory rollers—of proper static weight, vibration frequency, and
amplitude—are used to meet required densities with fewer coverages
than with static-weight tandem or pneumatic-tire rollers (or combinations
of the two). Regardless of the type of rollers used, the rolling pattern
should be developed in the same manner as for the initial, or breakdown,
rolling. This pattern should be continued until the desired density is
obtained.

Finish Rolling

Finish rolling is done primarily for the aesthetic improvement of the
surface. It should be accomplished with steel-wheel tandems operating in
static mode (without vibration) while the material is still warm enough for
removal of roller marks.

Compaction is the last step that will determine the performance of the
HMA. There is a seemingly endless variety of roller patterns to accomplish
this task. It is a critical function, and finding the right pattern for produc-
ing consistent density results while maintaining smoothness and texture is
a rewarding and long-lasting effort.

10.10.2 Monitoring Performance
Parameters

Specific methods used to monitor performance are included in the spec-
ifications, along with requirements for when and how often measurements
are to be taken. The owner generally makes these measurements, and it
is critical that clear acceptance criteria and detailed descriptions of conse-

10.10.3 Dispute Resolution

Many warranty specifications have alternative dispute resolution terms
for resolving disagreements. A method commonly used involves selec-
tion of a panel to evaluate and render an opinion on the specific item of
the disagreement. The parties with the disagreement first must meet and
clearly establish what the points of disagreement are. This keeps the panel
on-track with the correct issues and reduces the amount of time needed to
come to resolution. Each party selects one person who is independent and
unaware of the dispute but has special knowledge or expertise relative to
the issues disputed. A geotechnical engineer may be selected, for example,
if the dispute is related to soil conditions. After each party selects one
person, the two selected panel members meet and select a third. All three
panel members agree to be unbiased and their costs are shared evenly.
Information describing the disagreement and the position of each party
is presented to the panel, which has an established time for evaluating
the information. The panel then renders a decision, including any needed
corrective action and an allocation of cost responsibility.

11.1 Pavement Management
Concepts

11.1.1 Introduction

Transportation agencies have made significant investments in their
pavement assets when both the costs of original construction and ongoing
maintenance are considered. These assets are a vital part of the nation’s
economy, providing important links to markets, schools, medical facilities,
and places of employment. Demand on this road network continues to grow
with time, placing more importance on maintaining suitable pavement con-
ditions at a time when increasing loads are causing pavements to deteriorate
more rapidly.

Faced with these types of challenges, managers of transportation agen-
cies are making use of tools that provide the type of information needed
to support their decision-making processes. A Pavement Management Sys-
tem (PMS) is an example of this type of tool. The American Association
of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) defines PMS as:
“A pavement management system is a set of tools or methods that assist
decision-makers in finding optimum strategies for providing, evaluating,
and maintaining pavements in a serviceable condition over a period of
time.” A PMS uses reliable pavement inventory and condition information to
help identify and prioritize pavement maintenance and rehabilitation needs
within budget or other constraints that may exist. A PMS may or may not be
computerized, but a computerized PMS holds a significant advantage in the
ability to manage and analyze large databases of pavement inventory infor-
mation. Since most computerized PMSs are able to forecast future pavement
conditions, an agency can quickly evaluate the impact of today’s investment
decisions on the long-term conditions of the road network. Consequently,
agencies can use the information from a PMS to make more cost-effective
maintenance and rehabilitation decisions, which results in a more efficient
use of agency resources.

11.1.2 The Pavement Management
Concept

Pavement management concepts can be used to manage the maintenance
and rehabilitation of a road network by cost-effectively using a mixture
of low-cost maintenance treatments on roads in good condition and more
expensive rehabilitation treatments on roads with advanced levels of dete-
rioration. This “mix of fixes” results in continuing efforts to reduce the num-
ber of roads in poor condition (which are very expensive to fix) while
maintaining good roads in good condition for as long as possible.

Life-Cycle Cost Analysis

Determining the most cost-effective maintenance or rehabilitation
strategy for an existing pavement can be a daunting task. However, a life-
cycle cost analysis (LCCA) provides a means for comparing competing treat-
ment strategies over an analysis period of 20 to 40 years. In an LCCA, all
costs expected to be incurred over the life of a pavement are identified and
converted to a single point in time using economic equations that represent
the time value of money. Because all costs are converted to a single point
in time, different treatment strategies with different performance lives can
easily be compared. Additionally, an LCCA allows an agency to consider
both agency costs and user costs in the same analysis. Agency costs are
represented by direct costs to the agency for construction, maintenance, and
rehabilitation. User costs, which are not always considered in an LCCA, rep-
resent the costs borne by the users under each scenario. For example, user
costs might include differences in vehicle operating expenses, crash costs,
or delay costs associated with the strategies being considered.

Condition Survey

A pavement management system uses the results of pavement condition
surveys to help identify current and future maintenance and rehabilitation
needs. All recommendations from the pavement management system are
based on the results of the pavement condition survey so the importance
of this component cannot be underestimated. The results of the survey are
used to report current pavement conditions (usually in terms of a condition
index), to forecast future conditions, and to determine appropriate mainte-
nance and rehabilitation strategies.

The next section of this chapter discusses the different types of pave-
ment condition surveys that are used to support a pavement management
analysis. These techniques range from windshield surveys in which a sub-
jective rating is assigned by a rater to represent the current pavement con-
dition to more objective survey approaches in which distress type, severity,
and quantity are recorded. Many agencies have begun using automated
vehicles to collect pavement condition information. These vehicles oper-
ate at near-traffic speeds, collecting digital images of the pavement surface
and using lasers to record pavement surface irregularities like roughness,
faulting, and rutting.

Regardless of which approach is used, it is important that the survey pro-
cedures are followed consistently from one survey to another survey (and
from one rater to another rater) and that they represent actual conditions.
In general, objective condition surveys are more repeatable than subjective
rating approaches, but they are often more labor intensive. Since it is impor-
tant that pavement condition surveys are updated at least every three years,
each agency must carefully evaluate the costs associated with each pavement
condition survey procedure to ensure that the approach selected matches the
resources available to support this activity.

Database

The inventory and condition information is stored in a pavement manage-
ment database so it is available for use in generating reports and evaluating
different maintenance and rehabilitation strategies. Databases can take a vari-
ety of forms, but relational databases are most commonly used in pavement
management today.

where program recommendations for pavement maintenance and repair are

optimized within existing constraints, such as funding limitations. The most
common methods of optimizing pavement maintenance and rehabilitation
needs are described below:

° Ranking. A ranking formula is established giving higher weight to proj-
ects that reflect the agency’s priorities. For instance, higher volume roads
may have a higher priority than lower volume facilities.

° Benefit/Cost Analysis. Under this approach, the benefit associated with
each feasible treatment (usually defined in terms of additional perfor-
mance) is divided by the cost of the treatment to determine a benefit/cost
ratio. Projects are prioritized so that those with the highest benefit/cost
ratio are funded first.

To support the analysis, pavement performance models and treatment rules
must be established. Pavement performance models describe the deterioration
patterns of different types of pavements, using historical pavement condition
data when available. In addition, treatment rules must be defined to describe
the conditions under which each treatment is considered feasible and the
expected cost of the treatment. For instance, a treatment rule might be estab-
lished to apply an HMA overlay on roads in fair condition. These rules are
used to identify all feasible treatment options for existing and predicted con-
ditions during the analysis period. Finally, any budget constraints that limit
the number of projects that can be funded each year must be defined.

Reporting Tools

The results of a pavement management analysis, and the information
contained in the database, can be reported in a number of different formats,
including text, tables, and graphics. Many agencies link their pavement man-
agement databases to geographic information systems (GIS) or other map
packages to visually display the information. GIS displays of pavement
management information are very effective tools when reporting pavement
conditions to decision makers, elected officials, or the public.

Differences Based on Agency Size

Pavement management systems vary considerably in terms of the capa-
bilities they provide, the user-friendliness of the program, and the degree
to which the program can be customized to fit the particular needs of the
agency. Both public domain and proprietary software packages are avail-
able. In general, public domain programs cost less to install but have limited
capabilities and/or flexibility in meeting the needs of individual agencies. On
the other hand, proprietary software packages are typically more expensive
but also more sophisticated, with the flexibility needed to be tailored to meet
the needs of an individual agency. When selecting pavement management
software, an agency should first define its needs and then select a software
package that meets those needs. Otherwise, the agency may incur expenses
for features that are not needed and will not be used. For a pavement manage-
ment system to be useful to an agency, it should addresses the agency’s needs
and be maintained over time according to the available resources.

11.1.4 Pavement Management Analysis
Levels

Pavement management systems are used in transportation agencies to
support decisions at three different levels: the project level, the network level,
and the strategic level. Each of these levels is described further in this section
of the chapter.

Project-Leveil Analysis

As the name implies, a project-level analysis involves decisions regard-
ing the maintenance and rehabilitation of specific pavement sections. At
this level, detailed pavement evaluations are conducted to determine the
extent of any deterioration present, to identify the probable cause of the
deterioration, and to design the appropriate treatment to correct the deficien-
cies. Pavement management systems can support decisions at this level by
providing information on the deterioration rate of a pavement section, main-
tenance and construction histories, or other factors that may influence the
selection of the appropriate repair strategy.

Network-Level Analysis

Pavement management systems are most commonly used at the network
level, where the maintenance and rehabilitation needs of the entire road net-
work are considered with respect to the amount of funding available. At the
network level, the agency’s maintenance and rehabilitation needs are priori-
tized so the most cost-effective use of available funding can be determined.
Pavement needs are typically considered over a multiyear period and differ-
ent maintenance and rehabilitation strategies are considered in the pavement
management system. The initial recommendations from the pavement man-
agement system provide general guidelines for the type of work needed and
possible locations where the work should be performed. The development of
final work plans is usually an iterative process that involves moving neighbor-
ing projects from one year to another, or combining similar projects, to gain
economy of scale. The work list is further developed at the project level in
order to develop final project work plans and treatment designs.

Strategic-Level Analysis

In recent years, pavement management professionals have recognized the
contributions of pavement management information to support an agency’s
strategic decisions. At this level, individuals such as governmental officials,
upper agency management, and the engineers in charge of pavement main-
tenance, and bridge management all make decisions that play a part in deter-
mining pavement performance targets, establishing needed funding levels to
achieve the performance targets, distributing funds among regions or districts,
and establishing a pavement preservation policy. When strategic-level deci-
sions must be made, there is a direct need to be able to utilize the information
from the pavement management system in order to demonstrate the conse-
quences on the long-term pavement condition of various treatment strategies,
funding levels, or performance targets.

11.1.5 Role of Pavement Management in
Pavement Preservation

One cost-effective strategy that an agency may consider for preserving
its pavement network is a pavement preservation program that includes
the use of low-cost preventive maintenance treatments to preserve the
functional characteristics of a pavement in relatively good condition.

Definitions
The Federal Highway Administration’s (FHWA) Pavement Preservation
Expert Task Group defines a pavement preservation program as follows:

A program employing a network level, long-term strategy that enhances
pavement performance by using an integrated, cost-effective set of
practices that extend pavement life, improve safety and meet motorist
expectations.

These programs incorporate the use of preventive maintenance treat-
ments, which have been defined by the AASHTO’s Standing Committee on
Highways as follows:

A planned strategy of cost-effective treatments to an existing roadway
system and its appurtenances that preserves the system, retards future
deterioration, and maintains or improves the functional condition of
the system (without significantly increasing the structural capacity).

Preventive maintenance treatments are typically applied to roads in
relatively good condition before extensive amounts of deterioration are
present. Examples of preventive maintenance treatments include crack
sealing, chip seals, microsurfacing, and thin hot mix overlays.

Demonstrating Benefits of Pavement Preservation
Agencies that have initiated pavement preservation programs have used
their pavement management systems to illustrate the expected benefits in
terms of overall network conditions. For instance, the predicted overall net-
work conditions associated with a pavement preservation program can be
compared to another program in which a worst-first strategy is employed
(where the worst roads in the network are fixed first), as illustrated in
Figure 11.3.
The benefits of pavement preservation can also be demonstrated using
the following techniques:
* A summary of treatment costs needed to achieve a targeted condition
level using different strategies
° The expected distribution of network conditions in different condition
categories (such as good, fair, and poor) under different preservation
strategies
° A life-cycle cost comparison of the anticipated agency costs associated
with a pavement preservation strategy as compared to a more traditional
rehabilitation strategy

11.2 Pavement Evaluation

11.2.1 Introduction

A pavement management system relies on objective, repeatable pavement
condition information to determine current and future maintenance and
rehabilitation needs. Therefore, conducting a meaningful pavement evalu-
ation is one of the first steps in the identification of feasible maintenance
and rehabilitation strategies. Two types of pavement evaluations are nor-
mally conducted as part of an agency’s pavement management practices:
a functional evaluation and a structural evaluation. A functional evaluation
considers the surface characteristics of a road, including certain types of
cracking, surface smoothness, noise, and surface friction characteristics.
A structural evaluation is used to determine the ability of the pavement
structure to carry traffic loadings. A structural evaluation typically requires
detailed information about pavement layer thicknesses, paving layer mate-
rial properties, subgrade support conditions, traffic, and the response of
the pavement to loading. Nondestructive testing (NDT) using falling weight
deflectometer (FWD) devices is one of the most common approaches to
determining the response of the pavement under traffic loadings. More
detailed information on methods used to evaluate pavement functional
and structural characteristics follows.

11.2.2 Functional Characteristics and
Evaluation

Pavement surface characteristics are typically incorporated into pave-
ment condition surveys to support the types of analyses conducted with
a pavement management system. Three of the most common methods of
evaluating the functional condition of a pavement are discussed here.

Pavement Distress

By collecting pavement distress information, an agency is able to
determine the relative amount of pavement deterioration present and
the appropriate level of repair that is needed to correct the deficiencies.
Many different types of pavement distress are included in a pavement
condition survey, as discussed further in Section 11.3. Some types of pave-
ment distress (such as transverse and block cracking) reflect deteriora-
tion mechanisms that are more functional in nature, such as deterioration
caused by climate or material properties. Other types of pavement distress
(including alligator cracking or rutting) indicate load-related deterioration.

Manual Surveys

Pavement condition surveys may be conducted using either manual or
automated approaches. In a manual survey, individuals collect the pavement
distress information by walking the pavement surface or by viewing the pave-
ment surface from a slow-moving vehicle traveling along the shoulder. Man-
ual surveys are more commonly used in areas without heavy traffic volumes,
which might hinder the data collection process or impede the safety of the
survey crews. An example of a manual pavement condition survey proce-
dure for low-volume asphait-surfaced roads is provided as Appendix E in the
Asphalt Institute’s MS-17, Asphalt Overlays for Highway and Street Rehabili-
tation. A copy is also available in the Asphalt Institute’s Information Series,
IS-169, A Pavement Rating System for Low-Volume Asphalt Roads.

Automated Surveys

Where traffic conditions must be considered, some agencies conduct
automated surveys using data collection vehicles that capture digital or video
images of the pavement surface at prevailing speed limits; these images are
later processed in the office. In addition to addressing some of the safety con-
siderations associated with the conduct of pavement condition surveys, many
of the vehicles used to conduct automated surveys are also equipped with
sensors to record rutting, roughness, and faulting measures. These vehicles
may also include a camera for taking panoramic shots along the roadway,
which can be useful for monitoring the condition of signs or other road-
way hardware. Some devices are equipped with special lighting to eliminate
problems caused by shadows or to facilitate surveys conducted at night. An
example of an automated data collection van is provided in Figure 11.4.

The images captured during the survey process are typically post-processed
in an office. Sensor data are usually processed in real time and reported in
accordance with guidelines established by the agency. Distress data may be
processed. automatically using crack recognition software capable of recog-
nizing and quantifying variations in grayscale that relate to the cracks on the
pavement surface. Alternatively, the images may be viewed at a workstation
where distress details are manually reviewed at a computer and distress quan-
tities are recorded. This latter approach is referred to as a semi-automated
method of distress processing.

The use of this technology has some disadvantages. The primary disad-
vantage is that it is difficult to discern characteristics associated with verti-
cal displacement when using this technology. For example, it is difficult
to determine whether there is a vertical displacement along a crack using
this technology. Some pavement condition survey procedures use verti-
cal displacement along a crack as a means of determining crack severity.
It is‘also difficult to collect accurate sensor data in urban environments
that prevent the equipment from operating at constant speeds. Because
most agencies contract automated data collection services, an agency must
also develop procedures to ensure the quality of the data collection and

Sampling ;

Because of the resources required to conduct a pavement condition survey
of an entire pavement network, some agencies utilize sampling techniques
to determine the condition of each pavement section. Agencies that use this
approach typically divide each pavement section into inspection units of a
predetermined size, such as 2,500 square feet for asphalt-surfaced roads.
A representative number of inspection units (or samples) are selected using
statistical analyses. In general, a higher degree of sampling is required when
a higher level of confidence is required or when there is considerable vari-
ability in the overall pavement condition.

Ride Quality

The longitudinal profile of a road provides an indication of the road’s
tide quality. Early measures of ride quality were based on the subjective
ratings of passenger comfort within a vehicle traveling at posted speeds.
Since that time, equipment has been developed to measure ride quality,
which is typically reported in terms of an International Roughness Index
(IRD). Ride quality, which is also referred to as roughness or smoothness,
is a popular measure of a road’s functional condition because it represents
a condition measure that the public recognizes. The measurement of ride
quality is common on highways and other high-speed facilities.

Types of Equipment

Most modern pavement-profiling devices measure vertical acceleration
in response to the road and provide a pavement profile. Among the instru-
ments on these vehicles are accelerometers that provide a horizontal plane
of reference and sensors (laser, acoustic, or infrared) to measure the devia-
tions of the pavement surface from the horizontal plane. These vehicles
operate at near traffic speeds and are often outfitted with rut bars and
cameras so that distress information can be collected at the same time. An
example of this type of vehicle is provided in Figure 11.4.

Testing Approach

For acceptance testing on new construction, profilographs have been
used in the past. Today, more agencies are using IRI for acceptance pur-
poses. Profilographs produce a trace of the pavement surface, which is
then compared with acceptable limits established by the agency. More
information on the standard test method and equipment used to measure
the pavement profile is available in ASTM E 1274, “Standard Test Method
for Measuring Pavement Roughness Using a Profilograph.”

Surface Friction

Adequate skid resistance, or surface friction, is an important functional
characteristic that influences directional control and stopping ability on
a wet or dry pavement. Most agencies become aware of surface friction
problems during inclement weather, when the potential for hydroplaning
(in which the vehicle tire has lost contact with the pavement surface and
is actually riding on a thin film of water) is high. However, safety can also
be compromised on dry pavement surfaces where surface friction is low.

Surface friction is a measure of the resistance provided by the pavement
surface to the sliding of tires when they are prevented from rotating. In
addition to pavement surface characteristics, surface friction can also be
influenced by factors such as tire wear, vehicle speed, tire pressure, and
the presence of water.

Surface friction information is typically collected as part of a safety
program to reduce crash potential. However, agencies are beginning to
incorporate surface friction measures into their pavement management
systems to help identify areas that might benefit from treatments that
restore pavement surface friction characteristics.

11.2.0 Structural Characteristics and
Evaluation

A pavement structural evaluation is used to estimate the structural
adequacy of an existing pavement to determine whether the pave-
ment can sufficiently meet projected traffic loadings. In situations when
the pavement is not adequate to meet projected loadings, the results of
the structural evaluation can provide the information needed to design the
appropriate treatment.

Because of the level of detail normally associated with this type of eval-
uation, structural evaluations are typically conducted as part of a project-
level investigation. This may include more extensive pavement condition
surveys conducted in conjunction with destructive testing (such as coring
and boring) and with nondestructive testing using pavement deflection
devices. Pavement deflection testing does not disturb the underlying pave-
ment structure and does not require pavement materials to be removed for
laboratory testing. However, it does require accurate pavement thickness
information at each test location so coring is often conducted to verify
pavement layer thicknesses.

Pavement Deflection Testing

Pavement deflection testing measures the response of the pavement to
a load in terms of the resultant pavement deflection. The deflection data
are used to backcalculate in situ material properties, to calculate pavement
responses to load, and as inputs to other pavement analyses. By plot-
ting maximum deflection measurements with road stations, areas of high
deflections can be easily delineated to define project or subproject limits.
The deflection information can also be used in deflection-based overlay
design procedures.

Types of Equipment

Several types of pavement deflection devices are available, including
impulse deflection devices (such as the falling weight deflectometer),
steady-state dynamic deflection devices (such as the Dynaflect), and static
deflection devices (such as the Benkelman Beam). The falling weight
deflectometer (FWD) is the most commonly used deflection device for
assessing pavement responses.

The FWD is capable of simulating in magnitude and duration the
dynamic loads applied to a pavement by wheel loads. A dynamic load is
applied by dropping a set of weights onto a circular load plate. A range of
load magnitudes can be achieved by varying the number of weights used
and the heights from which they are dropped. The actual load magnitude
is measured using a load cell within the load plate, and the resulting
deflections are measured by transducers located at the center of the load

deflection device is the Benkelman Beam, which was developed in the
early 1950s at the WASHO Road Test. The beam is placed on the pavement
surface with its probe located between the dual tires of a loaded truck.
The rebound of the pavement as the truck moves away is measured and
reported. This device has also been replaced by the FWD.

Testing Approach

Pavement deflection testing requires traffic control because the test-
ing device must be stopped at each test location. On flexible pavements,
deflection testing is typically conducted in the outer wheelpath, but a
testing configuration may test both the inner and outer wheelpath at
staggered intervals. Additional information on recommended FWD test-
ing frequencies is provided in ASTM D 4694, “Standard Test Method for
Deflections with a Falling-Weight-Type Impulse Load Device.”

11.2.4 Use of Pavement Condition
information

The results of a functional pavement evaluation provide the information
needed for the pavement management system to identify and prioritize
maintenance and rehabilitation needs. Typically, pavement distress and
roughness information are used to calculate pavement condition indexes
that can be used to compare the condition of one pavement section with
another and to identify the level of repair that might be needed to address
the deficiencies identified. Over time, pavement condition information
helps establish rates of pavement deterioration that can be used to fore-
cast future pavement conditions under different funding scenarios.

Pavement Condition Indexes

To facilitate the use of pavement distress information in a pavement man-
agement system, pavement condition indexes are calculated based on the
type, severity, and quantity of distress present. Pavement condition indexes
typically range in value from 0 to 10 or 0 to 100, with the highest values
corresponding to pavements in excellent condition. Points are subtracted
from a perfect score based on the distresses observed in the field to cal-
culate the index. The complexity of the pavement condition index calcula-
tion varies depending on the type of survey being conducted.

Pavement condition indexes can be calculated for each type of distress
present or a composite index can be used that aggregates all of the dis-
tress into one index representing the overall condition of a pavement
section. Some agencies use a combination of these two types of indexes;
the individual indexes are used to identify appropriate maintenance and
rehabilitation treatments and the composite index is used to report the
overall network conditions.

In local agencies, a composite index is most commonly used. For exam-
ple, the pavement rating system for low-volume asphalt pavements that
is included in several Asphalt Institute publications requires the rater to
assign a value between 0 and 5 for all functional distress present (such as
longitudinal and transverse cracking) and 0 to 10 for distress representing
structural forms of deterioration (such as alligator cracking and rutting) or
other important deterioration mechanisms (such as deficient drainage or
poor ride quality). The defects associated with each distress are summed
and subtracted from 100 to determine the condition rating for a pavement
section.

A more detailed approach to calculating a composite index was
developed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for use in its Micro PAVER
pavement management system. This approach, which is documented in
ASTM D 6433, “Standard Practice for Roads and Parking Lots Pavement
Condition Index Surveys,” uses a similar approach, but separate deduction
values have been developed for each distress type, severity, and quantity
calculation.

Reporting Current Conditions

The most common use of pavement condition information is to report
overall network conditions. This information is reported in a number of
different formats depending on the needs of the intended recipients. For
some individuals, a report that presents the overall network conditions by
functional class, surface type, or some other delineator may be appropriate.
Several agencies regularly publish reports on the state of the road network
after each data collection cycle. Other agencies publish limited pavement
condition information on their web sites.

One of the most effective ways of communicating pavement condition
information involves displaying pavement condition information on color-
coded maps. This approach has a strong visual impact, especially when
roads in poor condition are shown in red. This method of displaying pave-
ment condition information is especially useful for presentations to elected
officials and upper management.

Forecasting Future Conditions

Once a pavement condition index has been developed, statistical tools
can be used to develop pavement performance models that reflect the rate
at which the pavements deteriorate over time. The inclusion of models that
forecast future pavement condition is one of the most important factors
differentiating a pavement management system from a pavement database.
Prediction models allow an agency to forecast future pavement condi-
tions under any number of budget scenarios or under different treatment
strategies so that the long-term impacts of these options can be more fully
understood and compared.

11.3 Pavement Distress Surveys

Whether part of a formal pavement management system or a project-level
pavement evaluation to select an appropriate maintenance or rehabilitation
treatment, pavement distress surveys provide information on the type and the
extent of deterioration present and the primary mechanisms causing the dis-
tress. These considerations are both very important in selecting and designing
an improvement. Pavement distress-surveys may be conducted in conjunction
with other testing, such as destructive or nondestructive testing.

11.3.1 Distress Survey Approaches

The previous section introduced methods of conducting both functional
and structural pavement evaluations. Pavement distress surveys are the
most common type of functional pavement evaluation used by highway
and public works agencies. As part of a network-level evaluation, a dis-
tress survey may be conducted on selected samples to provide an overall
condition for each pavement section in the network. As part of a project-
level investigation in which a rehabilitation treatment is being designed,
more detailed distress surveys are typically conducted. At the project level,
distress maps showing distress location, severity, and extent are often
created. Distress maps are time-consuming to prepare but provide an
excellent way of estimating repair quantities or marking the location of
drainage or geometric features. An example of a distress map is shown in
Figure 11.8.

Depending on the types of distress survey being conducted, the following
types of equipment may be required:

° A distress identification manual

° A map or utility layout sheet showing section limits

¢ A measuring wheel

¢ Survey forms and/or mapping sheets

° A clipboard

° Pencils

° A straightedge or string line

° Spray paint (to mark limits and section identifiers)

° A hand calculator

¢ A camera or other recording device

Pavement distress surveys are typically conducted using crews of at
least two people so that while one individual measures distress quantities,
the other serves as a recorder. Having two crew members is also important
for safety reasons because survey crews often have to mingle with traffic.

To help ensure the reliability of a pavement distress survey and to minimize
variations in survey results between raters, survey crews should follow the
established survey procedures as closely as possible. Detailed distress surveys
require raters to correctly differentiate between distress types and to use pre-
scribed definitions outlined in a distress identification manual to distinguish
between different severity levels. Distress identification manuals also provide
guidance on how to measure different types of distress and often provide tips
for recording a distress that is difficult to discern in the field.

Before beginning a pavement distress survey, each rater should be
trained in accordance with the distress identification procedures to mini-
mize discrepancies in the field. In addition to being able to identify each
distress type included in the survey procedures, the raters should be famil-
iar enough with the procedures to know whether all severities associated
with a particular distress should be collected or whether only the highest
severity is recorded. Other details, such as how distress in a patched area
should be handled, ought to be discussed before the start of the survey.
These considerations are important for maintaining consistency in the
survey data since raters may differ in what they consider a “good” road.

Characteristics of an Effective Survey
An effective pavement distress survey should have the following
characteristics:
° There should be little interpretation expected of the raters in the field.
° The methodology should be objective and repeatable.
° It should minimize incursions with traffic.
° It should provide a sufficient level of detail so the agency can estimate
maintenance and rehabilitation needs accurately.
It should include a method of estimating distress quantities.
It should differentiate distress severity levels in situations where distress
severity changes dramatically over a relatively short period of time.
° The importance of the distress in determining the appropriate repair
option should be factored into the calculation of the pavement condition
index.

The driving force behind this design innovation in the 1980s was a desire
to create a drum-mix facility that was more environmentally friendly by
introducing the new liquid-asphalt binder outside of the main gas stream
used to dry and heat the aggregate materials. This reduced the discharge
of very small (1-5 micron) hydrocarbon particles in the stack gases, which
sometimes created visible emissions or odors from the drum-mix facilities.
These new drum-mixers eliminate this problem.

Figures 7.23 through 7.26 show the different styles of drum-mixers
commonly found in the field at this time. It should be noted that many
batch-plant operators are now converting their plants from batch plants
to drum-mixers by replacing the tower with a mixing drum or continuous
pugmill mixer or by bypassing the tower and using a small mixing drum
mounted beside the tower, resulting in a hybrid system known as a com-
bination plant. This allows operators to use the same dryer and baghouse
to dry the material. As noted previously in this chapter, the cold-feed
bins were probably already converted to belt feeders with variable-speed
drives. By installing a continuous mixing device, a drum-mix liquid asphalt
pumping and metering system, and drum-mix controls, the batch-plant
operator now has all the advantages of drum-mix operation. Of primary
interest to producers is the ability to run at the production rate possible
with the dryer and not be limited by the capabilities of the screening unit,
being able to run higher percentages of RAP, and reducing maintenance
costs, thereby becoming more competitive in the marketplace. Having
consistently sized aggregates available makes this conversion possible.

7.4.2 Aggregate Gradation Control in a
Drum-Mixer

Controlling aggregate gradation with a drum-mixer is a much simpler
process than with a batch plant. The individual cold-feed bins are calibrated
for their speed/output function, and the final mix gradation is controlled

342 Manufaciure of Hot Mix Asphalt

=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 377 ===
by proportioning these individual bins to the target mix formula. This,
of course, depends on the availability of consistently sized individual
aggregates from the aggregate supplier. If the individual aggregates are
not consistent in gradation, then a consistent hot mix cannot be produced
with a drum-mixer. Drum-mixers and the drum-mixing process depend on
the availability of consistent aggregates to make high-quality mix.

The output from the cold-feed bins is really controlled in two ways. The
primary adjustment is the variable-speed motor driving the belt feeder. In
addition to this, the bins are fitted with adjustable gates. This procedure
allows the producer to control the maximum and minimum flows from
the bin. Some mixes require a large quantity of one material—in this case,
the gate is opened wide. Some mixes require a small quantity of another
material, and in this case the bin gate might be lowered. Variable-speed
motors can be inconsistent in their flow at very low rates. They can also
reach their maximum speed at the 95 percent, 96 percent, or 97 percent
range. The most linear range is typically between 10 percent and 90 per-
cent speed. Some are linear between 5 percent and 95 percent speed. It
is best practice, therefore, to use the adjustable gate to ensure that the
motor-drive combination is functioning in the 5-95 percent or 10-90 per-
cent range at all times. Between these speed ranges, if output is charted to
speed, the line usually has a consistent and predictable slope.

With a drum-mix plant, the aggregate belt scale is used for blending the
asphalt and other ingredients to the combined-aggregate flow. The belt
scale is the device that measures the combined-aggregate flow. The pres-
ence of a belt scale makes it very easy to calibrate the feeders. The flow
of the material from each bin can be easily determined in tons per hour
at different flow rates by simply allowing the aggregate material from the
cold-feed bin to flow over the belt scale and into a truck. Timed runs can
also be used, and the flow rate calculated, but using the belt scale is very
simple. Virtually all plants use this technique for calibrating the feeders.
The aggregate flow and gradation is controlled volumetrically by using
predetermined flow output for different speed ranges and then back-
calculating the required speed for each feeder after determining the flow
required from each feeder. Executing the feeder calibration process prop-
erly is critical for success with a drum-mixer. Feeder calibration should
also be checked and re-verified on a regular basis.

7.4.3 Asphalt Content Control in a
Drum-Mixer
As indicated above, the belt scale is used to indicate total aggregate flow

from the cold feed bins. This is the first step in asphalt content control with
a drum-mixer. Once the total aggregate flow is determined, the required

MS-4 345

=== ORIGINAL PDF PAGE 5 ===
=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 380 ===
asphalt flow is calculated based on the mix design. The plant control
system then regulates the asphalt flow to the required rate.

The asphalt pumping and metering system on a drum-mix plant is a con-
tinuously operating unit. It pumps liquid asphalt continuously and measures
the result of that flow with a meter. It can increase or decrease the flow
dynamically with the plant’s automation.

There are two key equipment elements to this process—the asphalt flow
control mechanism and the asphalt meter or measuring device.

Several types of flow control mechanisms have been used over the years,
but all manufacturers now favor a variable-speed motor on the asphalt pump
for this purpose. If more asphalt is required, the pump is accelerated. If less
asphalt is needed, the pump is slowed. Most producers have converted their
plants to this technique because it eliminates the maintenance on the wear
parts of the previous systems and is generally considered trouble-free.

Figure 7.30 illustrates how this process works. The belt scale reports the
wet aggregate flow to the control system. The control system will adjust the
wet scale value for the moisture in the material, based on the aggregate-
moisture data entered by the operator. Entering the proper moisture quantity
is important and is typically done by entering the moistures of the individual
materials into a “moisture screen.” The control system then calculates the
combined moisture based on the mix recipe and the individual material
moistures, and removes this combined moisture percentage from the wet
weight to arrive at a dry weight. The plant control assumes the dryer will dry
the material adequately and therefore uses this calculated dry weight signal
for proportioning the liquid-asphalt binder. Based on the required asphalt
percentage in the mix, which comes from the mix formula, the control sys-
tem calculates the required asphalt flow. It then sends a signal to the flow-
control mechanism, which more often than not is a variable-speed motor,
and then reads the result of its action by reading the asphalt flow meter. If

Changeable delay times are built into the flow-control circuits. When the
plant is set up, tests are done to determine the length of the delay time
between the belt scale reading and the aggregate material reaching the
asphalt injection point for that plant. Therefore, changes in the aggregate
scale do not cause an instantaneous change in the asphalt flow. The plant
control system has delay times set up not only for the asphalt flow but also
for each cold-feed bin and for every other ingredient. These flow delays are
very important for a continuous process. When changes to production are
made, mix integrity must be maintained, so the delay times are carefully
calibrated to ensure that production-rate changes and mix changes can
be executed without shutting down the process. An alarm notifies the
operator when a mix change is complete and material can be directed to
another storage silo.

Ingredients other than asphalt binder, including other liquid additives
and bulk additives, are controlled in the same fashion as the liquid asphalt
binder. They all take their key from the aggregate belt scale signal. When
mixes containing RAP are being produced, the control system also reads
the belt scale on the RAP conveyor and adjusts the virgin-asphalt binder
to compensate for the asphalt that is included in the RAP.

The belt scale reading is very critical in producing quality HMA in the
drum-mixer process. Additional attention needs to be paid to the area
of the main belt where the belt scale is located. Before startup, the area
should be inspected and cleaned to make sure the framework is free of
dust and dirt buildup that may affect the readings. Wind screens mounted
around the belt scale will prevent wind loading that can artificially add or
subtract weight from the readings. A belt tensioner or deadman (weighted
idler roller) to take up slack as the belt stretches helps to keep constant
tension on the belt as it passes across the belt scale and helps improves
the accuracy of the readings.

7.4.5 Recycled Mix Production with a
Drum-Mixer

With a drum-mixer plant, Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) is intro-
duced directly into the drum-mixer dryer after the virgin aggregates are
introduced. To the casual observer, RAP is being introduced into the drum
similar to the virgin aggregates, and the process does not seem any different.
What is actually occurring inside the drum-mixer is that the virgin aggregate
materials are being super-heated, and these super-heated aggregates are

348 Manufacture of Hot Mix Asphalt

=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 383 ===
then being used to dry and heat the RAP to produce the new recycled hot
mix at the required final temperature. The burner control system is used to
regulate the temperature of the final combined product, just as it is used to
control the final temperature of mix produced from only new aggregates.
The steam released from the RAP is evacuated from the drum-mixer with
the steam from the virgin aggregates.

One or more cold-feed bins are dedicated for the RAP products, but a
separate belt scale is used to measure the quantity of RAP introduced into
the system. The control system uses this scale figure to control the ratio of
RAP and virgin-ageregate materials, along with the quantity of new binder
based on the quantity of usable binder in the RAP.

More information on producing recycled mixes in a drum-mixer plant is
available from the National Asphalt Pavement Association in Information
Series 123, “Processing and Handling Reclaimed Asphalt Pavements.”

Mix Deficiencies and
Problem Solving

Successful mix production requires a diligent approach to both the
calibration process and daily operational best practices. For successful
placement, a mix must have accurate and consistent aggregate gradation,
asphalt content, and temperature. The success of the in-place product
starts with the mix-production process.

The entire production, transport, and placement process should run
smoothly and consistently. Starts and stops increase the likelihood of mix
inconsistencies at the plant, whether a drum-mix or batch plant is used.
Starts and stops in placement also affect the quality of the placed product.
Every activity—from scheduling the production rate of the plant, to schedul-
ing the lay-down and compaction rate of the placement crew, to selecting
the number of trucks for the project, to the cycling of those trucks—should
be as organized as possible. Success will directly correlate to not only the
proficiency in mix production and the proficiency of the placement crew
but also to the smooth coordination of each stage of the process.

Even with the best intentions, things can turn out wrong. Sometimes
equipment fails. Often the failures are operational. When test results and
mix results are not as anticipated, it is useful to have a troubleshooting or
problem-solving guide to help find the cause of the problem so it can be
corrected.

The following two sections provide problem-solving guides, one for
drum-mix plant production and one for batch-plant production. They are
organized by the types of tests typically performed on the mix—gradation
analysis, asphalt content analysis, voids calculation, etc. The two sections
do not cover every possible cause of every possible mix deficiency, but
they do cover most common problems found in the field.

8.1. Pre-paving Conference

The key to a successful paving project is good communication between
the paving crew, plant personnel, and the agency or owner. This communi-
cation generally begins with the pre-paving conference. At this conference,
the contractor’s personnel should explain their project plans and establish
a meaningful schedule to meet the agency’s or owner’s expectations.

The details of the project should be covered so that all the affected par-
ties understand the “who, what, why, when, and how.” The pre-paving con-
ference can be as simple as the contractor’s representative meeting with
the owner on the project site to explain his plan to pave the project—or as
complicated as an organized meeting with all interested parties (including
the owner, the designer, the inspectors, personnel from the testing firms,
the contractor’s plant and paving personnel, utility representatives, etc.).
No matter what form this pre-paving meeting takes, it is very critical and
will save time, money, and confusion provided it is held well before HMA
placement begins.

The discussion of the project details allows everyone involved with the

6.2.1 Surface Preparation and Protection

A properly prepared foundation, which includes adequate surface and
subsurface drainage and proper compaction during construction, will help
to ensure a long life for a pavement structure. Proper drainage is extremely
important. The necessity for lowering the water table below the depth of
frost penetration or the bottom of the improved subgrade by lateral drain-
age or subsurface drainage is generally accepted by paving technologists
as an essential element of good construction.

Proper compaction of each layer of embankment, subgrade, improved
subgrade, and base course is equally important. Compaction greatly
increases the supporting power of the subgrade. When the subgrade is
not sufficiently compacted, additional consolidation may occur under traf-
fic, resulting in settlement and possible failure.

The following roadway surfaces are generally considered unpaved sur-
faces:

* Subgrade

° Improved subgrade

* Untreated base

° Nonsurfaced granular roadway

Subgrade
Compaction tests are made in the laboratory on materials to be used
in construction to determine the maximum practical density that can
be obtained. Laboratory densities are determined using AASHTO T 180.
Recommended subgrade compaction criterion are as follows:
° Cohesive soils—minimum 95 percent density in top 12 inches (300
millimeters) and minimum 90 percent for all fill areas below the top
12 inches (300 millimeters)

° Cohesionless soils—minimum 100 percent density in top 12 inches
(300 millimeters) and minimum 95 percent for all fill areas below top
12 inches (300 millimeters)

A specifying engineer should verify these requirements with the appro-
priate local, state, or in some cases, federal agency where the project is
located.

The selected moisture content for compacting cohesive soils should
provide the highest remolded strength consistent with expansion consid-
erations. Specifications usually require the moisture content to be within
+2 percent of the optimum moisture content. Generally, fine-graded
nonexpansive soils should be compacted to 1 or 2 percent below the
optimum moisture content for best results. Fine-graded expansive soils

to reduce the potential for swelling. The compaction criteria should apply

adding moisture, replacement, and recompaction to produce uniformity in
the subgrade and to avoid differential settlement.

Although compaction increases the stability of most soils, some soils
decrease in stability when scarified, worked, and rolled. Some soils also
shrink excessively during dry periods and expand excessively when
allowed to absorb moisture. They may also lose strength when reworked—
for example, silts that can become “quick” when remolded. When these
conditions exist, special treatment is required under the supervision of a
qualified geotechnical engineer.

Surface irregularities in an HMA pavement structure can result from
traffic densification of the underlying courses, usually in the wheel paths.
Such irregularities indicate that one or more courses were not adequately
compacted during construction or that soft, unstable areas exist in the
structure. To detect these deficiencies, proof rolling of the subgrade is
sometimes performed during construction. Proof rolling is the application
of heavy, pneumatic-tire rollers or large loaded dump trucks over the sub-
grade, usually following initial compaction by conventional means. Proof
rolling accomplishes two purposes: it locates unstable areas and achieves
additional compaction.

Prepared Subgrade

A prepared subgrade is one that has been worked and compacted. This
may be the foundation soil or a layer of stabilized soil, select soil, or oth-
erwise improved subgrade material. The riding quality of the pavement
surface depends largely on proper construction and preparation of the
foundation material. The roadway should be shaped and rolled so that the
paving equipment has no difficulty in placing material at a uniform thick-
ness and smooth grade. Weather conditions should be suitable, and the
roadway surface should be firm, dust-free, dry, or just slightly damp when
paving operations are started.

Untreated Base

Untreated granular bases should be properly shaped and compacted
immediately before paving. This procedure will restore any proposed line or
grade that may have been damaged during construction. When asphalt pave-
ment courses are to be placed on an untreated aggregate base, loose granular
particles and dust should be lightly swept from the surface. Care should be
taken, however, not to dislodge or otherwise disturb the bond of the aggre-
gate in the surface of the base. When the loose particles have been properly
removed, only the tops of the aggregate in the surface will be exposed. After
the surface has been cleaned, it is ready to be primed with asphalt. For a
discussion about prime coats, see Section 8.2.4, “Prime Coats.”

Nonsurfaced Granular Roadway

If an HMA pavement is placed on an existing granular roadway, the sur-
face should be shaped similar to untreated granular bases. The granular
roadway differs primarily in that it has been used as a roadway by traf-
fic and has typically been in use for a considerable time. It is advisable,
when the granular roadway is rough and uneven, to bring the surface to
the proper line and grade by scarifying the top few inches, blending in
more aggregate material as necessary, compacting, and then priming with
asphalt. If the granular roadway has had a previous asphalt treatment
and is rough and uneven, but structurally sound, it is advisable to place a
leveling course of HMA ahead of the first layer of the surface course. Oth-
erwise, the surface may have to be recycled by scarifying the surface and
mixing it with a portion of the existing base. The engineer should inspect
the surface well in advance of placing the surface courses and determine
what grade corrections need to be made. As with untreated granular bases,
the surface should be cleaned and swept before initiating paving opera-
tions, including any leveling courses that may be needed.

8.2.2 Preparation of Existing Pavement
for Overlay

The amount of preparation needed for an existing HMA pavement
before overlay will depend on the condition and the type of distress in the
existing pavement. Portions of the pavement that have “failed” (fatigue/
alligator cracking) should be removed and replaced with full-depth HMA.
Potholes need to be properly repaired, cracks need to be sealed or filled,
and rutting areas must be removed by cold milling. If these distresses
cover more than 40 percent of the pavement area, the use of cold in situ
recycling or full-depth reclamation should be considered. The procedures
for proper pre-overlay repairs are described in Chapter 12, “Maintenance
of Asphalt Pavements,” and the procedures for cold milling, recycling, and
full-depth reclamation are described in Chapter 13, “Rehabilitation Meth-
ods for Asphalt Pavements.” The repair procedures for Portland cement
concrete (PCC) pavements are described in Chapter 14, “Rehabilitation of
Concrete Pavements with HMA.”

8.2.3 Horizontal and Vertical Grade Control

Construction of a high quality smooth HMA pavement requires that the
subgrade and base course be built to the correct horizontal and vertical
grades. Before placement of the first lift of the HMA pavement on the base
course, elevations should be checked to ensure that the correct grades have
been achieved. Grades for aggregate bases should generally be within 0.05-
0.10 foot (15-30 millimeters) of the plan elevations. Additionally, the surface

8.2.4 Prime Coats and Tack Coats

Prime Coats

A prime coat is an application of a penetrating liquid asphalt (cutback,
specially designed emulsion, or other suitable material) applied with a dis-
tributor (see Figure 8.1) to a compacted aggregate base course. The func-
tion of a prime coat is to provide a protective layer to prevent the surface
of the aggregate base course from raveling under construction traffic. It
also provides a waterproof layer, preventing it from absorbing or losing
excess moisture before paving. A prime coat helps bond the top layer of
the granular base to the first layer of HMA pavement. Although this bond-
ing helps prevent slippage between the two layers, it is very weak and
should not be considered when overall strength of the pavement is ana-
lyzed. The prime coat is applied to an absorbent surface, such as a granular
base or prepared subgrade, with an asphalt distributor truck. A cutback
asphalt (such as MC-30, MC-70, or a specially designed emulsified asphalt)
is typically applied at a rate of 0.20-0.50 gallons per square yard (0.9-2.3
liter per square meter) on the prepared surface. See Chapter 12, “Asphalt

penetrates the surface and should be entirely absorbed by the base layer. If
the asphalt is not absorbed within 24 hours after application, a light coat-

In many areas, the use of cutback asphalt is not permitted because of
the release of volatiles into the atmosphere. Local air quality requirements
should be checked to determine if cutbacks can be used. As a substitute,
asphalt suppliers have developed special penetrating emulsion materials
for use as prime coats, which are spray applied in the same manner as
cutback asphalts.

Tack Coats

Tack coats are a very light application of liquid asphalt applied with a
distributor to an existing nonporous surface. The purpose of a tack coat
is to promote bonding between the underlying surface and the new HMA
layer. Normally, an asphalt emulsion or hot asphalt cement is used for a
tack coat, but some agencies allow the use of other asphalt products. The
tack coat should be placed on a clean, dry pavement, free of any materials
that might prevent bonding. The application rate will vary depending on
the type and condition of the surface being overlaid. The goal is to place a
thin, uniform coating of asphalt covering 90-100 percent of the pavement
surface. The application rate for a tack coat is normally 0.05-0.15 gallons
per square yard (0.25-0.70 liter per square meter) of diluted slow-setting
cationic or anionic emulsion (SS-1, SS-1h, CSS-1, or CSS-ih). A diluted
emulsion is an emulsion that has been diluted using one part water to one
part asphalt emulsion. If a given emulsion contains 65 percent residual
asphalt in its original state, then after a 50-50 dilution, the product applied
to the roadway would contain a 32.5 percent residual asphalt. Spread rate
and uniformity are hard to control when using an asphalt distributor set
at a very low application rate. By diluting the tack coat, the emulsion can
be applied at a much higher spread rate while providing a uniformly thin
coating of residual asphalt. A low application rate is usually used between
new or un-trafficked pavement layers. An intermediate rate is required
when resurfacing an existing, relatively smooth, asphalt pavement. The
highest rate is used on old oxidized, cracked, pocked, or milled HMA or
PCC pavement surfaces.

Distributor operations should be monitored very closely to prevent the
accidental application of too much asphalt, which commonly occurs in
those areas where the distributor starts and stops. If this happens, the
distributor should be prevented from applying tack until the problem with

The asphalt emulsion should break and cure before placement of asphalt
concrete. Shortly after placement of the tack coat, the emulsion will break.
The color will change from brown to black, and the water will begin to
evaporate. Once the water has evaporated, the mechanical properties of
the residual asphalt will return and result in a strong adhesive bond. The
rate of this breaking-and-setting process will depend on the environmen-
tal conditions. Generally, the emulsions used for tack coats will break and
be fully cured shortly after placement. During this process, equipment
(rollers, delivery trucks, etc.) should be kept off the surface. Tack coats
leaving surplus asphalt on the surface can form a slip plane. Excessive
asphalt may also work its way up into the overlying course, which may
eventually flush asphalt to the surface. A light tack coat does no harm and
it bonds the surfaces, a necessity for good construction. Work should be
planned so that no more than the necessary tack coat for the paving shift
is placed on the surface. All traffic not essential to the work should be kept
off the tack coat.

Vertical faces of existing pavements such as curbs and gutters should also
be sprayed or painted with a uniform coating of approved tack material.
This work should be done such that exposed curb or gutter surfaces are
not stained. The use of low-penetration tack material such as an SS-1h or
CSS-1h will also reduce the amount of tracking. Tracking is the unwanted
coating or staining of adjacent pavement surfaces, both on and off the
project, by trucks or equipment tires.

A continuous flow of HMA material from the plant to the paver ensures
a consistently high-quality HMA pavement. The tonnage to be produced,
the size and types of trucks, the time to make one trip from plant to
paver, how much HMA to be placed and compacted—all are details that
must be considered. These parameters dictate the necessary number of
trucks. The time between each delivery must be maintained at a consis-
tent level, and the trucks must not be allowed to bunch up at the paver
or at the plant. Trucks may be delayed in traffic (construction traffic con-
trol can be a major cause of backups that delay the material delivery
trucks). If a break in the delivery of material occurs, the paver should be
stopped using appropriate techniques such as the rapid stop/start method
(the paver is stopped and started without an extended acceleration/
de-acceleration period). Paving should not resume until a sufficient num-
ber of trucks are on the job site to ensure nearly continuous paving. Start-
ing and stopping the paver for each truck as it arrives on the project will
reduce the smoothness and quality of the finished pavement.

8.3.1 Types of Haul Trucks

Three types of haul vehicles are used to transport HMA from the plant
to the paver: end-dump, bottom- or belly-dump, and live-bottom. Proper
trucking procedures are necessary for maintaining a continuous flow of
material to the paver hopper.

End-Dump Trucks

The end-dump truck (see Figure 8.2) delivers mix directly to the paver
hopper and may have three to six axles. It can have a capacity of 12 to
20 tons (11 to 18 tonnes). More axles mean more capacity. The advan-
tage of the end-dump truck is its short wheelbase, which makes it easy
to maneuver. The end-dump truck can also be a semitrailer truck (see
Figure 8.3), which is essentially a large dump truck, with a capacity of 20
to 25 tons (18 to 23 tonnes). The disadvantage with this type of truck is
that improved operator skill is needed and its large dump bed can be the
source of problems with overhead obstructions (wires, bridges, and trees).
Because of this truck’s size, special care must be exercised when loading
the truck because of the increased potential for aggregate segregation.

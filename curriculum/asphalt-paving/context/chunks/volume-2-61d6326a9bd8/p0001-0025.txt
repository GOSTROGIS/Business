Study chapter · Outline B2



Working safely around haul trucks
A haul truck brings material into a place where people and machines already have work to do. The transfer
becomes a coordinated operation: truck, paver, ground worker and traffic control each need a shared
understanding. Blind area means a space the operator cannot see from the operating position. Pinch point
means a place where a person can be caught between moving or closing surfaces. Neither disappears
because a worker is wearing high-visibility clothing.

At Cedar Learning Access, a delivery may arrive while someone needs the community entrance. The crew
must protect both the delivery path and the public route. The task is to keep people out of the interaction
between truck and paver while the approved delivery arrangement is followed.


  Consequences of lapses in communication

  Equipment specific hazards


LEARNING STEP 1




Consequences of lapses in communication
Communication fails when a message is unheard, misunderstood or directed at the wrong person. Agree
the signalling arrangement before the approach: the designated person, the signals or radio channel, the
acknowledged stop instruction and the response to lost contact. Use the established site procedure,
precise messages and confirmation. A driver looking toward a worker is not proof that the worker’s
intention was understood. Eye contact supports communication, but it does not replace a confirmed
message or a safe position.

The ground worker identifies themselves and remains in the planned visible position with an escape route,
away from the truck’s swept path and reversing space. If visual or radio contact is lost, the movement must
stop under the agreed procedure; the worker does not approach a moving vehicle to restore contact.
Restart only after the people involved have re-established the arrangement. Record a recurring
communication failure so the system can be improved.

LEARNING STEP 2




Equipment specific hazards
Study the whole movement envelope, not only the tyre tracks. A turning truck has a swept path; the body,
rear overhang and attachments can move through different areas. A reversing truck creates a changing
space between itself and the paver. That space stays clear of people and loose equipment. A raised body
changes height and stability; overhead utilities, structures and ground condition must be addressed before
the delivery operation.

Clean mirrors, windows, cameras and lights help the operator see, but visibility aids have limits and require
inspection. Spilled material, a dirty access step or a contaminated walking surface adds slip and
housekeeping risks. A person should never infer the safe location from an illustration’s scale. The actual
truck configuration, manufacturer instructions, assessment and traffic arrangement determine the
controlled area. A pre-delivery check connects these physical conditions to the agreed communication.

Study chapter · Outline B3



Safety working around and operating power
brooms
A power broom prepares a surface by moving unwanted loose material. Its brush also moves air, dust and
debris. A clean-looking surface may still have an uncontrolled airborne exposure, and a clear brush path
may cross a pedestrian route. Read the machine as a combination of travel, brush movement and dust
control.

The Rosco RB50 example in this chapter is the manufacturer’s Operations, Service and Parts Manual
1017258-03, Rev. B, for serial 255027 and above. Its component and warning pages are a real model
study. Match a different training machine to its own manual before practical instruction.


  Effects of noise and dust

  Dangers associated with vehicle movement

  Dangers associated with moving parts


LEARNING STEP 1




Effects of noise and dust
Noise can damage hearing and mask a spoken warning. Dust can irritate or contain hazardous constituents,
including respirable silica where the material produces it. Identify the material and exposure conditions;
visible dust is not a measurement of the respirable fraction. Controls may include the machine’s
appropriate dust-suppression system, a suitable method and access separation, with exposure assessment
and required respiratory/hearing programs.

PPE is chosen after the hazard assessment. High visibility addresses conspicuity; it does not control
inhalation. Respiratory protection must match the contaminant, exposure and required fit/program
conditions. Inspect the actual water system or other fitted dust-control equipment under the manual, and
respond if its performance changes. Do not assume every RB50 has every optional system shown in a
product picture.

LEARNING STEP 2




Dangers associated with vehicle movement
Travel and steering can create a collision even when the brush is disengaged. Establish the working route,
speed/movement controls, pedestrian separation, traffic coordination and operator visibility before
sweeping. Broom swing and attachments can extend beyond the tyre path. A person at the edge of the
travel lane may still be within the moving assembly’s envelope.

The operator checks access and the surroundings from the intended operating position and follows the
machine’s travel and sweeping instructions. The ground crew remains in the planned safe area. A new
parked vehicle, visitor or traffic queue changes the route; the operator stops and the arrangement is
reassessed. A warning horn tells people something is happening but does not give the machine a clear
route.

LEARNING STEP 3




Dangers associated with moving parts
The rotating brush, drive mechanism, hydraulic connections and moving linkages can entangle, strike or
pinch. Guarding and exclusion protect people during use; inspection, cleaning and adjustment require the
applicable shutdown and hazardous-energy control. A stopped brush may still be supported by hydraulics

Study chapter · Outline B4



Safety working around and operating air
hammers
An air hammer converts compressed-air energy into repeated impacts at its working tool. Air hose,
coupling, retainer, handle, trigger/control and tool steel are parts to identify before learning the task. The
energy that makes breaking possible can also create noise, vibration, flying material, hose movement and
physical strain. Preparation therefore includes the air supply and connections as well as the hammer itself.


  Noise and vibration hazards

  Hazards of flying debris

  Ergonomics and safe equipment handling


LEARNING STEP 1




Noise and vibration hazards
Repeated impact produces substantial noise and hand-arm vibration. Exposure depends on the actual tool,
maintenance, material, use time and working conditions. A rating in a brochure is not an individual’s
measured daily exposure. Use the assessed controls: appropriate equipment, maintenance, exposure
organization and correctly selected hearing/PPE arrangements. Report unusual vibration, looseness,
numbness or other concerns through the workplace process.

The CP1210/1230/1240 family manual provides actual pneumatic-tool safety and component information;
the applicable model and configuration still matter. A lower-vibration model may improve control but does
not eliminate the need to assess the job. For a training record, distinguish trigger time from total time spent
in the work area.

LEARNING STEP 2




Hazards of flying debris
Impact breaks material into fragments that can strike the operator or others. Assess the material, access
and nearby work; establish the required controlled area and suitable protection. Eye/face protection must
match the hazard, while dust controls address the airborne material. Positioning a bystander just outside a
sketch’s boundary is insufficient without the actual assessment.

Inspect the tool retention and hose/coupling arrangement under the manual. A loose connection can
become an uncontrolled hose, and an unsecured tool can be ejected. Before disconnecting or changing a
tool, isolate the supply and release stored pressure using the specified procedure. Never test for a leak with
a hand or direct compressed air at a person.

LEARNING STEP 3




Ergonomics and safe equipment handling
A heavy tool can strain the body while carrying, starting, working or stowing. Plan access and the working
position before lifting it into place. Choose the appropriate tool/support and approved handling method;
avoid twisting with the load or using the body to force an unsuitable tool. Hoses should be routed to reduce
tripping, dragging and damage while allowing the intended controlled movement.

Study chapter · Outline B5



Safety working around and operating
propane tanks
Propane equipment is an assembly: cylinder, valve, connector, regulator where required, hose and
appliance must be compatible. A familiar-looking fitting is not enough to establish compatibility. Identify
each part and the pressure/function it serves before preparing the task. The acquired Fairview Tiger Torch
instructions and the actual appliance markings are useful examples; they do not authorize mixing arbitrary
components.


  Potential fire hazards while working around propane tanks

  Proper storage and handling

  Proper connection procedures and check for leaks


LEARNING STEP 1




Potential fire hazards while working around propane tanks
Propane is stored under pressure and can form a flammable mixture with air when released. Escaping
liquid can cause cold injury; a leak can also create an oxygen-displacement concern in an unsuitable space.
Ignition sources include flames, hot surfaces, sparks and some equipment operations. Adequate
ventilation, separation and the correct assembly therefore belong in the task plan.

A cylinder must be suitable, identified and within the applicable inspection/requalification requirements.
Inspect its condition and the connected equipment through the prescribed pre-use checks. Odour may
indicate a release, but smell is not a complete leak or concentration test. An unexpected leak, damaged
connection or uncertain assembly is a reason to stop and use the emergency/isolation procedure, not to
experiment with ignition.

LEARNING STEP 2




Proper storage and handling
Storage and transport are different contexts. Secure the cylinder against unintended movement in the
required orientation, protect the valve and keep it in the permitted ventilated location away from prohibited
heat/ignition exposures. Follow the supplier, workplace and transport requirements for the actual quantity
and vehicle. The stowed torch and hose should be protected from damage; closing a valve alone does not
make loose equipment adequately secured.

Identify whether the task involves use, temporary placement, storage or transport. Each can have different
requirements. Keep the product SDS and relevant instruction accessible, and know the site’s
spill/fire/emergency arrangements. Do not place a cylinder in a vehicle space merely because it fits there.

LEARNING STEP 3




Proper connection procedures and check for leaks
Connection is a supervised procedural skill. Confirm the cylinder/appliance identity and the specified
connector type, inspect sealing surfaces and the complete hose/regulator assembly, and use the
manufacturer’s connection method. Some fittings have different thread directions or sealing arrangements;

Use the manufacturer-approved leak-test solution and test procedure; a flame is never a leak detector.
Observe the specified connection locations and response to bubbles or another leak indication. If the
assembly leaks, take it out of operation and follow the prescribed isolation/repair route. Rechecking after a
repair is part of the applicable procedure. An instructor assesses the actual connection and leak check with
approved equipment, not a learner’s confidence alone.

Worked example · The assembly register
  Fictional teaching sample: the cylinder and torch are identified, but the hose rating is unreadable and
  the regulator model cannot be matched to the appliance. A learner proposes connecting them
  because the threads fit.

  Think it through.

  The assembly has two unresolved compatibility/condition questions. Matching threads does not
  establish pressure suitability or the correct sealing design. Keep the assembly out of operation;
  obtain the appropriate component identification and manufacturer instructions, then have the
  supervised setup and leak test completed. Record the unknown components rather than replacing
  unknown values with guesses.

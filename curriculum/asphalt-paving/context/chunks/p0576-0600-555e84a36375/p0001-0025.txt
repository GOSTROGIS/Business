Typical Causes of Deterioration

Some of the primary causes of HMA pavement deterioration are listed here:

* Traffic loading

° Environment or climate

° Drainage deficiencies

° Material problems

° Construction deficiencies

¢ External causes (such as utility cuts)

As pavements age, one or more of these distress mechanisms begins to take
its toll. As cracking and other forms of disintegration begin to appear as the
primary cause of deterioration, secondary factors often contribute additional
amounts of deterioration. For instance, once cracking begins to appear in the
pavement surface, moisture can enter the pavement structure and accelerate
the deterioration caused by the initial distress mechanism. The timely appli-
cation of pavement maintenance techniques serves to help prevent or slow
down the effects of both primary and secondary distress mechanisms.

Cracking

A number of different types of cracking can occur in an HMA pavement
and the appropriate type of repair depends on the type of cracking present.
Some cracking, such as alligator cracking, indicates load-related deterioration
that requires a different maintenance strategy than block cracking, which is
typically caused by climatic forces. This section provides information on some
of the most common types of cracks included in a pavement evaluation.

Alligator (Fatigue) Cracking

Alligator, or fatigue, cracking appears on a pavement surface as a series
of interconnected cracks that resemble the skin of an alligator. Alliga-
tor cracking indicates load-related deterioration because it is caused by
fatigue failure of the HMA surface under repeated traffic loadings and is
often related to weakening of the base course or subgrade, insufficient
pavement thickness, excessive loading, or some combination of these fac-
tors. An example of alligator cracking is shown in Figure 11.9.

Traditionally, alligator cracking starts at the bottom of the asphalt layer,
where the tensile stress and strain under a wheel load is highest. The cracks
propagate up to the pavement surface where they first appear as parallel
longitudinal cracks in the wheelpaths. Under repeated traffic loadings,
further deterioration takes place and the cracks begin to interconnect,
forming many angular pieces whose appearance is similar to that of an
alligator’s skin or chicken wire.

In recent years, it has begun to be recognized that top-down fatigue cracking
may occur in HMA pavement. These commonly occur in thicker pavements
that are subjected to large horizontal tensile stresses developing in the surface

Block Cracking

Block cracks are interconnected cracks that form a series of large, rectangular
pieces on the pavement surface that are typically greater than one foot
(0.3 meter) in each direction. Block cracking is usually found when the HMA
surface has hardened and the daily temperature cycles cause shrinkage in
the HMA layer; however, it may also be caused by volume changes in a
fine-aggregate HMA mix containing low-penetration asphalt and adsorptive
aggregates. Low traffic levels can aggravate this latter situation because the
restorative working properties of asphalt require frequent traffic applications.

Block cracking typically covers a large area of pavement, including areas
without traffic. The size of the blocks and the presence of block cracking
in nontrafficked areas help to differentiate block cracking from alligator
cracking in the field. An example is provided in Figure 11.10.

Edge Cracking

Longitudinal cracks within one to two feet of the outer edge of a
pavement are referred to as edge cracks. Over time, as vehicles pass over
the weakened area, further break-up of longitudinal cracks may take
place, resulting in crescent-shaped cracks that branch to the edge of the
pavement. Edge cracking is illustrated in Figure 11.11.

Edge cracks form along a weakened pavement edge that does not have

Slippage Cracking

Slippage cracks are easily identified by their distinctive crescent- or half-
moon-shape cracks, as illustrated in Figure 11.15. They are usually caused by
vehicular turning or stopping movements in pavements with a low-strength
surface mix or a poor bond between pavement layers. The cracks are caused
by the horizontal forces of stopping or turning vehicles, so the ends of the
curved crack point in the opposite direction of the vehicle thrust.

Distortion

Distortions in the pavement layer are a result of instability in the HMA
layer or weaknesses in the base or subgrade layers. The distortion may
appear in a number of different forms, including rutting, shoving, corruga-
tions, depressions, or upheavals. Cracking may or may not accompany the
distortion. Each of the typical types and causes of distortions are discussed
further in this section.

Corrugations and Shoving

Corrugations and shoving, also known as washboarding, result in
ripples across the asphalt pavement surface at fairly regular intervals of
less than ten feet (3 meters) and perpendicular to the traffic direction. Cor-
rugations and shoving typically occur at points of severe horizontal stress

Rutting

A rut is a surface depression in the wheelpaths that may also have trans-
verse displacement along the sides of the rut. Rutting is caused by consoli-
dation or lateral movement of any of the pavement layers or the subgrade
under traffic. It may be caused by insufficient design thickness, lack of
compaction, weaknesses in the pavement layers due to moisture infiltra-
tion, or weak asphalt mixtures. Rutting is illustrated in Figure 11.17.

Settlement or Grade Depressions

Depressions and settlements are localized pavement surface areas with
elevations lower than the surrounding pavement area. Minor depressions are
often not noticed until after a rain when water ponds in the depression, caus-
ing a “birdbath” that can be hazardous to motorists. In dry weather, depres-
sions can be observed where staining is present. Depressions may be caused
by settlement or failure in the lower pavement layers or by improper con-
struction techniques. An example of a depression is shown in Figure 11.18.

Upheaval or Swell
An upheaval or swell is a localized upward displacement in a pavement
due to swelling of the subgrade or some portion of the pavement structure.

Utility Cuts and/or Patch Failure

A patch is a portion of a pavement that has been removed and replaced
or where additional material has been added. Patches are a form of
pavement distress regardless of how well they are performing, but the
severity of the problem increases when the patch has deteriorated.
The rate at which a patch deteriorates may be influenced by poor
installation techniques, such as inadequate compaction, inferior materials,
or failure of the surrounding or underlying pavement. An example of a
utility patch is provided in Figure 11.20.

Disintegration

Disintegration includes the breakup of the pavement into smaller pieces
that may become dislodged over time. It is important to address pavement
disintegration early, before too much of the pavement surface has been lost.
Weathering, raveling and potholes are examples of pavement disintegration.

Potholes

A pothole is a bowl-shaped hole caused by localized disintegration of the
pavement surface, as shown in Figure 11.21. Potholes are typically caused
by the continued deterioration of another type of distress, such as alligator

Raveling and Weathering

As pavements ravel and weather, the pavement surface wears away
because of a separation of the aggregate particles from the asphalt binder.
The aggregate typically separates first, leaving pockmarks in the surface.
As more particles dislodge, the pavement becomes rougher and further
deterioration takes place until large pieces have been displaced. Raveling
is usually found in the wheelpath, while weathering more commonly
covers the entire pavement surface. A raveled surface is illustrated in
Figure 11.22.

The presence of this distress indicates that either the asphalt binder has
hardened appreciably or that a poor-quality mixture is present. Raveling
usually requires the presence of both traffic and water to occur.

Skid Hazards

An important component of providing a safe pavement surface is keeping
the surface free of properties that might increase the likelihood of vehicles
skidding on the surface, such as surface water, polished aggregates, or excess oil
on the pavement surface. To address these issues, it is important to restore the
pavement surface in a way that allows water to flow off the pavement surface
and maintains sufficient contact between the vehicle tires and aggregate.

Bleeding or Flushing

Bleeding, or flushing, leaves a thin film of asphalt on the pavement surface
resulting from excess bitumen. Bleeding is easy to identify in the field by its
slick, glossy appearance and the tendency of the asphalt to often be sticky to
the touch (especially in hot weather), as illustrated in Figure 11.23. Bleeding
can be caused by an improperly constructed seal coat, too much asphalt in
a mix, too heavy a prime or tack coat, or excessive sealant in the cracks or
joints under an overlay. Traffic can also cause bleeding if the asphalt layers
are overcompacted and the binder is forced to the surface.

Polished Aggregate

Aggregates that have been worn to a smooth finish are called polished
aggregates. Some aggregates, such as limestone, may polish quickly under
traffic. Once the aggregates polish, they are a potential skid hazard, especially
when wet. An example of a polished surface is provided in Figure 11.24.

such as loss of cover aggregate and streaking. Other distresses discussed
earlier in this chapter, such as depressions, raveling, corrugations, and
potholes occur in both hot-mix asphalt and bituminous surface treatments.

11.4 Pavement Preservation

Program

As discussed earlier in this chapter, a pavement preservation program
can be a cost-effective way to manage a pavement network when a blend
of preventive maintenance, rehabilitation, and reconstruction treatments is
used appropriately. This section of the chapter describes the components
of an effective pavement preservation program in more detail and the
manner in which a pavement preservation program is supported using a
pavement management system.

11.4.1 Establishing a Pavement
Preservation Program

The decision to establish a pavement preservation program can be difficult
for an agency because it often requires a change in the way an agency does
business. Traditionally, agencies respond to pressure from outside forces
to continually allocate funds to the roads in the worst condition. However,
when a strong pavement preservation program is in place, in addition to
funding roads in poor condition, the agency dedicates some of its funds to
functional improvements on roads that are not substantially deteriorated in
order to delay the need for more costly treatments. It requires support from
the top of the organization to commit to this type of program and to remain
committed in the face of outside pressures to do otherwise.

Use of Pavement Management to Support Pavement
Preservation

The use of pavement management systems to demonstrate the benefits
associated with a pavement preservation program was discussed earlier
in this chapter. To fully support a comprehensive pavement preservation
program, a pavement management system must include the following
considerations in its analysis:

° A method of assessing pavement conditions that includes the types of
deterioration that normally trigger the selection of preventive mainte-
nance and rehabilitation treatments

° Pavement performance models that can differentiate the performance
of a pavement section with and without different types of treatments

* Treatment rules that can be used to determine when different types of
treatments are considered feasible

* Models that quantify the effects on pavement condition after differ-
ent types of treatments have been applied. These models are used to
forecast future conditions under various scenarios.

Developing a Program

A pavement preservation program can be initiated with a decision to
allocate funds to preservation treatments at different levels depending
on the pavement conditions. The appropriate amount of funds allocated
to preventive maintenance, rehabilitation, and reconstruction activities
depends on the current and projected condition of the agency’s pavement
network. However, using the pavement management system, an agency
can evaluate different funding allocations to determine which strategy
comes closest to meeting the agency’s targeted conditions. Agencies that
have been most successful with their pavement preservation program have
initiated other activities to support the preservation program, including
training at all levels within the organization, the establishment of dedicated
funds for preventive maintenance treatments, and the establishment of
guidelines that help ensure that treatments are consistently used effectively
throughout the organization.

Estimating Needs

The pavement management system can be used to identify funding needs
based on current and projected pavement conditions. To estimate needs, an
agency must first establish either a minimum condition level (above which
the network should be maintained) or a targeted condition level for each
road classification (such as arterials, collectors, and local roads). Using
the pavement condition information in the pavement management system,
the number of miles of roads that do not meet these condition levels can
be estimated along with the cost of repair at these levels. Together, this
information is used to estimate the total pavement needs of the agency. By
tracking the change in pavement needs over time, an agency can quickly
determine if it is “catching up” or “falling behind.”

Setting Goals

Information from the pavement management system can also be used
to set performance targets for the pavement network. This is typically
an iterative process in which anticipated funding levels are input into
the pavement management system and expected condition levels at a
particular point in time are calculated. By varying the funding levels input
into the analysis, a realistic estimate of future conditions can be determined
and a targeted goal can be established. In most cases, different pavement
condition levels are established for different road classifications in a
typical network, reflecting the differences in traffic levels, traffic speeds,
and customer expectations. For example, a lower targeted condition might
be established for local streets than for arterial roads that serve a larger
number of vehicles traveling at higher traffic speeds.

The establishment of a goal is an important part of a pavement
preservation program because it helps the agency measure the program’s
effectiveness. The best goals are based on information from the pavement
management system, can easily be remembered, define the expected
conditions, and set the timeframe for achieving the targets.

11.4.2 Selecting the Appropriate
Treatment

One of the primary uses of a pavement management system is the
identification of appropriate maintenance and rehabilitation treatments to
address the types and the extent of pavement deterioration. A pavement
management system can recommend either the appropriate level of repair
or a treatment. Each approach is described in this section.

Determining the Appropriate Level of Repair

In some agencies the pavement management system is used to determine
the level of repair needed rather than the specific treatment that might be
recommended. For instance, a pavement section might be recommended
as a candidate for preventive maintenance, minor rehabilitation, major
rehabilitation, or reconstruction, depending on the results of the pavement
condition survey and the agency’s treatment policies. Average cost
information is associated with each level of repair so the estimated cost
of each project can be determined. Average rates of deterioration for each
treatment are used to predict future conditions. While this approach is
fairly easy to set up and use in the pavement management system, the
use of average cost and performance data may not provide the level of
accuracy desired by some agencies. Agencies that prefer to conduct more
detailed project-level pavement evaluations before selecting the appropriate
treatment might prefer to use this approach in their network-level pavement
management analysis.

Selecting Appropriate Treatments

An alternate approach is to define specific treatments in the pavement
management system so the analysis can determine the optimal treatment
and timing for each project. This approach requires more detail in setting up
the pavement management system because treatment rules, performance
models, and impact rules must be established for each potential treatment.
Treatment rules define the feasibility of a treatment, performance models
approximate the rate of deterioration after a treatment has been applied,
and impact rules define the changes that take place after a treatment has
been applied (for example, any change in surface type or a change in
pavement condition).

Two approaches used to select appropriate maintenance and
rehabilitation trees—decision trees and decision matrices—are discussed
here in more detail.

Benefits and Limitations of Tools

There are advantages and disadvantages to each approach used to
determine appropriate treatments in a pavement management system.
All approaches are similar in that they define the conditions that must exist
before a treatment is considered feasible in the analysis. Some agencies
prefer the decision-tree approach because it is generally easier to explain
than “if-then” statements. However, they can become quite detailed and
difficult to draw when many factors are taken into account.

There are several limitations to the use of these tools that should be
considered by any agency establishing a pavement management system.
First, in some cases, treatment rules are defined in a manner so that only
one given treatment is appropriate for a given set of conditions. While
this might sometimes be true, in many cases, multiple treatments may
in fact be appropriate, and the pavement management system should
determine the cost-effectiveness of each strategy. Therefore, the treat-
ment rules should be set up to allow multiple treatments to be selected
when appropriate.

Second, the agency has to be sure that no conditions are excluded in
the analysis. This is a common mistake made when defining treatment
rules because of gaps in the rules. For instance, one rule might define a
treatment for pavement conditions between 50 and 70 and another might
define treatments for pavement conditions between 75 and 90. A gap occurs
between the pavement conditions 70 to 75 where no treatment is defined.

Finally, because treatment practices can change within an agency,
it is important that the treatment rules and cost be reviewed at least
annually to ensure that they reflect current conditions. Otherwise, the
pavement management system could be making treatment recommen-
dations that do not reflect agency practices or estimating the cost of the
treatment erroneously.

Determining Treatment Effectiveness and Timing

The effectiveness of any maintenance or rehabilitation treatment is
dependent on its design, construction, and timing. This is especially true
with preventive maintenance treatments because there is a narrow window
during which they can be applied effectively. A pavement management
system can help an agency determine the most cost-effective time to place
various treatments using the benefit/cost ratio described earlier.

Developing Guidelines for Treatment Selection

To assist agencies in consistently using treatments effectively, a number
of agencies have developed guidelines for treatment selection that assist
field personnel in choosing projects and that guide construction inspection
procedures. These guidelines define the conditions that might exist for a
treatment to be considered a feasible alternative, including distress type,
severity, and extent. Photos of appropriate pavement surfaces for each
treatment might also be included in the guidelines to help field personnel
better understand when a pavement section is considered appropriate.

11.4.3 Types of Maintenance and
Rehabilitation Treatments

Three general treatment categories that are considered for a pavement
depending on the type and amount of deterioration present: preventive
maintenance, rehabilitation, and reconstruction. A general description of
each level of repair that might be considered is presented here. Further
information on pavement maintenance and rehabilitation treatments can
be found in Chapter 12, “Maintenance of Asphalt Pavements” and Chapter
13, “Rehabilitation of Asphalt Pavements” respectively.

Preventive Maintenance

Preventive maintenance treatments are generally applied to pavements
in good condition and without much structural deterioration. They include
a variety of low-cost treatments aimed at addressing specific types of
functional deterioration or slowing the rate of pavement deterioration.
Examples of preventive maintenance treatments include crack sealing,
surface seals, thin hot mix overlays, and microsurfacing.

Rehabilitation

As pavement deterioration progresses, more substantial treatments may
become necessary. Treatments included in this category extend the life of
existing pavement structures by either restoring existing structural capacity
or by increasing pavement thickness to accommodate existing or projected
traffic loading conditions. Pavement rehabilitation may be considered
minor rehabilitation or major rehabilitation. Minor rehabilitation treatments
include non-structural enhancements, such as thin functional overlays.
Major rehabilitation, on the other hand, adds structural enhancements to
a pavement section. An example of a major overlay treatment is a thick
overlay.

Reconstruction

Reconstruction is the replacement of the entire existing pavement
structure, typically including the removal and replacement of all pavement
layers including the base. New or recycled materials may be used in a
reconstructed pavement section. Reconstruction is the most substantial
level of repair considered in a pavement management system. It is
recommended on pavement sections that are considered to be in failed
condition.

11.4.4 Prioritizing Project Needs

Most agencies are faced with the challenge of having insufficient funds
to address all their pavement needs. Pavement management systems assist
agencies in this situation by determining the most cost-effective use of
available funds. A number of different techniques can be used to determine
the optimal use of funds, as discussed in the following sections.

Approaches Used

Three approaches are generally used to prioritize pavement needs: ranking,
benefit/cost analysis, and optimization. Each technique is discussed here in
more detail in increasing order of sophistication.

Ranking

The simplest method of prioritizing project needs is to rank them according
to agency-specified criteria. In most agencies that use a ranking technique,
pavement needs are ranked from those in worst condition to those in the best
condition. Along with the ranked listing are treatment costs so that projects can
be selected from the ranked list until funding is depleted. In some instances,
traffic volumes are used to supplement the ranked list so that higher volume
facilities are selected for the program before facilities with the same pavement
condition but with lower traffic volumes. Since most agencies using a ranking
technique are doing so to fix the worst roads first, this approach often results
in the least cost-effective strategy for managing a pavement network. This is
especially true because alternative strategies (such as preventive maintenance
strategies) are not typically incorporated into the ranked list.

Benefit/Cost Analysis

A slightly more sophisticated approach for identifying cost-effective
projects and treatments is a multi-year benefit/cost analysis. Under this
technique, all feasible treatments over the analysis period are considered in
terms of the increased benefit provided by the treatment and the associated
cost of the treatment. In most pavement management systems, benefit is
defined as the additional performance provided by a treatment, as shown
in Figure 11.28. Some agencies multiply the area under the performance
curve by a traffic factor so that roads with higher traffic volumes are given
more “benefit” than roads with the same pavement condition but with lower

Optimization

The most sophisticatedapproach for determining the optimal program uses
mathematical programming techniques as part of a two-step optimization
process. First, the objectives for the analysis are defined in mathematical
terms, such as maximizing network conditions. Any constraints to the
analysis are also defined, such as limits on funding available or minimum
allowable conditions. Using mathematical programming techniques such
as linear programming or dynamic programming, the optimal solution
for the problem set is determined. At this first stage, recommendations
are stated in terms of the number of miles to move from one condition
category to another.

The second stage of the analysis matches pavement sections to the
recommended strategy. For instance, if the optimal strategy indicates that
20 miles of roads in poor condition should be moved into good condition
(through a treatment such as reconstruction), then the agency must identify
the 20 miles of poor pavement that will be included in the program.

Because of its complexity, a true optimization analysis is used by only
a few large agencies.

11.4.5 Maintaining the Pavement
Preservation Program

To ensure the success of a pavement preservation program, the
preservation philosophy should be integrated into the agency’s decisions at
all levels. Several keys to the success of an ongoing pavement preservation
program are listed here.

Support of Upper Management

The support of upper management is critical to the success of a
pavement preservation program, especially in the provision of the funds
needed for pavement preservation activities. Upper management support
is also important when outside pressure for fixing the worst roads first is
applied. Using a pavement management system, upper management can
demonstrate the negative long-term impacts of projects initiated through
political or other outside pressure and often minimize the inclusion in the
program.

integrating the Philosophy into the Agency

A pavement preservation program often represents a change in the
way an agency does business. Rather than strictly focusing on roads in
poor condition, the agency adopts a more cost-effective strategy that is
sometimes hard for personnel to understand without training. One of the

most effective ways of integrating pavement preservation strategies into
decisions is to demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of the program and the
anticipated conditions that will result. Personnel at all levels of the agency
must understand and buy into the philosophy for it to be most effective.

Demonstrating Benefits

The benefits associated with a pavement preservation program can be
demonstrated in a number of ways. Most agencies focus on the expected
improvement in pavement conditions and safety for the traveling public.
However, it may be more effective to convey the cost-effectiveness of a
pavement preservation program to politicians and upper management
because they often have business backgrounds rather than engineering
backgrounds. For example, an agency may demonstrate the cost of
achieving a targeted condition level under both a worst-first strategy and
a pavement preservation strategy. In most instances, the cost of achieving
the same condition under a worst-first strategy is four to six times more
expensive than it would be under a pavement preservation strategy.

importance of Training

The importance of communicating the pavement preservation philosophy
cannot be overstated. To be fully integrated within the agency, it is
important that personnel understand the underlying philosophy and their
role in supporting the agency’s pavement preservation efforts. A number
of training programs on pavement preservation techniques are available
through the Asphalt Institute, the American Society of Civil Engineers, and
the FHWA’s National Highway Institute.

Study chapter · Outline D1



Environmentally friendly properties
An environmental claim is useful when it names the material, exposure conditions, service provided and
evidence. Asphalt can be recovered and used again, but 'recyclable' is not the same as 'recycled here', and
neither word proves the lowest overall impact. The learning goal is a defensible comparison that respects
the people, land and water affected by a project.


  Define Green properties of asphalt


LEARNING STEP 1




Define Green properties of asphalt
Reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) contains recovered aggregate and aged binder. Processing and an
approved mix or other permitted use can recover value from those materials. The receiving process must
account for variability, contamination, binder properties and the required final performance. A recycling
pathway has collection, processing, transport and production effects of its own.

Avoid blanket claims that all installed asphalt is 'non-toxic' or 'does not leach' under every condition.
Product composition, contamination, exposure, receiving environment and applicable criteria determine
what evidence is needed. An intact pavement, hot production process, milling dust and a stored pile are
different exposure situations. A claim about one does not automatically apply to the others.

A fair comparison defines the same required service, area/loading, service period and maintenance
assumptions. Include production, transport, construction, use/maintenance and end-of-life within the
stated boundary. A lower production impact per tonne can be outweighed by different quantities, travel or
service life. Environmental decisions can also involve water, noise, land, access and local priorities that a
single emissions number does not capture.

In B.C., meaningful local engagement identifies the appropriate people and decision processes; a class
cannot supply a Nation’s permission or knowledge. Use public/fictional evidence for the exercise and invite
contributions only by choice. A careful conclusion can acknowledge a documented reuse pathway while
asking for the data needed to compare actual options.

Study chapter · Outline D2



Use of storage areas
Storage keeps the right material identifiable and available while controlling release, incompatibility and
exposure. A storage plan includes the container/tank, surrounding area, access, inspection, emergency
response and destination for waste. A tidy-looking tank is not proof that its containment or product identity
is adequate.


  Type 1 storage

  Type 2 storage

  Use of recycled materials in hot mix asphalt

  Use of recycled materials in asphalt plant burners


LEARNING STEP 1




Type 1 storage
For this lesson, Type 1 describes a primary storage container with a separate spill-containment
arrangement, such as a tank within a designed containment area. It is a course grouping, not a universal
legal classification. Fuels, primers, emulsions such as an identified SS-1 product, asphalt binder and waste
streams require their actual SDS, compatibility, heating/handling and regulatory arrangements.

Identify the product and hazards before deciding which area is suitable. Natural gas service has its own
engineered supply and safety requirements; it is not handled as an ordinary open liquid tank. Containment
must remain effective, including when rainwater or other material occupies space. Drainage and disposal of
collected liquid follow the approved procedure; opening a drain to clear capacity can release
contamination.

LEARNING STEP 2




Type 2 storage
Type 2 here describes a self-contained storage arrangement, such as equipment with integral secondary
containment, where its design provides that function. The word self-contained does not mean leak-proof,
maintenance-free or automatically suitable for every fuel or liquid asphalt product. Verify the actual design,
product compatibility, capacity, inspection and permitted location.

A biodiesel blend can have different handling/storage considerations from another fuel. A heated binder
storage system introduces thermal and other hazards. Labels, isolation, overfill prevention, transfer
connections and emergency access must match the actual installation. Read the tank/assembly
instructions and product records together.

LEARNING STEP 3




Use of recycled materials in hot mix asphalt
Using RAP in hot mix can reduce demand for some new material and may offer cost benefits, but the
outcome depends on processing, transport, consistency, binder contribution and required performance.
Mobile recycling changes where processing occurs and may change traffic, emissions and material-control

arrangements. Identify what is being recycled and into which approved product; do not treat every
recovered pile as acceptable feed.

Keep recovered material segregated by the required source/type and protect it from unacceptable
contamination. The quality record follows it through processing and blending. A low purchase price alone is
not the cost of a compliant finished mixture.

LEARNING STEP 4




Use of recycled materials in asphalt plant burners
A plant burner must use only fuel permitted by its design and applicable environmental authorization.
Recovered fuel or other recycled material is not automatically acceptable because it burns. Composition,
contamination, handling, burner suitability, combustion control and emissions requirements matter.
Heating temperature is established by the approved process and equipment, not by a generic classroom
number.

Storage/transfer for a permitted recovered fuel needs the actual SDS and emergency arrangements. A
change in fuel can affect burner performance and emissions and must be controlled through the authorized
process. Do not conduct a classroom trial with an unidentified waste fuel.

Study chapter · Outline D3



Asphalt plant and placement emissions
Emissions are releases from a process into the environment; worker exposure is contact with a hazard at
the workplace. They are related but are not measured or regulated in exactly the same way. A stack result
does not establish a worker’s breathing-zone exposure, and a workplace mask does not establish
compliance with an environmental permit.


  Discuss role of Metro Vancouver (formerly GVRD) in enforcement and regulation of emissions

  Role of MOE in establishing regulations

  Use of Bag house

  Emission controls


LEARNING STEP 1




Discuss role of Metro Vancouver (formerly GVRD) in enforcement and
regulation of emissions
Metro Vancouver has a regional air-quality regulatory program. Determine whether the plant or activity is
within that jurisdiction and which permit, bylaw or authorization applies. The retained B.C. Asphalt Plant
Regulation explicitly states that it does not apply in the Metro Vancouver Regional District. Do not carry an
outside-region authorization into the region by assumption.

The practical record identifies the actual site, operator, authorization, operating conditions,
monitoring/reporting requirements and responsible contacts. A historical count of member communities is
not a compliance test. Consult the current regional program and actual permit when preparing the work.

LEARNING STEP 2




Role of MOE in establishing regulations
Outside Metro Vancouver, the B.C. Asphalt Plant Regulation establishes requirements for the covered hot-
mix and hot-in-place recycling plants, with applicable registration, emission, operating and reporting
provisions. Its text also addresses additional municipal/regional requirements. Identify the actual activity
and location before selecting the rule. Provincial ministry names and responsibilities can change; the legal
instrument and current authorized contact must be recorded.

An environmental authorization is different from the workplace exposure-control program. Both can be
relevant to the same plant. Keep monitoring data and reports associated with their method, location,
interval and required interpretation.

LEARNING STEP 3




Use of Bag house
A baghouse is a fabric-filter dust collection system. In the MS-4 concept, dust-laden gas passes through
the filtering arrangement, particles collect and cleaning releases collected material to a hopper. The gas
and collected-solids paths are different. Primary collection may remove larger particles before secondary
collection. The actual plant arrangement determines how fines are returned or removed.

Filter condition, seals, airflow and the designed cleaning/collection system affect performance. A visible
change, alarm or damaged component needs the operator’s prescribed response. Do not assume a
baghouse removes every gaseous pollutant: its principal dust-control function does not establish control of
all emissions.

LEARNING STEP 4




Emission controls
Burner operation, aggregate moisture, production rate, material temperature, handling and dust-control
performance can influence emissions. Heating asphalt excessively or using an unsuitable fuel can create
problems; actual limits and operating settings come from the approved plant process and authorization.
Placement work also needs exposure controls suited to the product and conditions.

Record observations without diagnosing the entire process from colour or odour alone. An unusual visible
plume can prompt the required response and investigation, but it is not a measured concentration. Keep
the operating interval and material/fuel identity so the responsible people can relate the observation to
production and monitoring records.

Study chapter · Outline E1



Determine asphalt mix design
A mix design establishes a suitable combination of aggregate, binder and other permitted ingredients for
the required pavement performance. The accepted job mix formula (JMF) then provides the production
target under the contract. It is more than a recipe by eye: the materials, proportions, test basis and
performance requirements belong together.


  Asphalt/aggregate composition

  Types of mix designs

  Mix temperature characteristics


LEARNING STEP 1




Asphalt/aggregate composition
The aggregate skeleton, binder and air voids form a mixture whose response depends on both materials
and compaction. Marshall design uses its specified specimen preparation and stability/flow and volumetric
evaluations. Superpave design uses its specified material selection, gyratory compaction and volumetric
framework, with additional tests where required. Their results and specimen methods are not
interchangeable labels.

A field crew does not redesign a mixture by adding binder when the surface looks dry. Document the
observation, identify the supplied JMF and refer the condition through quality control. Changes in
aggregate source, binder grade or recovered material can affect the design and may require the specified
approval process.

LEARNING STEP 2




Types of mix designs
Dense-graded, open-graded, gap-graded and other designed mixtures distribute aggregate sizes differently
and may serve different functions. A mixture chosen for drainage is not automatically suitable for every
structural or traffic demand. The specification identifies the required mixture type, nominal size, binder and
other design properties; the accepted formula identifies the actual approved combination.

Read the formula’s product/source identities, target proportions, binder grade/content, recovered material
where permitted, test basis and version. A production ticket with the wrong JMF identifier is a traceability
issue even if the mixture looks familiar. Keep design approval separate from daily production conformity.

LEARNING STEP 3




Mix temperature characteristics
Hot-mix asphalt is produced and placed under its required elevated-temperature process. Warm-mix
technologies permit specified production/placement approaches at reduced temperatures relative to the
corresponding conventional process. Cold-mix products use a different material/process arrangement and
are selected for the intended application. These categories do not supply one universal upper/lower
temperature.

Confirm that samples match mix design
A test result belongs to a sample, and a sample must represent an identified material and interval.
Sampling plan, increment, composite sample, sample reduction, container, chain of custody and test
method are connected terms. A precise laboratory number is not useful if the wrong material was sampled
or its identity was lost.

LEARNING STEP 1




Aggregate sampling
Aggregate sampling must follow the specified location, timing, number of increments and method. A
convenient scoop from the visible top of a pile may not represent the material being supplied. Weigh-bin or
other plant sampling requires the designed safe access and procedure; exposure to hot material, gravity
and connected equipment must be controlled.

Preserve product/source identity and the relevant production interval. Combine or reduce samples only as
the method permits. Record unusual conditions and contamination. A sample is not made representative
by increasing its label detail after an unsuitable collection.

LEARNING STEP 2




Belt sampling
Belt sampling can characterize a material stream when performed under the approved method and safe
arrangement. A worker must not reach across or into a live moving belt. The required isolation/access and
sampling device/procedure determine how the sample is obtained. Include the full required
stream/fraction rather than selecting the pieces that look most typical.

A drum-plant process record connects the sampled feed with its bin, proportioning and time. A stopped-
belt method and an approved automatic sampling device have different controls; neither is replaced by a
generic drawing. Keep the sample method and the safe-work method together.

LEARNING STEP 3




Lab tests
Laboratory tests can determine gradation, binder content and mixture volumetric or other specified
properties. The result must identify the method, units, sample and applicable comparison. Method
differences and corrections can affect interpretation. A result outside a limit prompts the defined quality
process; it does not justify editing the result or replacing it with a more convenient sample.

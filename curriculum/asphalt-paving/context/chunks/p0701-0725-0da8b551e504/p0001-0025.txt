13.7.2 Stabilization with Asphalt
Materials

Asphalt works best where minimal plastic clay fines are present. If sampling
and testing an existing pavement reveals that some plastic clay is present,
it is may be possible to treat the material with lime to reduce the plasticity
in preparation for asphalt stabilization. Stabilization with asphalt materials
should not be used when the PI of the soils being stabilized exceeds 10 or
the minus 200 (0.075 millimeter) material exceeds 25 percent.

Emulsified Asphalt

Various asphalt emulsion binders have been used for FDR. Mixing grade
asphalt emulsions (slow set cationic or anionic) are usually used, with
the specific type dependent on the characteristics of the materials on site.
To increase the initial strength and resistance to water damage, small
amounts (1-1.5 percent) of either lime or cement can be added to a mix
using an asphalt emulsion. They can be added as dry material or as slurry
(lime, cement, and water). The mix design procedures discussed for use
with cold-recycling should be followed when asphalt emulsions are used
for FDR.

Foamed Asphalt

Foamed asphalt, also used for FDR, is created when small amounts of
water and compressed air come into contact with a hot asphalt binder in
a specially designed foaming nozzle. As the water vaporizes, it expands
to create tiny bubbles within the asphalt binder, decreasing the asphalt
binder’s viscosity and providing an appearance similar to black shaving
cream. Only 1-3 percent water by weight of the asphalt is required. to
foam an unmodified PG asphalt binder. The tiny foam bubbles carry a thin
film of asphalt that interacts with fine aggregates to create a mortar that
coats large particles. The water flashes to steam immediately during the
reaction and is not part of the asphalt binder.

Foam-stabilized material can be placed, shaped, compacted, and opened
to traffic shortly after mixing. It remains workable for a period of time and
can be stockpiled and placed at a later date by including additional mois-
ture for compaction. Generally, the material being treated with foamed
asphalt should have between 5 and 20 percent minus 200 materials. Fines
that exist in most crushed-limestone base materials or that are created
in the reclaiming process from soil-cement base are ideal for use with
foamed asphalt.

Mix Design with Foamed Asphalt

Foamed asphalt is characterized by expansion ratio and half-life. Expan-
sion ratio is defined as the ratio between the maximum volume achieved
by the foamed asphalt binder and the volume of the asphalt binder after
the foam has completely subsided. Half-life is the time required, in seconds,

for the foam to settle to half the maximum volume attained. The foaming
characteristics of the binder are influenced by these factors:

e Temperature of the asphalt binder—typically about 320°F (160°C);

e Amount of water added to the asphalt binder—typically 2 percent
plus or minus 1 percent by weight of the asphalt binder,

° Pressure used to inject the asphalt binder into the expansion
chamber—should be about 45 psi (3 bars); and,

° Presence of antifoaming agents, such as silicone additives, in the
asphalt binder. These materials should not be added to an asphalt
binder that will be used as a foamed asphalt.

The selection of the asphalt binder for a project is based on the in-
service environmental characteristics of the pavement being built as
recommended in Chapter 2 “Petroleum Asphalt.”

The first step in the mix design process is to optimize the foaming
characteristics of the foamed asphalt. The Iowa DOT has developed an
excellent procedure for designing foamed asphalt mixes. It is described in
a research report, “Development of a Mix Design Process for Cold In-place
Rehabilitation using Foamed Asphalt.” They use a small laboratory asphalt-
foaming plant (see Figure 13.25) to produce the foamed asphalt. This
equipment simulates the foamed asphalt to be used during construction.
The expansion and half-life characteristics of the foamed asphalt binder
are determined for at least three asphalt binder temperatures and a range
of water contents of 1-5 percent by weight of asphalt binder. The com-
bination of the asphalt binder temperature and water content that maxi-
mizes the expansion ratio for the longest half-life is selected for use.

The optimum moisture content for the reclaimed asphalt pavement/base
material is determined using standard procedures (modified Proctor) for
determining the optimum density and moisture content for an unstabilized
material.

To determine the optimum asphalt-binder content, samples of the reclaimed
material are oven dried and cooled to room temperature and then rewetted
to 90 percent of the optimum moisture content. A range of foamed asphalt
contents is added to the reclaimed material, mixed thoroughly at room tem-
perature, and compacted using either 50 or 75 blows with a Marshall hammer.
After compaction, the samples are extruded from the molds. After extrusion,
they are further cured for 3 days in an oven at 100°F (40°C). The density of the
specimens is determined by measuring the bulk specific gravity. The samples
should be handled very carefully after compaction. Because of the fragility
of the samples, the bulk specific gravity should be determined using the
CoreLok procedure discussed in Chapter 4 “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.”
As described in Chapter 4, the samples are tested to determine the Marshall
stability and the indirect tensile strength of the samples.

Additional companion samples are manufactured at each of the asphalt
contents discussed above and soaked at 77°F (25°C) for 24 hours. They
are surface dried and tested for strength using the same test procedure
as that used on the dry samples. The water-soaked strength is compared

13.7.0 Construction of a FDR Pavement

Pulverization of the Existing Pavement

Before the introduction of modern reclaimers, the FDR of a pavement was
done by scarifying the existing pavement system with a motor grader and large
rippers attached to crawler tractors. This scarification was followed by the use
of rotary mixers that pulverized the ripped material. The introduction of cold-
milling machines led to the production of large, self-propelled high-powered
reclaimers such as the one shown in Figure 13.26. Most reclaimers have an

8 feet (2.4 meters) wide drum and can pulverize a maximum of 12 inches
(300 millimeter) deep. During the pulverization process, the cutting drum will
“up-cut,” or rotate opposite to the direction the reclaimer travels.

In addition to the FDR reclaimer, the FDR process requires the use of a
motor grader, to spread and fine-grade the reclaimed material, and com-

Multiple passes of the reclaimer over the pavement section are neces-
sary to ensure that the target depth is adequately pulverized and to pulver-
ize the entire roadway width. This results in a series of longitudinal joints
and some overlap between the passes. The minimum overlap width is
normally 4 inches (100 millimeter).

The gradation of the reclaimed material should be monitored to ensure
that the gradation being produced matches the mix design. The gradation
of the reclaimed material is influenced by these factors:

® Front- and rear-door opening of the pulverization/mixing chamber. The
more the door is closed, the longer the pulverized material is retained in
the pulverization/chamber, which allows the larger chunks to be broken
down.

° Position of the breaker bar setting in the pulverization chamber. The
closer the breaker bar is to the cutting drum, the finer the gradation
will be.

° The speed of the cutting drum. For thick lifts, a lower cutting drum speed
is used, and for thinner lifts, a faster-cutting drum speed is used.

° The forward speed of the reclaimer. The slower the forward speed, the
finer the gradation of the material will be.

¢ The condition of the existing pavement. When the existing pavement is

The physical properties of the asphalt pavement are affected by the
ambient temperature. If the pavement is cool, the asphalt layer is stiff and
brittle and can be pulverized into small pieces very easily (see Figure 13.27).
Increasing temperatures cause the pavement to become more plastic, and
slabbing (asphalt lifting or breaking off in large pieces) can occur. The most
efficient temperature range for sizing the reclaimed material is 50°-90°F
(10°-30°C).

If additional pulverization or remixing is required for uniformity, the
reclaimed material should be lightly compacted and reshaped. This
process accurately controls the mixing depth. The rear wheels of the
reclaimer compact or reduce the thickness of the fluffed-up material dur-
ing pulverization. With light compaction and reshaping, the reclaimed
material will have uniform thickness, which will allow better control of
the treatment depth.

When an asphalt emulsion is added to the reclaimed material, a nurse
truck is connected to the reclaimer (see Figure 13.28). The reclaimer is
equipped with a meter capable of controlling the rate of flow and the total
amount of asphalt emulsion being added to the reclaimed material. The
meter should be linked to the forward speed of the reclaimer so that the
amount of asphalt emulsion added will change according to the opera-
tional speed of the reclaimer. If asphalt emulsion is used, some agencies
add lime or cement to improve the early strength of the mix and to speed
up the curing process.

13.7.4 Shaping and Compaction
Procedures

A motor grader is used to move and place the reclaimed material to
the desired longitudinal grade and cross-slope (see Figure 13.29). The
amount of motor grader work required to place the reclaimed material
depends on the original shape of the roadway, the specified final shape of
the roadway, and the type of wearing surface that will be used. The final
grade should be similar to that required of an untreated base course. The
smoothness of the final HMA surface is directly related to the smoothness
of the pulverized and reclaimed layer. To ensure a smooth final surface,
tight blading or trimming the reclaimed surface may be necessary.

The reclaimed material is generally not at the optimum moisture content
for compaction. Either aeration will be required to dry the reclaimed mate-
rial, or moisture will have to be added to aid compaction. A water truck can
be used as a supply truck to the reclaimer’s onboard liquid additive, or it can
be used to add water to the reclaimed material through a spray bar.

The level of compaction achieved in the reclaimed material is directly
related to the performance of the final pavement. Figure 13.30 shows
a reclaimed surface before compaction. Compaction of reclaimed mixes
using an asphalt emulsion as a stabilizing agent must be accomplished
soon after the emulsion begins to break. This is when the emulsion begins
to change from brown to black, which also indicates that the moisture is
evaporating from the reclaimed material. The compaction process becomes
more difficult as the emulsion breaks.

Compaction of reclaimed mixes using foamed asphalt should occur
shortly after placement of the mix. The mixes will remain workable as
long as the moisture content stays at or above the moisture content at
the time of foamed-asphalt injection and mixing. The compaction process
should be completed before the foamed asphalt mix begins to dry out.

Compaction should be done with heavy pneumatic or steel-wheel
rollers. The depth of the reclaimed material that must be compacted
and the desired degree of compaction will influence the weight and the
amplitude/frequency of vibration for vibratory compactors and the static
weight and tire pressure of pneumatic rollers. The roller pattern should
be established to determine the best mixture of roller types and number
of passes.

As with CIPR mixes, FDR mixes using emulsions must be cured before a
wearing course can be placed. The time required for this process depends
on daytime and nighttime temperatures, the humidity levels and rainfall

13.7.5 Inspection

Prior to the start of any FDR project, the centerline of the existing road-
way should be referenced by offset markers along the edge of the roadway

outside the limits of construction. Such markers will facilitate the construc-

tion of the new pavement without the need for a survey layout crew.

During the construction process, the following should be checked:

° The treatment depth should be physically checked by removing the
reclaimed material on either side of the reclaimer.

¢ The gradation of the reclaimed material should be checked periodi-
cally. This generally consists of checking the maximum particle size of
the reclaimed layer. The sample should be taken from the full depth
to ensure that the reclaimer is treating the entire depth of the layer
being reclaimed.

° The yield rate of the emulsion or the foamed asphalt and additional
aggregate should be computed and compared with the mix design.

° The FDR mix should be checked to ensure that it is homogeneous,
with no segregation, and that the aggregate is coated with emulsion
or foamed asphalt throughout its depth. It should also be checked to

14.1 Introduction

Many miles of Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavement are
rehabilitated each year by placing an asphalt overlay on them. According
to the FHWA’s “Highway Statistics 2005,” there were more than 40,000
more miles of composite pavements than rigid (PCC surface) pavements
in the United States. Chapter 6, “Thickness Design” discusses the thickness
design procedures for those overlays. This chapter discusses techniques
used to either manage the reflection cracking in those overlays or elimi-
nate the occurrence of reflection cracking.

14.1.1 Reflection Cracking

Reflection cracking is the result of horizontal and vertical movements
at the joints and cracks in the underlying pavement, resulting in a pattern
of cracking in the surface that reflects the joints or cracks in the underly-
ing PCC pavement. Temperature changes in the pavement cause the slabs
to contract and expand. This movement is most pronounced at joints and
cracks, which open and close in response to the volume changes in the
PCC slabs. Opening of joints and cracks due to cooling causes tensile
stress directly over these locations in the HMA overlay (see Figure 14.1).
Reflection cracking may occur after an extreme cold temperature event or
after repeated opening and closing.

Traffic loading may produce differential vertical movement across the
joint or crack, particularly when load transfer is inefficient. This causes
shear and bending stress in the overlay. As the wheel (see Figure 14.2)
approaches, the shear stress will reach a maximum at point A, and as it
passes over the joint, it will reverse at point C. As the wheel load passes
over the joint at point B, the bending stress will reach a maximum.

The key to delaying or avoiding reflection cracking is to reduce or
eliminate the stresses and deformations produced in the overlay. Some
treatments include the placement of an interlayer to reduce or dampen
the movement at the joint or crack caused by thermal contraction of the
PCC. Some of the techniques are discussed in Chapter 13, “Pavement
Rehabilitation Methods for HMA Pavements,” including the use of geo-
textiles, asphalt-rubber “stress-absorbing membrane interlayers” (SAMIs),
etc. Single- and multiple-surface treatments have also been used as an
interlayer for rehabilitation of HMA- and PCC-surfaced pavements, as have
specialty asphalt paving mixtures that are extremely ductile and resistant
to cracking. Another widely used approach is to control reflection crack-
ing by sawing and sealing the overlay directly over the joints. This will be
discussed in more detail later in this chapter.

14.2 Saw-and-Seal

Saw-and-seal has been used for many years to slow the rate of deterioration
of reflection cracks in relatively thin overlays of jointed PCCP. The technique
consists of carefully referencing all the joints in the existing pavement before
overlay and then sawing and sealing contraction joints in the HMA overlay
directly over the underlying joints. When properly done, a weakened plane is
formed directly above the joint defining the ultimate cracking pattern in the
overlay. The saw-and-seal procedure will do the following:

° Provide maintainable joints

e Reduce or eliminate the spalling associated with reflection cracking

° Provide for good ride quality

° Provide a neat, well-maintained appearance

Saw-and-seal has most commonly been used on jointed-reinforced con-
crete (JRPC) pavements on highway and street projects in New England
and Louisiana, as well as on airfield pavements. JRPC pavements gener-
ally have longer joint spacing than jointed-plain concrete (JCPC) pave-
ments, resulting in longer slabs and thus greater opening and closing
movement at the joints. It should only be considered for jointed concrete
pavements that are in good structural condition, have good joint load
transfer, and have intact panels (no working cracks) and minimal spalling.
Thought should be given to how the joints can be practically referenced
before selecting this approach, because missing the joint by more than
one inch usually results in a jagged, irregular reflection crack alongside a
neat sawed and sealed crack.

This technique should not be considered for the following:

¢ Overlays on JCPC that have a high frequency of mid-panel cracking

(more than 20 percent) or badly deteriorated cracking. Panels with
working cracks should either be patched or replaced with a new con-
crete panel.

¢ Overlays on JCPC with badly deteriorated joints and/or significant

patching at or near the joints

* Overlays where there is insufficient load transfer across the joints as

evidenced by %-inch (0.0625 millimeters) faulting and/or pumping.
When tested using nondestructive testing equipment, joints should
have a minimum 60 percent load-transfer efficiency.

° Overlays of pavements with wide joints—greater than 34 inches

(19 millimeters)

14.2.1 Design Considerations

Minimum Thickness

Saw-and-seal is commonly considered when the overlay thickness that can
be placed is limited. Examples include urban streets with curb and gutter
sections, intersections and driveways, and highways with vertical clearance
restrictions. As previously suggested, an overlay is best applied in two lifts to
provide the greatest opportunity to improve ride quality and surface drain-
age, but there have been numerous examples where a single lift of HMA
has been placed, has been sawed and sealed, and has provided excellent
performance. The minimum recommended thickness for a single lift of
HMA is 2 inches (50 millimeters). For two lifts, the minimum thickness is
3% inches (90 millimeters).

Joint Configuration

Many state DOTs have specifications for this process. The engineer should
check with the local DOT to ensure that his or her specification follows their
requirements. The typical requirement for the joint is that the HMA layer be
cut to one-third to one-half of the thickness of the HMA layer. This can range
from % inch wide Gf a reservoir for the sealant is used) to % inch wide if
no reservoir is used. Figure 14.3 presents the procedure most DOTs utilize,
which is the % inch cut with a reservoir for the joint sealant material.

Urban and Other Areas

When using saw-and-seal in urban areas where curb and gutter sections
are present, the saw cuts should line up with the control joints in the gut-
ter sections, even if a slightly skewed saw cut results. The saw cuts should
not be spaced more than 40 feet (12 meters) apart, and for thin JPCP, it
may be beneficial to have the cuts spaced closer, such as 30 feet (9 meters)
apart. For parking lots, airports, and other large areas of pavement,
saw-and-seal has been shown to be effective when the saw cuts are made
so that the pavement is cut into 50-foot (15-meter) squares.

joints in the concrete pavement is critical. Fixed reference markers should
be used. Chalk or paint is used to mark the joint (see Figure 14.4) after the
HMA overlay is placed. A deviation of 1 inch (25 millimeters) can result

Timing

The saw-cutting and sealing should be done as soon as possible after

than 48 hours after the wearing course is placed. The saw-cutting might

joint spalling. The concern is that the joint will reflect through before the
saw cut is made. The saw-cutting can be done with either wet or dry cut-
ting. Sawing and sealing should not be done if the pavement is wet or if
the ambient temperature is less than 40°F (4°C).

Sealing of Saw-Cut
The sawed joint must be clean and dry when the backer rod and sealant
material are placed. The sealant material should meet the requirements of
ASTM D 6690. If dry sawing is used, the joint should be cleaned with com-
pressed air to remove the dirt, dust, and other deleterious material. If wet
sawing is used, the cut should be flushed with high-pressure water until it
runs clear. It then should be dried using compressed air.
The sealing of the saw-cut should follow these steps (see Figure 14.6):
¢ Install the backer rod at the specified depth. If the reservoir style is
used, a backer rod may not be needed, but steps should be taken to
ensure that the sealant does not enter the remainder of the joint.
° The hot sealant should be placed in the saw-cut according to the
manufacturer’s recommendations.
¢ The sealant material should fill the joint so that after cooling, the level

14.3 Fractured Slab Techniques

Two procedures have been developed to fracture PCC slabs, thereby
reducing the stresses and deformations that occur in an asphalt overlay—
crack/break-and-seat and rubblization. The crack/break-and-seat procedure
reduces the effective size of the PCC slabs and seats them firmly onto the
underlying layers with heavy rollers. The rubblization process eliminates
slab action by reducing the PCCP to aggregate-size pieces, resulting in a
layer that functions in a similar way to an aggregate base course.

14.3.1 Crack/Break and Seat

Crack/break and seat minimizes joint and crack movement by shortening
the effective slab length and firmly seating the broken PCC pieces onto the
underlying layer (see Figure 14.7). The PCC is broken using a drop hammer,
and then seated with a large, ballasted rubber-tire roller. The intent is to create
slabs that are small enough to reduce the movement at the cracks in the PCC
layer that were caused by low temperatures and daily temperature variation,
while still being large enough to develop flexure under loading.

Cracking and seating is used on JCPC pavement, while breaking and seat-
ing is used when the PCC pavement is JPCP. The broken slabs are seated
to re-establish firm support between the underlying layers and the slabs,
reducing the amount of differential vertical movement under traffic.

Project Selection and Overview

Cracking/breaking and seating has been shown to be an effective tech-
nique when the following distresses occur in the PCCP pavement:

° Seriously faulted transverse joints and cracks

° Rocking of slabs due to the presence of voids

¢ Presence of working transverse and longitudinal cracking

* Uneven slab settlement

* Corner breaks

* Blowups, shattered slabs

The quality of the underlying layers should be thoroughly investigated dur-
ing the design process. It has been determined that the performance of the
crack-and-seat process is not very good when the PCC was placed directly
on a fine-grain subgrade, particularly in wet conditions. It is important that a
thorough sampling and testing program be conducted during the design pro-
cess. The thickness of the concrete pavement must be determined by cores.
The properties of the subgrade should be determined by cutting test pits in
the existing PCC pavement to determine the type, thickness, and strength
of the base and subgrade materials. These underlying materials should be
evaluated in the laboratory to determine their strength, using R-Value, CBR, or
elastic modulus testing. The base materials should be evaluated to determine
their drainability if a subdrainage system is to be used. The subgrade materi-
als should also be evaluated for potential swelling problems.

Design Considerations

The overlay should be designed according to the procedures outlined in
Chapter 6. If the existing PCC pavement shows signs of pumping, installa-
tion of a subdrainage system to drain the water away from the pavement
structure should be seriously considered. The Asphalt Institute recommends
a minimum overlay thickness of 5 inches (125 millimeters) of HMA over
cracked or broken and seated PCC.

14.3.2 Construction

The objective of the crack-and-seat process is to break the pavement into slabs
ranging in size from 18 to 48 inches (0.5 to 1.5 meters). Breaking the pave-
ment into the smaller sizes has shown to provide better performance. The
PCC slab must be cracked full depth. A test strip should be established to
ensure that the equipment and processes used are sufficient to thoroughly
crack the PCC pavement. Cores should be drilled directly over cracks to verify
that the slab is being cracked throughout the entire depth of the layer.

Cracking and Seating JCPC Pavement

Various types of equipment have been used to crack JCPC, including pile
drivers, guillotine hammers, whip hammers, and impact hammers. Production
rates will vary with the equipment type and the thickness of the pavement.
The most common method used is the guillotine hammer (see Figure 14.8),
which has high productivity and covers the full width of a lane.

After the JCPC has been cracked, the pieces must be firmly seated onto
the underlying layer to prevent rocking under traffic. Seating is accom-
plished with a large pneumatic roller that forces the broken slabs into
full contact with the foundation. Two passes of a 50-ton (450 kilonewton)
roller are specified for the seating operation. Excessive rolling should be
avoided because it can result in a loss of interlock between the cracked
pieces or in shear failure of the existing subbase or subgrade.

Breaking and Seating JRCP Pavement

As previously defined, breaking and seating is conducted on existing JRCP
pavement, and the process must rupture the reinforcing steel to be effective.
This process requires that the slabs be broken into pieces smaller than those
required for crack and seating. For breaking and seating, it is recommended
that the slabs be broken into pieces measuring 12-18 inches (300-460
millimeters). The steel should be cut by transverse sawing of the pavement at
routine intervals to ensure debonding in the transverse direction. The seating
process is accomplished with a heavy pneumatic roller in a manner similar
to that used in the crack-and-seat process, except that additional passes are
needed to ensure rupturing of the steel. Five to seven passes of a 35-50 ton
(315-450 kilonewton) rubber tire roller are recommended. Any soft spots or
local failures identified in the seating process require careful removal and
replacement. It is important to address drainage or unsuitable foundation
conditions during this process. The broken PCC should be replaced with
a full-depth HMA patch. The areas to be replaced should be sized to accom-
modate the equipment necessary to properly place and compact the new
material.

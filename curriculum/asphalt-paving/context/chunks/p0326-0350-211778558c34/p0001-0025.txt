Air-carrier Airports

Air-carrier airport pavements accommodate aircraft of 60,000 pounds
(27,000 kilograms) gross weight or more (Figure 6.4). The comprehensive
design manual MS-11, details the air-carrier airport design method, and it
is incorporated into SW-1.

The air-carrier design method uses pavement design curves developed from
numerous runs of a computer-based M-E analytical model. To account for the
differences between aircraft gear and wheel geometry, design aircraft passes
are converted to strain repetitions of an equivalent standard aircraft.

The design curves allow variation in MAAT from 40°F (4.4°C) to 75°F (24°C).

The air-carrier design method considers only full-depth asphalt
pavements, and no consideration is given to pavement sections utilizing
granular base materials. This conforms to Federal Aviation Administration
(FAA) design requirements for stabilized base materials for design aircraft
over 100,000 pounds (45,000 kilograms) gross weight.

The basic steps of the air-carrier pavement design procedure are as
follows:

1. Climate—establishing the appropriate MAAT for the project

2. Traffic—identifying design aircraft and number of departures during
the design period

3. Subgrade—describing subgrade strength in terms of resilient
modulus

4. Solution—working through the curve (or software) to establish the
design thickness for a full-depth asphalt section.

6.1.5 Heavy Wheel Loads

Design of HMA pavements for port and industrial facilities with heavy
wheel loads is based on the methods detailed in Asphalt Institute Manual

Heavy wheel-load vehicles include log-hauling trucks, dump-body haulers,
forklift trucks, straddle carriers, rubber-tire hoists, and many special vehicles hav-
ing four or more tires (Figure 6.5). Heavy-ioad vehicles can reach gross vehicle
weights in excess of 400,000 pounds (180,000 kilograms). Unlike the standard-
ization found in the highway trucking industry, there is little standardization
among heavy-vehicle manufacturers for port and industrial applications. Con-
sequently, wheel geometry can vary greatly from one vehicle to another.

The heavy wheel-load design curves published in MS-23 were devel-
oped by presolving numerous problems using DAMA. Due to the expected
low number of design vehicle passes, HMA pavements in port and indus-
trial applications are not expected to fail from fatigue cracking. The design
curves are based only on subgrade rutting.

A further simplifying assumption is that only full-depth asphalt materials
are considered for heavy wheel applications.

Climate variability is greatly simplified for heavy wheel-load pavements
in that only two climate options are available:

° MAAT less than or equal to 55°F (13°C)

° MAAT greater than 55°F (13°C)

Essentially, the choice is between a cold climate, where the subgrade is
subjected to freeze hardening and thaw weakening, and a warm climate
with no freeze/thaw concerns.

An important aspect of understanding the design vehicle loads and
wheel geometry is to ascertain whether the problem can be considered a
single-wheel, dual-wheel, or multiple-wheel design problem. For single-
wheel loads, subgrade rutting is analyzed only at the point directly below
the wheel. On the other hand, dual- and multiple-wheel loads can super-
impose at depth, complicating the process of determining the location
and magnitude of the maximum subgrade compressive strain. If a distance
greater than eight times the radius of a loaded contact area separates the
two wheels, the problem may be considered a single-wheel problem.

Single-wheel load design problems are relatively straightforward,
requiring only the magnitude of the wheel load, the tire-ground contact
pressures, the contact area, and the number of load repetitions. Dual-
and multiple-wheel problems are more complicated, requiring additional

6.1.6 Planned Stage Construction

Planned stage construction is the construction of roads and streets by
applying successive layers of asphalt concrete according to design and a
predetermined time schedule. Two possible circumstances where stage
construction is advantageous are the following:

e When adequate funds are not immediately available for a full design,

but anticipated at some later date

° When long-term traffic projections (20-25 years) are not considered

reliable. In this situation, traffic estimates for the second-stage
construction can be made while the facility is in use after first-stage
construction.

The procedure is based on the assumption that the second stage will be
constructed before the first stage shows serious signs of distress. Gener-
ally, it is held that the first stage should be designed such that 60 percent
of the pavement’s remaining life is used during the first stage, leaving
40 percent to be used up during the second stage.

Planned stage construction should not be confused with major maintenance
or rehabilitation of existing pavements. Readers are referred to MS-1 for fur-
ther details about this method and example problems.

6.1.7 Overlay Design

Design of HMA overlays is based on either the effective thickness or the
deflection method, as detailed in Asphalt Institute Manual MS-17, Asphalt
Overlays for Highway and Street Pavements.

Effective Thickness Method

The effective thickness procedure for structural evaluation and overlay
design is based on the concept that pavements deteriorate over time as a
result of load repetitions imposed by traffic. Furthermore, as a pavement
uses part of its total life, it behaves as if it were an increasingly thinner
pavement. Consequently, the “effective” thickness of an aging pavement is
always less than its actual thickness.

The effective thickness of an existing pavement layer can be estimated
from its pavement condition and knowledge of its composition and actual
thickness. Conversion factors ranging from 0.0 to 1.0 are assigned to the layer
based on its composition and condition. The actual thickness of the pavement

Relationship to Other
Design Methods

The Asphalt Institute recognizes that other flexible-pavement design
methods are available to engineers. These other design procedures fall
into two basic categories: empirical and mechanistic-empirical. A separate
discussion on Perpetual Pavement design is included in this section.

6.2.1 Empirical Methods

Empirical methods are based entirely on the experience of evaluating
well-performing and poorly-performing pavements along with specifically
constructed test sites such as the AASHO test road. The commonly used
CBR method is an empirical method developed in the 1920s by members
of the California Department of Highways from experience with its pave-
ments. It was validated with field testing and improved for use on airfields
during World War I and is still in wide use today.

The 1993 AASHTO Guide is an empirical method derived from the
AASHO Road Test of the 1950s. While the method is widely used and
highly respected, the AASHO Road Test pavements were constructed of
relatively uniform mixes, built on a single subgrade soil, and exposed to a
single environment—Illinois. These and other facts anchor the validity of
the data to the specific mixes, soil types, and climatic conditions experi-
enced in Illinois in the 1950s and open the door to criticism of its validity
in other conditions.

Empirical methods are limited by the depth and breadth of the data
set. For example, it is well established that HMA stiffness varies with
pavement temperature, yet the CBR method makes no allowance for
HMA stiffness variation due to monthly or seasonal climate variation.
Likewise, the AASHTO method has similar difficulty in transferring the
experience of one climate to that of another. For these reasons, empirical
methods, while convenient, may not make the best predictions for pave-
ment thickness in other locations or for other conditions.

6.2.2 Mechanistic-Empirical Methods

The Asphalt Institute methods are based on mechanistic-empirical prin-
ciples and are derived from work done in the 1970s and 1980s by the
Asphalt Institute staff, member company engineers, and a team of consul-
tants consisting of Mr. Fred N. Finn, Dr. Carl L. Monismith, and Dr. Matthew
W. Witczak. These experts established fatigue and rutting criteria that are
still in use today in the AI methods and others. Dr. Witczak developed the

computer-based M-E analytical model, DAMA, which was used to create
the Asphalt Institute design curves published in MS-1 and other manuals.
These efforts established the Asphalt Institute as a pioneer in M-E pave-
ment design methods at a time when the concept was relatively new.

In the past few decades M-E design methods have become widely
accepted. It can be said that M-E-based flexible-pavement design is on the
path to becoming the standard rather than the exception. Several state high-
way departments have developed their own mechanistic-empirical design
systems including WINFLEX, MnROAD, and EverPAVE, the official design
methods of the Idaho, Minnesota, and Washington State Departments of
Transportation, respectively. Air-carrier airport-pavement designers are able
to tap the power of M-E methods in LEDFAA, a computer program devel-
oped by the FAA. PCASE is the M-E design method applicable to military
airfields operated and maintained by the U.S. Department of Defense.

At the time of this writing, a new national M-E Pavement Design Guide
(MEPDG) has been developed under sponsorship of the National Cooper-
ative Highway Research Program and is in the process of being introduced
to the state highway departments by the Federal Highway Administration.
It is widely expected that the MEPDG will be adopted by AASHTO as its
pavement design guide at some time in the future.

As discussed in the next section, the Perpetual Pavement design con-
cept, based entirely on M-E principles, is a pavement design strategy that
is gaining support across the country.

The Asphalt Institute M-E design method is now part of an increas-
ingly populated field of powerful pavement design tools available to civil
engineers worldwide. The reader is referred to Tables 6.1 and 6.2 for a
comparison of some of the features of a select group of M-E methods for
highway and airport applications.

6.2.3 The Perpetual Pavement Method

The basic concept of a Perpetual Pavement is that through careful mix
type and material selection together with high-quality construction, a
pavement structure can be built that is resistant to permanent deformation
and fatigue cracking. Surface-initiated distress, such as top-down cracking
and weathering/raveling, may still occur, but through a regular process
of milling and overlaying, the pavement structure never deteriorates. Like
the roof on the typical residential structure, the surface layer will need to
be replaced periodically, but the underlying pavement structure remains
solid and strong.

The Perpetual Pavement design concept consists of the following layers:

1. An unyielding subgrade forms the pavement foundation.

2. The base layer consists of a flexible and fatigue-resistant HMA
mix—possibly a “rich” mix with a slightly higher binder content.

3. Intermediate layers consist of stiff, rut-resistant mixes such as
Superpave or stone matrix asphalt (SMA).

4, The wearing course is a high-quality Superpave, open-graded
friction course, or SMA intended to be milled and replaced at

The thrust of Perpetual Pavement design is to combine a rut-resistant,
impermeable, and wear-resistant top structural layer with a rut-resistant and
durable intermediate layer and a fatigue-resistant and durable base layer.
Though a Perpetual Pavement is focused at high-volume traffic roadways,
the justification may be made for medium- and low-volume roads as well.

Essential to the Perpetual Pavement concept is the assumption that the
subgrade soils form a strong foundation. Improvements to drainage, soil
stabilization, or over excavation and replacement may be required to cre-
ate the proper foundation for a successful Perpetual Pavement.

The HMA layers in a Perpetual Pavement may include a “rich” bottom layer
with a slightly higher-than-typical binder content to create additional fatigue
resistance. The intermediate layers are intended to be stiff rut-resistant mixes,
such as the Superpave mixes that are currently used in the United States. The
mixes used for the wearing course can vary depending on the specific needs
of the area. Superpave mixes provide additional rut-resistance and a smooth
riding surface. SMA mixes might be selected for exceptional durability on
high-volume or heavily loaded streets and highways. An open-graded friction
course (OGFC) may be chosen to reduce splash and spray and/or reduce
pavement noise while providing a smooth-wearing surface. In a Perpetual
Pavement, the wearing course is intended to be milled and overlaid at regular
intervals to remove surface distresses and maintain smoothness.

At the core of the Perpetual Pavement design method is the concept of
an “endurance limit.” This concept holds that there is a level of strain below
which fatigue damage does not occur for any number of load repetitions. For
the condition of fatigue at the bottom of the lowest HMA layer, an endurance
limit of 70 microstrain has been established. Long-term research studies will
validate this limit. In the life of a Perpetual Pavement, horizontal tensile strain
at the bottom of the lowest asphalt-bound layer does not contribute to fatigue
failure if it does not exceed the 70 microstrain threshold.

Similarly, a threshold of 200 microstrain is currently understood to be
the limit for vertical compressive strain in subgrade materials. It is held
that a strain level below 200 microstrain does not contribute to subgrade
rutting in Perpetual Pavement design.

Perpetual Pavement design is conducted using M-E design principles for
layered elastic materials using the PerRoad Perpetual Pavement Software.
The cumulative-damage factor concept is applied to both fatigue and rut-
ting conditions, but rather than allow pavement damage to reach 1, dam-
age in a Perpetual Pavement design is never allowed to exceed 0.1.

6.3.1 SW-1 Asphalt Pavement Thickness
Design Software

SW-1 is the Asphalt Institute’s Windows-based asphalt pavement
thickness design software that provides users a single computer-based
design solution for highways, airports, heavy wheel applications, and other
applications such as parking lots. It includes the Asphalt Institute pave-
ment design curves for highways, airports, heavy wheel loads, and other
applications, which have been digitized for use in SW-1. Also included
is the DAMA analysis tool for advanced structural analysis. Tying it all
together is a graphical user interface to ease handling of user inputs,
pavement design reports, and management of data files.

One of the benefits of SW-1 is a versatile and powerful user interface,
as shown in Figure 6.6. From this interface, users collect input data,
report output, and manage data files for asphalt pavement design for
all types of applications. SW-1 uses a tabular format to clarify the type
of input required and to aide the user in stepping through a pavement
design.

A design wizard is automatically invoked on the initial setup of a new
problem. The wizard steps the user through the process of inputting all the
required data—climate, traffic, subgrade, materials. The final step through
the wizard leads to a design solution and the report page.

7.1. Types of Plant Facilities/
Production Processes

Two types of plant facilities/production processes for producing hot
mix asphalt CHMA) paving mixtures are popular today:

° Batch facilities

° Drum-mix facilities

In each type of facility, aggregates are proportioned according to the
requirements of the job-mix formula, dried and heated, and then mixed
with the required asphalt binder to create the proper ratio of aggregates
and liquid asphalt binder. With both types of facilities, supplemental fine
aggregate or liquid additives can be added and reclaimed asphalt pave-
ment (RAP) can be used to make recycled mixes. However, additive proce-
dures are slightly different in each process.

7.1.1 Batch Facilities

The first hot mix asphalt plants were batch facilities. These were popu-
lar from the early 1900s until the advent of drum-mixers in the 1970s.
Many batch facilities still exist, producing mix daily, although over 80
percent of HMA tonnage in North America is now produced with drum-
mix plants. Figure 7.1 shows the key equipment components in a batch
facility, and Figure 7.2 shows a cutaway view of the flow process through
the batch tower.

In the batch-plant process, individual aggregates are proportioned from
the cold feed bins onto a single collector belt and sent to the dryer to be
dried and heated. The dried and heated materials flow into a bucket eleva-
tor to be carried to the top of the batch tower and deposited onto sizing
screens. Multiple screen decks separate the aggregate into different sizes
and direct the fractionated aggregate into hot bins. The screens in the
tower determine the size of the aggregates in the hot bins and how the
blended aggregate mixture from the dryer is fractionated.

Aggregates are then dried, heated, sized, and stored in the hot holding
bins, for proportioning according to the mixture specified.

The mixture is made by weighing the fractionated aggregates stored in
the hot bins with an accumulative aggregate weigh hopper, positioned
below the hot bins. Each predetermined fraction of material is individu-
ally weighed into the aggregate weigh hopper until the entire batch is
weighed.

In a separate action, asphalt is weighed in a weighing device, typically
called an asphalt weigh bucket or asphalt scale.

After the aggregate and the binder are weighed, the aggregates are
dropped into the pugmill mixer to be blended together in what is called a

7.2.1 Liquid Asphalt Storage

Liquid asphalt binder is generally stored inside insulated and heated
tanks (see Figure 7.5) adjacent to the other plant equipment. Multiple
storage tanks are common at stationary facilities and are not uncommon
with portable plants. This allows the producer to keep different grades of
asphalt binder properly separated.

Tanks must be kept heated to maintain the asphalt binder at the proper
mixing temperature. Tanks are usually heated with hot oil using a heat
transfer oil system that keeps the other plant equipment at operating
temperature.

Tanks that store modified binders are often equipped with agitators
or stirrers to keep the modified binder constantly circulating. Circula-
tion pumps can be used for the same purpose, even without agitators or
stirrers. Some asphalt binders can have specific agitation requirements.

HMA producers are encouraged to consult with the liquid asphalt supplier
for proper storage requirements.

Pipes that transfer the liquid asphalt to the mixing area of the plant are
insulated and surrounded with an outer pipe or “jacket” containing hot oil
from the plant’s heating system to keep the binder at the proper tempera-
ture throughout the entire process.

Special processing equipment is available to blend mixture additives
into the hot asphalt lines or plant mixing area. Antistrip additives, fibers,
RAP, or other mixture modifiers are often added after the aggregate has
entered the drum. Special processing equipment is also used for mixing
crumb-rubber additives and latex into the liquid asphalt for special mixes.
This equipment varies by application requirement and is usually computer
controlled.

Plant personnel should monitor storage temperatures regularly to make
sure the binder is kept at the proper temperature. Thermometers in the
tanks and lines are connected to digital displays and graph recorders in the
control room for this purpose. Binder temperature affects not only pump-
ing and metering efficiency but also coating characteristics, so ensuring
that binder temperature is maintained throughout the process is critical for
proper mixing operations. Heater temperatures should be set at required
storage temperatures, or not more than 20°-30°F (11°-13°C) above those
temperatures. Excessively high temperatures cause the asphalt to build up
and harden around the heating elements. These excessively high tempera-
tures can also prematurely age the binder. The recording devices provide
visual proof that specification temperature limits have not been exceeded.
The objective of storing asphalt binders at an HMA plant is to maintain
an adequate supply of material to keep the plant running between binder
deliveries. The amount of storage needed at an HMA plant is a function of
anticipated production rate, type of binder(s), and the anticipated delivery
schedule.

7.22 Aggregate Stockpiling

Aggregates are delivered to plant sites by truck, rail, barge, or belt con-
veyor directly from the aggregate source. Aggregates are usually stockpiled
onsite and then fed into cold feed bins with a front-end loader. A plant
can also be fed directly from the stockpile by feeders under the stockpiles
or by trucks dumping directly into the cold feed bins. The most common
approach is to feed the bins from a stockpile with a front-end loader.

Operational concerns for HMA production at the plant site focus on the
following key areas.

Cross Contamination
Materials should be kept separated from one another by space or by
dividers before being loaded into the cold feed bins for mixing.

=== COMPLETE BOOK PDF PAGE 350 ===
Subgrade Contamination

If materials are moved with a front-end loader, the operator must ensure
that the bucket does not pick up subgrade materials along with the desired
aggregate. Producers usually treat the ground to stabilize it or place a base
aggregate-type material below the stockpile to minimize this problem. The
latest trend at permanent plant sites is to actually pave the stockpile area
with HMA to prevent subgrade contamination and to expedite moisture
drainage in the stockpiles.

Wheel and Track Contamination

Driving on the aggregate stockpiles with haul trucks or stockpiling
equipment can also cause contamination from materials that stick to the
vehicle wheels or tracks, and should be discouraged. The importance of
this guideline is relative to local conditions.

Breakdown/Gradation Change

Materials can change in gradation if haul trucks or stockpiling equipment
are allowed to drive on and to work the aggregate stockpile. Degradation
of aggregate materials is a function of material handling and aggregate
characteristics. The gradation of the material can change every time it is
handled. A thorough understanding of the aggregate materials and aggre-
gate handling procedures must be accounted for in the design and con-
struction process.

Segregation/Gradation Change

Materials that are not of uniform size are prone to segregation during
handling operations. When aggregates are dropped from a significant
height, such as from a stacking conveyor, or if aggregates are allowed to
flow down the face of a stockpile, segregation can occur. Both practices
should be discouraged. Conveyors that rise up and.down minimize this
problem. Elevated stockpiles do not always have to be constructed using
haul trucks and ramps. Available space for stockpiling, however, often dic-
tates compromises in best practices. Materials that are segregated slightly
can often be reblended satisfactorily if the loading equipment mixes coarse
sides and fine sides from the stockpile face before charging the cold-feed
bins. Segregation is probably the single biggest concern in stockpile man-
agement at a hot mix plant. If segregation concerns exist, material samples
can be taken directly from the discharge of the cold bins to verify whether
the gradations of the individual materials have been compromised.

Minimizing Stockpile Moisture

Providing a solid draining surface under the stockpile area not only
helps keep the subgrade material from contaminating the cold feed bins
but also helps keep aggregates dry. Moisture drastically affects the produc-
tion of the plant and fuel consumption. If moisture contents in the stock-
piles change, the mix temperature will also fluctuate unless the operating
characteristics (burner and draft settings) are constantly adjusted. This is

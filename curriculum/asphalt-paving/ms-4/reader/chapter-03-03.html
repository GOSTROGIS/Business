Gradation Adjustment for Different Specific Gravities

Aggregate gradations and grading curves are determined and expressed in percentages by weight for convenience of measurement. The grad- ing curve of each material is determined by weight using sieve analysis. However, grading specifications are established to require certain percent- ages of material to achieve the various volumetric sizes in the hot mix asphalt. The blending process assumes that all the aggregates have the same specific gravity. As long as the specific gravities of the individual aggregate stockpiles are reasonably alike, the percentages by weight will approximate the percentages by volume for all practical purposes.

However, when the specific gravities of the individual aggregates differ significantly (by 0.20 or more) and they are to be blended together to make a target gradation, the aggregate proportions that are set up in the plant controls (percentages by weight) should be adjusted for this vari- ance. Without this correction, the blend being made by weight at the plant to produce final volumetric proportions in the mix could deviate significantly from the blend of sizes designed in the laboratory on paper, since the different sized materials have different unit weights or densities. The adjustment is based on the fact that

VOLUME x SPECIFIC GRAVITY = WEIGHT

Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement

3.3.1 Introduction

Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) is removed and/or reprocessed pavement that contains asphalt and aggregates. RAP has become a very valuable commodity in the paving industry. Properly processed RAP yields high-quality, graded aggregates and small-aggregate agglomerations coated by asphalt cement (sometimes called black sand). Black sand contains a high percentage of (old) binder. Black sand alone is not considered RAP. Its addition to the new mix in high percentages will result in a dry and noncompactable mix. When RAP is included as a component in new HMA mixtures, the needed amount of new or virgin materials is reduced. Not only does this save money and natural resources, but it also eliminates a waste product that otherwise would require disposal. RAP has been used extensively in new HMA pavements for several decades and has consistently exhibited equal performance to mixtures made with all new material.

3.3.2 Reclaiming Existing Pavements

Processing RAP

The processing of RAP entails the removal of the existing asphalt pave- ment material and the crushing and screening of the material to reduce it to an appropriate size. Removal is generally accomplished by milling or full-depth conventional ripping.

Milling is achieved using a rotary-drum, cold-milling machine. The mill- ing or cutting drum on a milling machine contains a patterned layout of carbide teeth. As the milling machine passes over the pavement, the teeth on the rotating drum grind into the pavement to a controlled depth. Typi- cal mill depths have been up to 2 inches (50 millimeters) for a single pass, but equipment is now available to mill 12 inches (00 millimeters) or more. The milling or grinding action of the rotating teeth crushes the RAP as the material is removed such that almost all the particles are smaller than 2 inches (50 millimeters). Many times, the milled RAP is suitable for use in new HMA without further crushing or screening. Typical milling equipment (see Figure 3.12) is configured to pick up the milled material and place it on an integral conveyor belt where the material can be loaded directly into trucks for hauling to a production facility or stockpile site.

Because the RAP pieces before crushing basically con- tain aggregate with a typical dense-graded gradation, care should be taken in the RAP crushing operation not to destroy this existing RAP aggregate gradation nor to create new fractured faces in the aggregates themselves. In reality, both milling and crushing cause some aggregate degradation, with milling causing the greater amount. Hence, the gradation of milled RAP is generally finer than crushed RAP and the gradation of pavement is finer than virgin aggregates crushed with the same type of equipment.

Stockpiling

As is typical in all aspects of HMA construction, consistency of product is paramount, and the same holds true for RAP. RAP may be very con- sistent if it is removed from one project site. However, RAP may include materials from patches and surface treatments or consist of materials from multiple and differing projects, which can significantly increase its variability. Depending on the percentage of RAP used, variability may be experienced in the HMA in gradation, asphalt content, compaction, and volumetric properties. With higher percentages of RAP, it may be necessary to fractionate the RAP into multiple stockpiles.

Proper stockpiling procedures should control variability in the RAP. RAP materials with different asphalt content, different aggregate type, and different aggregate grading should be stockpiled separately. When logistics do not allow for separate stockpiles but instead require combining RAP products from different sources or with different properties, they should be blended together as well as possible during the various stages of pro- cessing and handling. This includes appropriate layer stacking during the stockpiling of the preprocessed material, appropriate stockpile load-out and feeding of crusher and/or screen hoppers by front-end loaders, and appropriate stockpile layering of the processed RAP.

Regardless of the source(s) or processing methods of the RAP, consolida- tion and moisture retention are two major areas of concern in stockpiled RAP. Consolidation of the RAP, coupled with moisture, tends to resolidify or “reagglomerate” the RAP particles into a mass, which can be difficult to rebreak into its individual processed particles. Low, horizontal stockpiles have historically been preferable to high, conical stockpiles in which it was believed that greater consolidation of the RAP took place. However, it has been found that, regardless of the size of the pile, a crust forms 8-10 inches (20-25 centimeters) thick over the outer surface of the stockpile and the RAP does not recompact or consolidate below the crust. The crust is easily penetrated and broken by a front-end loader. The crust helps to hinder mois- ture from penetrating into the RAP, and along with the greater slope areas of large conical piles, water tends to shed more easily, further preventing the compaction of the underlying material. As stated, the same thickness of crust is present in low, horizontal stockpiles, but front-end loaders have greater difficulty working around low piles. As with natural aggregates, stockpiles should be built on appropriate surfaces to prevent contamination.

Moisture retention in RAP material can significantly affect the produc- tion of HMA. Because of the asphalt binder component of RAP and the associated problems it can cause, RAP is not put through the dryer of a hot plant separately or with the virgin aggregates. The RAP is typically introduced into the HMA production process after the dryer, when the hot virgin aggregates from the dryer mix with and heat the RAP. Any moisture in the RAP is driven off primarily by the heat of the virgin aggregates. Because there is an upper threshold to which the aggre- gates can be heated, the heat available to the RAP is limited. Increased moisture content of the RAP material requires a reduction in the amount of RAP in the HMA or a reduction in the rate at which HMA is pro- duced. Hence, it is imperative to keep moisture out of RAP as much as possible. Much better drainage of RAP is achieved in tall conical stockpiles than in low, flat stockpiles. In areas of higher annual precipita- tion, it may be beneficial to protect RAP from moisture by covering it with tarps or by storing it under roofed structures.

3.4 Aggregate Additives

3.4.1 Introduction

Aggregate additives are materials that are incorporated with the natural aggregate to improve the overall performance of hot mix asphalt and/or to address various environmental concerns. These additives can be grouped into the following categories:

e Mineral filler

¢ Antistrip additives

° Other additives

Some of these aggregate additives have long been used in HMA. Others, although commercially available, have not been used extensively in rou- tine highway construction, rehabilitation, and pavement preservation. As such, it is recommended that a user agency evaluate their performance in relation to the agency’s individual needs and expectations to prove the usefulness of an aggregate additive regarding overall hot mix asphalt performance.

3.4.2 Mineral Filler

As discussed in “Composite-Aggregate Size Fractions” in this chapter, the composite-aggregate structure in HMA mixtures consists of three cate- gories of particle sizes: coarse aggregate, fine aggregate, and mineral filler. In this context, mineral filler is considered that portion of the composite aggregate passing the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. The mineral filler plays an important role in HMA mixtures because it fills a portion of the voids in a paving mix and serves to improve the cohesion of the asphalt binder and the stability of the mixture. It is especially important in stone matrix asphalt mixes where it contributes significantly to compactability, perme- ability, and in-service pavement performance.

There are times when a separate material must be included in the aggre- gate blend to make up or supplement that mineral filler fraction. ASTM D 242, Standard Specification for Mineral Filler for Bituminous Paving Mixtures, states: “Mineral filler shall consist of finely divided mineral mat- ter such as rock dust, slag dust, hydrated lime, hydraulic cement, fly ash, loess, or other suitable mineral matter. At the time of use, it shall be suf- ficiently dry to flow freely and essentially free from agglomerations.” This standard requires that the material be free from organic impurities and have a plasticity index no greater than 4. The dust fraction of crushed aggregates, including naturally occurring rocks (such as limestone) and synthetic aggregates (such as slag), can be used as mineral filler. Many times, the fine aggregate may be processed further in grinders or mills to create mineral filler.

Hydrated lime is a dry powder that comes from the specialized pro- cessing of limestone. Limestone is crushed and calcinated (controlled time and temperature heating) at temperatures above 1650°F (900°C) to create quicklime. The quicklime is in turn treated with sufficient water to satisfy its chemical affinity for water, creating hydrated lime. Hydrated lime is also used as an antistrip additive. See “Hydrated Lime” in this chapter.

Hydraulic cements are fine powders that chemically react with water creating a binder that sets and hardens into a stone-like material. The most commonly available hydraulic cement is Portland cement.

Fly ash is the fine-grain, powdery particulate material that is produced from the burning of pulverized coal in a coal-fired boiler or furnace and collected from the flue gas. Fly ash has been used as a substitute mineral filler in HMA mixtures for many years. Most fly ash sources are capa- ble of meeting the gradation requirements and other pertinent physical (nonplastic) and chemical (organic content) requirements of mineral filler specifications. It is possible that some sources of fly ash that have a high lime (CaO) content may also be useful as antistripping agents in HMA mixes. See “Fly Ash” in this chapter.

Loess comes from sedimentary, unconsolidated deposits of windblown, silty material. This fine material (cock flour) was created by the grinding of rocks during glacial activity. Loess is typically thought of as the silt that makes up the fertile topsoil in some parts of the world.

Kiln dusts are fine by-products of PCC and lime high-temperature rotary-kiln production operations that are captured in the air pollution control dust collection system (e.g., cyclones, electrostatic precipitators, and baghouses). Both fresh Cement Kiln Dust (CKD) and Lime Kiln Dust (LKD) can be used as mineral fillers in HMA mixtures. The lime compo- nents of the CKD and LKD can assist in promoting stripping resistance.

3.4.3 Antistrip Additives

Stripping in HMA mixtures has been referred to as the “loss of adhesion between the aggregate surface and asphalt cement binder in the presence of moisture.” Materials added to HMA to reduce moisture sensitivity and stripping are known as antistrip additives. Liquid antistrip is commonly used, and typically added at the asphalt refinery or terminal upon request. Only those additives that are added directly to the aggregate either in the stockpile or before introduction into a batch or drum mixer are discussed in this section.

Hydrated Lime

Hydrated lime is the most common aggregate-added antistrip material. It reacts with aggregates, strengthening the bond between the asphalt binder and the aggregate. Quicklime should not be added to HMA unless it first has been completely hydrated. If quicklime remains unhydrated in the HMA, it will change to Ca(OH), when it comes into contact with water during the service life of the pavement. This reaction (i.e., changing from CaO to Ca(OH),) is expansive and will create a volume change in the HMA and losses in strength and performance.

Hydrated lime can be added to HMA at various stages during the HMA production process. These stages range from the addition of lime to aggre- gate several days before proportioning and mixing to adding dry lime to the drum mixer at the point of the asphalt binder entry. Lime can be successfully proportioned and mixed in HMA in both batch and drum mixers. Dry lime can be added to dry aggregate and wet aggregate. Mois- ture levels in wet aggregate are typically 2-3 percent above the saturated surface dried condition of the aggregate. Moisture ionizes the lime and helps distribute it on the aggregate surface. Lime-treated aggregates can be stockpiled for “marination” or can be conveyed directly to the drying and mixing portion of the HMA production unit.

Lime slurries made from hydrated lime or quicklime have also been used. Lime-slurried aggregates are conveyed directly to the drying and mixing portion of the HMA facility or placed into stockpiles for marina- tion. The use of lime slurries has several advantages: improved resistance of the treated hot mix to stripping, reduced dusting associated with the addition of dry lime to the aggregate, and improved distribution of lime on the aggregate. However, the use of lime slurries adds more water than is typically used for conventional lime applications and can substantially increase the water content of the aggregate before entering the drying and mixing portions of the HMA facility. Increased fuel consumption and reduced HMA production can result. The use of lime slurries also requires purchasing or renting specialized equipment to prepare the lime slurry at the mixing operation site.

Fly Ash

The performance of fly ash as an antistrip has been mixed. It is believed that the calcium content, and in particular the free or available lime (CaO) content, is instrumental in the performance of a fly ash as an aid to prevent- ing asphalt stripping. Fly ash can be introduced into the HMA production process in the same ways as lime.

Polymeric Treatment

Polymeric aggregate surface treatment (SBR latex) provides a protective barrier on the aggregate that repels water and waterproofs the aggregate while providing an improved bonding with the asphalt. When properly applied, the polymeric-aggregate treatment will turn a hydrophilic aggregate into a hydrophobic aggregate, increasing the water resistance of the HMA.

One of the additional benefits of using polymeric-aggregate treatment is that the amount of asphalt binder required in the mixture may be lowered, resulting in cost savings for the HMA contractor. As the polymer coats the porous aggregate, less asphalt binder is absorbed into the aggregate.

The SBR latex concentrate must be diluted to 15 percent solids before use, which is usually accomplished automatically when using an approved application unit. The latex is then applied to the aggregate on the aggre- gate feed belt just before entry into the drum dryer. Typical latex appli- cation rates vary from 0.05 percent to 0.10 percent with an estimated reduction in asphalt binder content of 0.85 percent to 0.40 percent for marginal aggregates.

When compared with conventional HMA, SMA mixtures generally have a higher asphalt content and have a higher concentration of coarse aggre- gate. These conditions can cause the asphalt binder to drain from the mixture, by grav- ity, during transportation and placement, which is commonly referred to as draindown. Scrap Tires (Ground Rubber)

Ground rubber may be sized from particles as large as 34 inch (19 millimeters) to as fine as the No. 100 sieve (150 microns) depending on the type of size- reduction equipment and the intended application. Production of fine-grain tire rubber is achieved by buffing at ambient temperature, while larger pieces of tire rubber are produced in a cryonic process (at a very low temperature where rubber is brittle). The production of ground rubber is achieved by granulators, hammer mills, or fine-grinding machines. Granulators typically produce particles that are regularly shaped and cubical with a comparatively low-surface area. The steel-belt fragments are removed by a magnetic sepa- rator. Fiberglass belts or fibers are separated from the finer rubber particles, usually by an air separator.

Ground rubber has been used as a fine-aggregate substitute in HMA pavements. In this process, ground-rubber particles are added to the HMA as a fine aggregate in a gap-graded friction-course type of mixture. This process, commonly referred to as the dry process, typically uses ground- rubber particles ranging from approximately “4 inch (6.4 millimeters) down to the No. 20 sieve (0.85 millimeter). HMA mixes in which ground- rubber particles are added as a portion of the fine aggregate are referred to as rubberized asphalt. Another process is where very fine-grain tire rubber is homogenized in the asphalt at the refinery or terminal. This is called terminal blend asphalt rubber.

Roofing-Shingle Scrap (Mineral Matter Only)

There are two types of roofing-shingle scrap. They are referred to as tear- off roofing shingles and roofing-shingle tabs—also called prompt roofing- shingle scrap. Tear-off roofing shingles are generated during the demolition or replacement of existing roofs. Roofing-shingle tabs are generated when new asphalt shingles are trimmed during production to the required physical dimensions. The quality of tear-off roofing can be quite variable.

The largest component of roofing shingles (60-70 percent by mass) is the mineral material. There are several types in each shingle. These can include ceramic granules (comprising crushed-rock particles—typically trap rock, coated with colored, ceramic oxides), lap granules (coal slag ground to roughly the same size as the ceramic granules), backsurfacer sand (washed, natural sand used in small quantities to keep packaged shingles from sticking together), and asphalt stabilizer (powered limestone that is mixed into the asphalt).

Roofing shingles incorporated into HMA mixes not only modify the asphalt binder but also, depending on the size of the shredded material, function like an aggregate or mineral filler. Organic-felt and glass-felt particles in particular tend to function like a mineral filler substitute.

4 The Drum-Mix Production Process

7.4.1 The Development of the Drum-Mix Process

Drum-mixers started appearing during the early 1970s. The first drum- mixers used elongated drying drums not only to dry and heat the aggregate but also to mix the liquid asphalt with the heated aggregate to produce the final hot mix all in the same drying cylinder.

These first drums were parallel-flow dryers, meaning that the aggre- gate and the process gas flowed in the same direction. The wet, ambient aggregates were introduced to the end of the dryer where the burner was located, and the aggregate was dried as it moved away from the burner. In this fashion, the liquid-asphalt binder could be introduced into the exit end/cooler part of the drum.

This process required less capital expenditure for initial equipment, greater mobility for relocation to remote projects, less installation time, less maintenance, and higher RAP capability.

This process could not have been developed without the following:

> The availability of uniformly produced, consistently sized aggregate

° The development of large, inexpensive variable-speed motors that

can be fitted to the cold-feed bins to easily proportion aggregates to the desired final mix gradations

e The development of belt scales and flow meters for measuring the

flow of materials continuously

° The development of finished mix storage silos to elevate the material

so it can be dispensed into trucks

Each equipment development occurred independently and it became obvious that the need for sizing and fractioning material in a batch plant was no longer necessary.

Drum-mix plants were lower to the ground, easier to move, and less expensive to buy and maintain than batch facilities. As a result, they became the process of choice for hot mix asphalt producers starting in the late 1970s, and today over 80 percent of the mix produced in the United States is with drum-mix plants.

The driving force behind this design innovation in the 1980s was a desire to create a drum-mix facility that was more environmentally friendly by introducing the new liquid-asphalt binder outside of the main gas stream used to dry and heat the aggregate materials. This reduced the discharge of very small (1-5 micron) hydrocarbon particles in the stack gases, which sometimes created visible emissions or odors from the drum-mix facilities. These new drum-mixers eliminate this problem.

Figures 7.23 through 7.26 show the different styles of drum-mixers commonly found in the field at this time. It should be noted that many batch-plant operators are now converting their plants from batch plants to drum-mixers by replacing the tower with a mixing drum or continuous pugmill mixer or by bypassing the tower and using a small mixing drum mounted beside the tower, resulting in a hybrid system known as a com- bination plant. This allows operators to use the same dryer and baghouse to dry the material. As noted previously in this chapter, the cold-feed bins were probably already converted to belt feeders with variable-speed drives. By installing a continuous mixing device, a drum-mix liquid asphalt pumping and metering system, and drum-mix controls, the batch-plant operator now has all the advantages of drum-mix operation. Of primary interest to producers is the ability to run at the production rate possible with the dryer and not be limited by the capabilities of the screening unit, being able to run higher percentages of RAP, and reducing maintenance costs, thereby becoming more competitive in the marketplace. Having consistently sized aggregates available makes this conversion possible.

by proportioning these individual bins to the target mix formula. This, of course, depends on the availability of consistently sized individual aggregates from the aggregate supplier. If the individual aggregates are not consistent in gradation, then a consistent hot mix cannot be produced with a drum-mixer. Drum-mixers and the drum-mixing process depend on the availability of consistent aggregates to make high-quality mix.

The output from the cold-feed bins is really controlled in two ways. The primary adjustment is the variable-speed motor driving the belt feeder. In addition to this, the bins are fitted with adjustable gates. This procedure allows the producer to control the maximum and minimum flows from the bin. Some mixes require a large quantity of one material—in this case, the gate is opened wide. Some mixes require a small quantity of another material, and in this case the bin gate might be lowered. Variable-speed motors can be inconsistent in their flow at very low rates. They can also reach their maximum speed at the 95 percent, 96 percent, or 97 percent range. The most linear range is typically between 10 percent and 90 per- cent speed. Some are linear between 5 percent and 95 percent speed. It is best practice, therefore, to use the adjustable gate to ensure that the motor-drive combination is functioning in the 5-95 percent or 10-90 per- cent range at all times. Between these speed ranges, if output is charted to speed, the line usually has a consistent and predictable slope.

With a drum-mix plant, the aggregate belt scale is used for blending the asphalt and other ingredients to the combined-aggregate flow. The belt scale is the device that measures the combined-aggregate flow. The pres- ence of a belt scale makes it very easy to calibrate the feeders. The flow of the material from each bin can be easily determined in tons per hour at different flow rates by simply allowing the aggregate material from the cold-feed bin to flow over the belt scale and into a truck. Timed runs can also be used, and the flow rate calculated, but using the belt scale is very simple. Virtually all plants use this technique for calibrating the feeders. The aggregate flow and gradation is controlled volumetrically by using predetermined flow output for different speed ranges and then back- calculating the required speed for each feeder after determining the flow required from each feeder. Executing the feeder calibration process prop- erly is critical for success with a drum-mixer. Feeder calibration should also be checked and re-verified on a regular basis.

The asphalt pumping and metering system on a drum-mix plant is a con- tinuously operating unit. It pumps liquid asphalt continuously and measures the result of that flow with a meter. It can increase or decrease the flow dynamically with the plant’s automation.

There are two key equipment elements to this process—the asphalt flow control mechanism and the asphalt meter or measuring device.

Several types of flow control mechanisms have been used over the years, but all manufacturers now favor a variable-speed motor on the asphalt pump for this purpose. If more asphalt is required, the pump is accelerated. If less asphalt is needed, the pump is slowed. Most producers have converted their plants to this technique because it eliminates the maintenance on the wear parts of the previous systems and is generally considered trouble-free.

Figure 7.30 illustrates how this process works. The belt scale reports the wet aggregate flow to the control system. The control system will adjust the wet scale value for the moisture in the material, based on the aggregate- moisture data entered by the operator. Entering the proper moisture quantity is important and is typically done by entering the moistures of the individual materials into a “moisture screen.” The control system then calculates the combined moisture based on the mix recipe and the individual material moistures, and removes this combined moisture percentage from the wet weight to arrive at a dry weight. The plant control assumes the dryer will dry the material adequately and therefore uses this calculated dry weight signal for proportioning the liquid-asphalt binder. Based on the required asphalt percentage in the mix, which comes from the mix formula, the control sys- tem calculates the required asphalt flow. If the aggregate flow is increased, a new target asphalt flow rate is calculated and the control system will adjust the asphalt flow. If the aggregate flow decreases, the opposite happens. This entire process is continuous.

Changeable delay times are built into the flow-control circuits. When the plant is set up, tests are done to determine the length of the delay time between the belt scale reading and the aggregate material reaching the asphalt injection point for that plant. Therefore, changes in the aggregate scale do not cause an instantaneous change in the asphalt flow. The plant control system has delay times set up not only for the asphalt flow but also for each cold-feed bin and for every other ingredient. These flow delays are very important for a continuous process. When changes to production are made, mix integrity must be maintained, so the delay times are carefully calibrated to ensure that production-rate changes and mix changes can be executed without shutting down the process. An alarm notifies the operator when a mix change is complete and material can be directed to another storage silo.

Ingredients other than asphalt binder, including other liquid additives and bulk additives, are controlled in the same fashion as the liquid asphalt binder. They all take their key from the aggregate belt scale signal. When mixes containing RAP are being produced, the control system also reads the belt scale on the RAP conveyor and adjusts the virgin-asphalt binder to compensate for the asphalt that is included in the RAP.

The belt scale reading is very critical in producing quality HMA in the drum-mixer process. Additional attention needs to be paid to the area of the main belt where the belt scale is located. Before startup, the area should be inspected and cleaned to make sure the framework is free of dust and dirt buildup that may affect the readings. Wind screens mounted around the belt scale will prevent wind loading that can artificially add or subtract weight from the readings. A belt tensioner or deadman (weighted idler roller) to take up slack as the belt stretches helps to keep constant tension on the belt as it passes across the belt scale and helps improves the accuracy of the readings.

Mix Deficiencies and Problem Solving

Successful mix production requires a diligent approach to both the calibration process and daily operational best practices. For successful placement, a mix must have accurate and consistent aggregate gradation, asphalt content, and temperature. The success of the in-place product starts with the mix-production process.

The entire production, transport, and placement process should run smoothly and consistently. Starts and stops increase the likelihood of mix inconsistencies at the plant, whether a drum-mix or batch plant is used. Starts and stops in placement also affect the quality of the placed product. Every activity—from scheduling the production rate of the plant, to schedul- ing the lay-down and compaction rate of the placement crew, to selecting the number of trucks for the project, to the cycling of those trucks—should be as organized as possible. Success will directly correlate to not only the proficiency in mix production and the proficiency of the placement crew but also to the smooth coordination of each stage of the process.

Even with the best intentions, things can turn out wrong. Sometimes equipment fails. Often the failures are operational. When test results and mix results are not as anticipated, it is useful to have a troubleshooting or problem-solving guide to help find the cause of the problem so it can be corrected.

The following two sections provide problem-solving guides, one for drum-mix plant production and one for batch-plant production. They are organized by the types of tests typically performed on the mix—gradation analysis, asphalt content analysis, voids calculation, etc. The two sections do not cover every possible cause of every possible mix deficiency, but they do cover most common problems found in the field.

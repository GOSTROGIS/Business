12.1 Introduction

This chapter provides practical information about methods and termi- nology that applies to the use of asphalt in pavement maintenance.

Large differences in soil types, climate, traffic, and other factors make for a variety of problems, even within small areas. Some regions have heavy rainfall, while others are hot and dry; some pavements must accom- modate vehicles carrying heavy loads, while others are subjected to only lightweight traffic.

Despite these differences, there are maintenance methods that can be used equally well in all regions. This chapter provides an overview of the methods used to maintain hot mix asphalt (HMA) pavements.

There are about two million miles of paved roads across the United States. All require maintenance. The original investment in these structures was in the billions of dollars. Their maintenance now costs millions of dollars annually. Obviously, timely and proper maintenance is imperative. Pave- ment maintenance is the routine work performed to keep a pavement, under normal traffic conditions and forces of nature, as nearly as possible in its as-constructed condition. Preventive maintenance not only preserves the pavement surface but also prevents early deterioration. It should not be regarded as a temporary expedient but as an investment in the pavement structure and as insurance against costly pavement renovation.

The three categories of pavement maintenance operations are the following: ;

° Preventive maintenance is the routine work done to prevent deterio- ration of a pavement. It includes crack sealing, surface treatments, thin overlays, drainage maintenance, etc. The idea behind preventive main- tenance is to expend funds and materials while the pavement is in rela- tively good condition in order to retard deterioration and thus prolong the life of the pavement. This can be done at a very reasonable cost. A good analogy for preventive maintenance is the painting of a house. When the paint fades, it is repainted; the responsible homeowner does not wait until the paint is cracked and peeling. As with painting a house, the effectiveness of the preventive maintenance is directly related to the condition of the pavement. Conducting preventive maintenance activities on a sound pavement in good condition is very effective. Conducting an inappropriate repair (in either method or timing) can accelerate the rate of pavement deterioration. In summary, preventive maintenance is com- pleting the right maintenance activity at the right time.

° Corrective maintenance consists of those activities that are accom- plished after a deficiency occurs. It is performed when the pave- ment needs repair, and it is the most costly kind of maintenance. It is

12.2 Patching

Most pavements require patching at some time. If holes do not occur from traffic loading and natural causes, then service cuts and trenches produce them. The kind of defects requiring patching vary from cracked areas and shallow abrasions to deep holes.

Patching requires skill and close supervision. Prompt repair of small breaks helps to control expenditures because once an area is broken and water enters the subgrade, a large failure will result.

42.2.1 Materials

Hot Mix Asphalt

HMA mixes make long-lasting patches that rapidly develop stability. They should be used whenever it is practical and economical. See Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design,” and Chapter 7, “Manufacture of Hot Mix Asphalt.”

Cold Mixes

Other maintenance materials are cold-plant asphalt mixtures employing emulsified asphalt for immediate use or for short-term stockpiling. Slow- curing cutback asphalt or emulsion containing solvent may be used for stockpiled patching mixes. When using cutback, be sure to check local envi- ronmental regulations. See Chapter 15, “Special Uses of Asphalt and HMA.”

Proprietary Mixes

Producers have developed proprietary mixes, with special blends of aggregate and specialized binders, for specific purposes. As a rule, these mixes work well when used for their intended purpose. Information on these mixes can be found in trade magazine advertisements.

Specialty Mixes (Innovative State Highway Mixes)

Many state Department of Transportation (DOT) maintenance divisions have innovative mixes they have developed for pothole patching, spalled concrete joints, skin patching, etc. The DOTs are a good source of informa- tion about these specialty mixes.

In general, it is prudent to check with the local DOT to determine what materials are recommended, especially regarding proprietary mixes.

12.2.4 Spray-Injection Patching

Spray-injection patching has existed since the early 1980s. The proce- dure gained prominence after the Strategic Highway Research Project of the late 1980s and early 1990s determined that it is a viable method of repairing small pavement defects with semipermanent repairs, particularly during inclement weather (wet and/or cold weather).

Crack Sealing/Filling

Crack sealing/filling has two purposes:

° To prevent the intrusion of water into the underlying layers of the pavement structure, thus further weakening the pavement

° To prevent incompressible materials from entering the crack, thus causing further deterioration as the pavement expands and contracts with temperature changes

Crack sealing is usually done with liquid asphalt, cutbacks, or asphalt emulsions, and is considered a temporary procedure. These crack seal- ers usually maintain a tightly sealed crack only as long as the pavement remains stationary, both longitudinally and vertically. Once expansion and contraction or excessive vertical movement occurs, the seal’s effective- ness can be lost and the cracks must be resealed. In climates with severe temperature changes, resealing may be necessary every year. In moderate or mild climates, resealing will be necessary less often. Although the cost per unit of this type of crack sealing is relatively low, the frequency with which it must be done might dictate a more lasting solution.

Crack filling is accomplished using a specially prepared hot poured sealant. Crack filling is considered a permanent seal. Three to eight years is the expected life, depending on the material used, the pavement condi- tion, and the techniques used when applying the crack seal.

Along with proper drainage, crack sealing/filling is probably the single most important maintenance activity. Many pavement distresses can be traced to the intrusion of water into the pavement structure. Keeping water out of the pavement is essential to delay or prevent more serious distresses.

Some guidelines regarding crack sealing follow, but the details may vary according to local policies:

° Hairline cracks are % inch @ millimeters) or less in width and too small to seal effectively. If numerous hairline cracks cover an area, a surface seal can be used for the repair. Fog seals, chip seals, slurry seals, and sand seals work well. This procedure is sometimes referred to as mass crack filling. The surface treatment selected must be fluid enough to flow into the hairline cracks.

° Small cracks are ¥% inch (3 millimeters) to % inch (12.5 millimeters) in width and usually routed to a width of 4 inch (6 millimeters) or greater to provide a reservoir for the sealant. The crack is then cleaned and sealed. If the cracks are more than 2 inches (50 millimeters) deep, a backer rod should be installed to conserve sealant.

° Medium cracks are % inch (12.5 millimeters) to %4 inch (19 mil- limeters) wide and usually require only cleaning and sealing. A backer rod should be installed in cracks that are more than 2 inches (50 milli-meters) deep.

12.3.2 Crack-Sealing Procedures

Clean the cracks using high-pressure air, sandblasting, wire brushing, hot-air blasting, or high-pressure water.

Cleaning is an essential step, because when the crack is not thoroughly dried and cleaned, the sealant will not adhere to the sides of the crack. The most effective procedure is sandblasting, but it is very labor intensive. Hot-air blasting is done with a hot compressed-air lance or heat lance con- nected to an air compressor (see Figure 12.11). This method helps dry the crack, and when the sealing operation follows closely behind the hot-air drying, the heated crack surfaces help the sealant adhere to the crack. Care should be taken when using a heat lance. The heat lance produces superheated air that can burn the binder in the mix along the crack sur- faces. This will cause early failure of the sealant.

After cleaning, the cracks should be checked for depth. A backer rod should be placed in large deep cracks to conserve sealant. The backer rod should be a compressible, non-shrinking, nonabsorbent material with a melting point higher than the temperature of the sealant. The backer rod should be about 25 percent wider than the crack so that it does not slip into or float out of the crack after installing the sealant.

Once the cracks are prepared, they are sealed with liquid asphalt. The material may be applied using hand-carried pour pots, wheel-mounted pour pots, or the wand of a small distributor. Pour only the amount of material that will seal the crack. Do not try to completely fill the crack because this is a waste of sealant. The idea is to just coat the surfaces of the crack, with a small amount of sealant settling in the bottom of the crack (see Figure 12.12).

Any spillage or excess material on the pavement surface should be removed with a squeegee. Any surface so cleared of material should also be dusted with blotter sand to prevent tracking of the sealant by traffic.

This procedure applies to small, medium, and large cracks. Hairline cracks will not accept sealant, and because hairline cracks are so nar- row, significant amounts of moisture will not penetrate anyway. If hairline cracks are numerous, a mass crack-filling procedure should be used.

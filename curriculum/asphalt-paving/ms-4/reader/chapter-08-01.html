8.1. Pre-paving Conference

The key to a successful paving project is good communication between the paving crew, plant personnel, and the agency or owner. This communi- cation generally begins with the pre-paving conference. At this conference, the contractor’s personnel should explain their project plans and establish a meaningful schedule to meet the agency’s or owner’s expectations.

The details of the project should be covered so that all the affected par- ties understand the “who, what, why, when, and how.” The pre-paving con- ference can be as simple as the contractor’s representative meeting with the owner on the project site to explain his plan to pave the project—or as complicated as an organized meeting with all interested parties (including the owner, the designer, the inspectors, personnel from the testing firms, the contractor’s plant and paving personnel, utility representatives, etc.). No matter what form this pre-paving meeting takes, it is very critical and will save time, money, and confusion provided it is held well before HMA placement begins.

The discussion of the project details allows everyone involved with the project to identify concerns they may have with the proposed schedule and the possible impacts on pavement users (in terms of lane closures), traffic patterns, on businesses adjacent to the project, and local or regional events such as major sporting events.

The most important item to be discussed at the pre-paving conference is safety—of the construction personnel as well as of the traveling public. Clear responsibility for the maintenance of traffic-control devices, such as pavement marking and flagging, should be delineated. Attention to proj- ect safety is a moral, legal, and financial responsibility of the contracting owner or agency, the contractor, all subcontractors, and suppliers.

Testing and sampling methods and frequencies should be reviewed at the pre-paving conference to ensure that everyone involved in the project understands the following:

¢ The purpose of the testing

° The frequency of the testing

° Who will conduct the tests

¢ How and to whom the test results will be reported

Preparing the Surface for Paving

6.2.1 Surface Preparation and Protection

A properly prepared foundation, which includes adequate surface and subsurface drainage and proper compaction during construction, will help to ensure a long life for a pavement structure. Proper drainage is extremely important. The necessity for lowering the water table below the depth of frost penetration or the bottom of the improved subgrade by lateral drain- age or subsurface drainage is generally accepted by paving technologists as an essential element of good construction.

Proper compaction of each layer of embankment, subgrade, improved subgrade, and base course is equally important. Compaction greatly increases the supporting power of the subgrade. When the subgrade is not sufficiently compacted, additional consolidation may occur under traf- fic, resulting in settlement and possible failure.

The following roadway surfaces are generally considered unpaved sur- faces:

* Subgrade

° Improved subgrade

* Untreated base

° Nonsurfaced granular roadway

Subgrade Compaction tests are made in the laboratory on materials to be used in construction to determine the maximum practical density that can be obtained. Laboratory densities are determined using AASHTO T 180. Recommended subgrade compaction criterion are as follows: ° Cohesive soils—minimum 95 percent density in top 12 inches (300 millimeters) and minimum 90 percent for all fill areas below the top 12 inches (300 millimeters)

° Cohesionless soils—minimum 100 percent density in top 12 inches (300 millimeters) and minimum 95 percent for all fill areas below top 12 inches (300 millimeters)

A specifying engineer should verify these requirements with the appro- priate local, state, or in some cases, federal agency where the project is located.

The selected moisture content for compacting cohesive soils should provide the highest remolded strength consistent with expansion consid- erations. Specifications usually require the moisture content to be within +2 percent of the optimum moisture content. Generally, fine-graded nonexpansive soils should be compacted to 1 or 2 percent below the optimum moisture content for best results. Fine-graded expansive soils

In some instances, this requires scarifying, adding moisture, replacement, and recompaction to produce uniformity in the subgrade and to avoid differential settlement.

Although compaction increases the stability of most soils, some soils decrease in stability when scarified, worked, and rolled. Some soils also shrink excessively during dry periods and expand excessively when allowed to absorb moisture. They may also lose strength when reworked— for example, silts that can become “quick” when remolded. When these conditions exist, special treatment is required under the supervision of a qualified geotechnical engineer.

Surface irregularities in an HMA pavement structure can result from traffic densification of the underlying courses, usually in the wheel paths. Such irregularities indicate that one or more courses were not adequately compacted during construction or that soft, unstable areas exist in the structure. To detect these deficiencies, proof rolling of the subgrade is sometimes performed during construction. Proof rolling is the application of heavy, pneumatic-tire rollers or large loaded dump trucks over the sub- grade, usually following initial compaction by conventional means. Proof rolling accomplishes two purposes: it locates unstable areas and achieves additional compaction.

Prepared Subgrade

A prepared subgrade is one that has been worked and compacted. This may be the foundation soil or a layer of stabilized soil, select soil, or oth- erwise improved subgrade material. The riding quality of the pavement surface depends largely on proper construction and preparation of the foundation material. The roadway should be shaped and rolled so that the paving equipment has no difficulty in placing material at a uniform thick- ness and smooth grade. Weather conditions should be suitable, and the roadway surface should be firm, dust-free, dry, or just slightly damp when paving operations are started.

Untreated Base

Untreated granular bases should be properly shaped and compacted immediately before paving. This procedure will restore any proposed line or grade that may have been damaged during construction. When asphalt pave- ment courses are to be placed on an untreated aggregate base, loose granular particles and dust should be lightly swept from the surface. Care should be taken, however, not to dislodge or otherwise disturb the bond of the aggre- gate in the surface of the base. When the loose particles have been properly removed, only the tops of the aggregate in the surface will be exposed. After the surface has been cleaned, it is ready to be primed with asphalt. For a discussion about prime coats, see Section 8.2.4, “Prime Coats.”

Nonsurfaced Granular Roadway

If an HMA pavement is placed on an existing granular roadway, the sur- face should be shaped similar to untreated granular bases. The granular roadway differs primarily in that it has been used as a roadway by traf- fic and has typically been in use for a considerable time. It is advisable, when the granular roadway is rough and uneven, to bring the surface to the proper line and grade by scarifying the top few inches, blending in more aggregate material as necessary, compacting, and then priming with asphalt. If the granular roadway has had a previous asphalt treatment and is rough and uneven, but structurally sound, it is advisable to place a leveling course of HMA ahead of the first layer of the surface course. Oth- erwise, the surface may have to be recycled by scarifying the surface and mixing it with a portion of the existing base. The engineer should inspect the surface well in advance of placing the surface courses and determine what grade corrections need to be made. As with untreated granular bases, the surface should be cleaned and swept before initiating paving opera- tions, including any leveling courses that may be needed.

8.2.2 Preparation of Existing Pavement for Overlay

The amount of preparation needed for an existing HMA pavement before overlay will depend on the condition and the type of distress in the existing pavement. Portions of the pavement that have “failed” (fatigue/ alligator cracking) should be removed and replaced with full-depth HMA. Potholes need to be properly repaired, cracks need to be sealed or filled, and rutting areas must be removed by cold milling. If these distresses cover more than 40 percent of the pavement area, the use of cold in situ recycling or full-depth reclamation should be considered. The procedures for proper pre-overlay repairs are described in Chapter 12, “Maintenance of Asphalt Pavements,” and the procedures for cold milling, recycling, and full-depth reclamation are described in Chapter 13, “Rehabilitation Meth- ods for Asphalt Pavements.” The repair procedures for Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements are described in Chapter 14, “Rehabilitation of Concrete Pavements with HMA.”

8.2.3 Horizontal and Vertical Grade Control

Construction of a high quality smooth HMA pavement requires that the subgrade and base course be built to the correct horizontal and vertical grades. Before placement of the first lift of the HMA pavement on the base course, elevations should be checked to ensure that the correct grades have been achieved. Grades for aggregate bases should generally be within 0.05- 0.10 foot (15-30 millimeters) of the plan elevations. Additionally, the surface

8.2.4 Prime Coats and Tack Coats

Prime Coats

A prime coat is an application of a penetrating liquid asphalt (cutback, specially designed emulsion, or other suitable material) applied with a dis- tributor (see Figure 8.1) to a compacted aggregate base course. The func- tion of a prime coat is to provide a protective layer to prevent the surface of the aggregate base course from raveling under construction traffic. It also provides a waterproof layer, preventing it from absorbing or losing excess moisture before paving. A prime coat helps bond the top layer of the granular base to the first layer of HMA pavement. Although this bond- ing helps prevent slippage between the two layers, it is very weak and should not be considered when overall strength of the pavement is ana- lyzed. The prime coat is applied to an absorbent surface, such as a granular base or prepared subgrade, with an asphalt distributor truck. A cutback asphalt (such as MC-30, MC-70, or a specially designed emulsified asphalt) is typically applied at a rate of 0.20-0.50 gallons per square yard (0.9-2.3 liter per square meter) on the prepared surface. The asphalt penetrates the surface and should be entirely absorbed by the base layer. A prime coat should not be applied when the surface is wet, the air temperature is below 50°F (10°C), or the air temperature over the preceding 24 hours has been 40°F (4.5°C) or less.

In many areas, the use of cutback asphalt is not permitted because of the release of volatiles into the atmosphere. Local air quality requirements should be checked to determine if cutbacks can be used. As a substitute, asphalt suppliers have developed special penetrating emulsion materials for use as prime coats, which are spray applied in the same manner as cutback asphalts.

Tack Coats

Tack coats are a very light application of liquid asphalt applied with a distributor to an existing nonporous surface. The purpose of a tack coat is to promote bonding between the underlying surface and the new HMA layer. Normally, an asphalt emulsion or hot asphalt cement is used for a tack coat, but some agencies allow the use of other asphalt products. The tack coat should be placed on a clean, dry pavement, free of any materials that might prevent bonding. The application rate will vary depending on the type and condition of the surface being overlaid. The goal is to place a thin, uniform coating of asphalt covering 90-100 percent of the pavement surface. The application rate for a tack coat is normally 0.05-0.15 gallons per square yard (0.25-0.70 liter per square meter) of diluted slow-setting cationic or anionic emulsion (SS-1, SS-1h, CSS-1, or CSS-ih). A diluted emulsion is an emulsion that has been diluted using one part water to one part asphalt emulsion. If a given emulsion contains 65 percent residual asphalt in its original state, then after a 50-50 dilution, the product applied to the roadway would contain a 32.5 percent residual asphalt. Spread rate and uniformity are hard to control when using an asphalt distributor set at a very low application rate. By diluting the tack coat, the emulsion can be applied at a much higher spread rate while providing a uniformly thin coating of residual asphalt. A low application rate is usually used between new or un-trafficked pavement layers. An intermediate rate is required when resurfacing an existing, relatively smooth, asphalt pavement. The highest rate is used on old oxidized, cracked, pocked, or milled HMA or PCC pavement surfaces.

The asphalt emulsion should break and cure before placement of asphalt concrete. Shortly after placement of the tack coat, the emulsion will break. The color will change from brown to black, and the water will begin to evaporate. Once the water has evaporated, the mechanical properties of the residual asphalt will return and result in a strong adhesive bond. The rate of this breaking-and-setting process will depend on the environmen- tal conditions. Generally, the emulsions used for tack coats will break and be fully cured shortly after placement. During this process, equipment (rollers, delivery trucks, etc.) should be kept off the surface. Tack coats leaving surplus asphalt on the surface can form a slip plane. Excessive asphalt may also work its way up into the overlying course, which may eventually flush asphalt to the surface. A light tack coat does no harm and it bonds the surfaces, a necessity for good construction. Work should be planned so that no more than the necessary tack coat for the paving shift is placed on the surface. All traffic not essential to the work should be kept off the tack coat.

Vertical faces of existing pavements such as curbs and gutters should also be sprayed or painted with a uniform coating of approved tack material. This work should be done such that exposed curb or gutter surfaces are not stained. The use of low-penetration tack material such as an SS-1h or CSS-1h will also reduce the amount of tracking. Tracking is the unwanted coating or staining of adjacent pavement surfaces, both on and off the project, by trucks or equipment tires.

A continuous flow of HMA material from the plant to the paver ensures a consistently high-quality HMA pavement. The tonnage to be produced, the size and types of trucks, the time to make one trip from plant to paver, how much HMA to be placed and compacted—all are details that must be considered. These parameters dictate the necessary number of trucks. The time between each delivery must be maintained at a consis- tent level, and the trucks must not be allowed to bunch up at the paver or at the plant. Trucks may be delayed in traffic (construction traffic con- trol can be a major cause of backups that delay the material delivery trucks). If a break in the delivery of material occurs, the paver should be stopped using appropriate techniques such as the rapid stop/start method (the paver is stopped and started without an extended acceleration/ de-acceleration period). Paving should not resume until a sufficient num- ber of trucks are on the job site to ensure nearly continuous paving. Start- ing and stopping the paver for each truck as it arrives on the project will reduce the smoothness and quality of the finished pavement.

8.3.1 Types of Haul Trucks

Three types of haul vehicles are used to transport HMA from the plant to the paver: end-dump, bottom- or belly-dump, and live-bottom. Proper trucking procedures are necessary for maintaining a continuous flow of material to the paver hopper.

End-Dump Trucks

The end-dump truck (see Figure 8.2) delivers mix directly to the paver hopper and may have three to six axles. It can have a capacity of 12 to 20 tons (11 to 18 tonnes). More axles mean more capacity. The advan- tage of the end-dump truck is its short wheelbase, which makes it easy to maneuver. The end-dump truck can also be a semitrailer truck (see Figure 8.3), which is essentially a large dump truck, with a capacity of 20 to 25 tons (18 to 23 tonnes). The disadvantage with this type of truck is that improved operator skill is needed and its large dump bed can be the source of problems with overhead obstructions (wires, bridges, and trees). Because of this truck’s size, special care must be exercised when loading the truck because of the increased potential for aggregate segregation.

Bottom-dump trailers (see Figure 8.4) used in conjunction with a wind- row pickup machine require proper cleaning and maintenance. The bot- tom dump doors must be cleaned and sprayed with a proper release agent

Horizontal-discharge hauling units have a conveyor system that moves the material from the hauling unit into the paver hopper without the need to raise the truck bed (see Figure 8.5). This system has a safety advantage because the bed does not need to be raised, and segregation is minimized as the material is moved in mass on the conveyor system as it is dis- charged into the paver hopper or material transfer device (described later). Appropriate maintenance procedures must be followed for these systems to operate properly. Proper cleaning of the conveyor ensures that cold chunks are not left in the truck bed. Daily checks between loads ensure that the conveyor system is operational and that no loose or damaged components will break during the workday.

Avoiding Heat Loss

The temperature of the HMA must be maintained within specified limits during production, storage, transportation, and laydown. Loaded hauling units must deliver the HMA with minimal heat loss. If delivery time to the paving site is such that the material cools below acceptable placement and compaction temperatures—or if adverse conditions are such that the mate- rial will cool in the hauling unit—steps must be taken to retain the heat. Tarps and insulated truck beds are commonly used to reduce heat loss. The temperature of the HMA in the hauling unit should be monitored. If the temperature fluctuates beyond the limits allowed in the specifications, steps must be taken to reduce or eliminate the excessive temperature variations.

Condition of Haul Trucks

Numerous styles of haul trucks exist today, each with unique mainte- nance procedures. Some basic maintenance procedures, however, apply to all styles. The dumping lip should be cleaned with a scraper between loads, and tailgate-latching devices should be lubricated properly so they release on command. An approved release agent must be applied to the inside of the hauling unit before the next load is dumped into the haul- ing unit. The floor of the hauling unit should be flat and free of dimples that might cause the release agent to puddle. The truck should be in good condition and capable of safely and legally transporting material to the job site. The lifting device should be maintained so that the bed can be lifted to continuously move the material from the truck bed to the paver hopper.

8.3.2 Delivery of HMA to the Paver

When the loaded hauling unit has arrived at the paving site, the HMA must be moved into the paver without disturbing the HMA gradation or without losing temperature. The driver, after arriving in the staging area, should “break the load” by raising the truck bed slightly until the HMA moves against the tailgate. This procedure allows the HMA to exit the truck bed in a mass rather than in a dribble, avoiding segregation as the HMA flows into the hopper (see Figure 8.6).

The paver operator or dump operator, should position the loaded truck 12-18 inches (300-450 millimeters) short of the paver, with the dump bed partially raised. This positioning keeps the truck from knocking the paver backward and creating an indentation in the mat. As the paver moves forward and makes contact with the hauling unit, the driver should be instructed to release the tailgate and raise the dump bed as quickly as possible. When the tailgate opens, the material will move in mass into the hopper, thereby reducing the chances of segregating the material. When a wheel-locking device is not being used, the experienced truck driver will anticipate this sudden rush of material and briefly apply additional brake pressure to prevent the hauling unit from being forced out of the hopper and spilling material on the pavement directly ahead of the paver. While the dump bed is emptying, the driver should maintain just enough pres- sure on the brakes to keep the truck from rolling away from the paver. The flow of HMA is coordinated between the paver and truck driver. The bed should be raised to the fully extended position as quickly as possible without overflowing the paver hopper. Once the bed is empty, the paver signals for the truck bed to be lowered before pulling out of the paver hop- per. The hauling unit should be driven to a predetermined cleanout area, where remaining HMA can be removed from the truck bed and around the tailgate area and apron. Do not dump HMA in front of the paver or allow beds and aprons to be cleaned in front of the paver. See Figure 8.7 for the steps associated with properly loading the paver.

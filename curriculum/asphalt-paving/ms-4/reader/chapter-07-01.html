7.1. Types of Plant Facilities/ Production Processes

Two types of plant facilities/production processes for producing hot mix asphalt CHMA) paving mixtures are popular today:

° Batch facilities

° Drum-mix facilities

In each type of facility, aggregates are proportioned according to the requirements of the job-mix formula, dried and heated, and then mixed with the required asphalt binder to create the proper ratio of aggregates and liquid asphalt binder. With both types of facilities, supplemental fine aggregate or liquid additives can be added and reclaimed asphalt pave- ment (RAP) can be used to make recycled mixes. However, additive proce- dures are slightly different in each process.

7.1.1 Batch Facilities

The first hot mix asphalt plants were batch facilities. These were popu- lar from the early 1900s until the advent of drum-mixers in the 1970s. Many batch facilities still exist, producing mix daily, although over 80 percent of HMA tonnage in North America is now produced with drum- mix plants. Figure 7.1 shows the key equipment components in a batch facility, and Figure 7.2 shows a cutaway view of the flow process through the batch tower.

In the batch-plant process, individual aggregates are proportioned from the cold feed bins onto a single collector belt and sent to the dryer to be dried and heated. The dried and heated materials flow into a bucket eleva- tor to be carried to the top of the batch tower and deposited onto sizing screens. Multiple screen decks separate the aggregate into different sizes and direct the fractionated aggregate into hot bins. The screens in the tower determine the size of the aggregates in the hot bins and how the blended aggregate mixture from the dryer is fractionated.

Aggregates are then dried, heated, sized, and stored in the hot holding bins, for proportioning according to the mixture specified.

The mixture is made by weighing the fractionated aggregates stored in the hot bins with an accumulative aggregate weigh hopper, positioned below the hot bins. Each predetermined fraction of material is individu- ally weighed into the aggregate weigh hopper until the entire batch is weighed.

In a separate action, asphalt is weighed in a weighing device, typically called an asphalt weigh bucket or asphalt scale.

Plant automation is similar in both—and typically computer controlled— although blending automation is different because of the differences between the batch and the drum-mix processes. Most automated plant- control systems, whether batch oriented or drum-mixer, provide for multiple stored-mix formulas and automatically handle all the blending, metering, and mixing. All computer-controlled plants can provide cumu- lative material totals for a daily production report, which aids inventory control.

In both plant types, truck load-out and ticketing systems are also com- puterized and provide not only weight figures but also detailed project information on the load ticket.

These common equipment groups are discussed below, along with key optimum operating practices for controlling the process and producing consistent high-quality mix with these common components. 7.2.1 Liquid Asphalt Storage

Liquid asphalt binder is generally stored inside insulated and heated tanks (see Figure 7.5) adjacent to the other plant equipment. Multiple storage tanks are common at stationary facilities and are not uncommon with portable plants. This allows the producer to keep different grades of asphalt binder properly separated.

HMA producers are encouraged to consult with the liquid asphalt supplier for proper storage requirements.

Pipes that transfer the liquid asphalt to the mixing area of the plant are insulated and surrounded with an outer pipe or “jacket” containing hot oil from the plant’s heating system to keep the binder at the proper tempera- ture throughout the entire process.

Special processing equipment is available to blend mixture additives into the hot asphalt lines or plant mixing area. Antistrip additives, fibers, RAP, or other mixture modifiers are often added after the aggregate has entered the drum. Special processing equipment is also used for mixing crumb-rubber additives and latex into the liquid asphalt for special mixes. This equipment varies by application requirement and is usually computer controlled.

Plant personnel should monitor storage temperatures regularly to make sure the binder is kept at the proper temperature. Thermometers in the tanks and lines are connected to digital displays and graph recorders in the control room for this purpose. Binder temperature affects not only pump- ing and metering efficiency but also coating characteristics, so ensuring that binder temperature is maintained throughout the process is critical for proper mixing operations. Heater temperatures should be set at required storage temperatures, or not more than 20°-30°F (11°-13°C) above those temperatures. Excessively high temperatures cause the asphalt to build up and harden around the heating elements. These excessively high tempera- tures can also prematurely age the binder. The recording devices provide visual proof that specification temperature limits have not been exceeded. The objective of storing asphalt binders at an HMA plant is to maintain an adequate supply of material to keep the plant running between binder deliveries. The amount of storage needed at an HMA plant is a function of anticipated production rate, type of binder(s), and the anticipated delivery schedule.

7.22 Aggregate Stockpiling

Aggregates are delivered to plant sites by truck, rail, barge, or belt con- veyor directly from the aggregate source. Aggregates are usually stockpiled onsite and then fed into cold feed bins with a front-end loader. A plant can also be fed directly from the stockpile by feeders under the stockpiles or by trucks dumping directly into the cold feed bins. The most common approach is to feed the bins from a stockpile with a front-end loader.

Operational concerns for HMA production at the plant site focus on the following key areas.

Cross Contamination Materials should be kept separated from one another by space or by dividers before being loaded into the cold feed bins for mixing.

Subgrade Contamination

If materials are moved with a front-end loader, the operator must ensure that the bucket does not pick up subgrade materials along with the desired aggregate. Producers usually treat the ground to stabilize it or place a base aggregate-type material below the stockpile to minimize this problem. The latest trend at permanent plant sites is to actually pave the stockpile area with HMA to prevent subgrade contamination and to expedite moisture drainage in the stockpiles.

Wheel and Track Contamination

Driving on the aggregate stockpiles with haul trucks or stockpiling equipment can also cause contamination from materials that stick to the vehicle wheels or tracks, and should be discouraged. The importance of this guideline is relative to local conditions.

Breakdown/Gradation Change

Materials can change in gradation if haul trucks or stockpiling equipment are allowed to drive on and to work the aggregate stockpile. Degradation of aggregate materials is a function of material handling and aggregate characteristics. The gradation of the material can change every time it is handled. A thorough understanding of the aggregate materials and aggre- gate handling procedures must be accounted for in the design and con- struction process.

Segregation/Gradation Change

Materials that are not of uniform size are prone to segregation during handling operations. When aggregates are dropped from a significant height, such as from a stacking conveyor, or if aggregates are allowed to flow down the face of a stockpile, segregation can occur. Both practices should be discouraged. Conveyors that rise up and.down minimize this problem. Elevated stockpiles do not always have to be constructed using haul trucks and ramps. Available space for stockpiling, however, often dic- tates compromises in best practices. Materials that are segregated slightly can often be reblended satisfactorily if the loading equipment mixes coarse sides and fine sides from the stockpile face before charging the cold-feed bins. Segregation is probably the single biggest concern in stockpile man- agement at a hot mix plant. If segregation concerns exist, material samples can be taken directly from the discharge of the cold bins to verify whether the gradations of the individual materials have been compromised.

Minimizing Stockpile Moisture

Providing a solid draining surface under the stockpile area not only helps keep the subgrade material from contaminating the cold feed bins but also helps keep aggregates dry. Moisture drastically affects the produc- tion of the plant and fuel consumption. If moisture contents in the stock- piles change, the mix temperature will also fluctuate unless the operating characteristics (burner and draft settings) are constantly adjusted. This is very difficult with high-production HMA plants. For these reasons, hot mix

7.2.0 Virgin Aggregate and RAP Cold-Feed Bins

Feed bins for virgin aggregate (see Figure 7.6) and RAP (see Figure 7.7) are similar for both batch and drum-mix plants.

While vibratory pan feeders and plate-style feeders were used in the past, virtually all plants, both batch and drum/continuous, now use con- veyors with variable-speed motors located below feed bins to regulate the flow of materials to the drying drum.

Variable-speed belt feeds are consistent and repeatable, and therefore easy to calibrate. The ton-per-hour output can be determined at different belt speeds, and then the required speed setting can be calculated for a given recipe and given production rate. The feed-bin calibration process is the act of measuring and recording output at different speed ranges for a specific gate opening. This process is usually computerized on today’s modern plants. Gate openings should be proportional to the amount of ageregate being added to the plant to ensure uniform loading of the belt.

7.2.5 Aggregate Dryer

An aggregate dryer is needed for both batch and drum-mixer plants.

With some styles of drum-mixers, the dryer is very long because it is also used as a mixing drum to mix the liquid asphalt binder with the aggre- gate to produce the final mix. Some drum-mixers are two piece units—the dryer and the mixing device are separate units.

It is the responsibility of the dryer to produce mix with a consistent temperature. Wide variations in temperature in the final mix affect place- ment technique and in-place density. A temperature swing greater than 10°-20°F (6°-11°C) typically affects field performance. Aggregate moisture that varies widely in the stockpiles can make meeting this directive diffi- cult for the plant operator. This is one reason why managing the stockpile area for moisture control is so thoroughly discussed.

Aggregate dryers and drum-mixers come in both counterflow configu- rations (aggregate and hot gases traveling in opposing directions) and parallel-flow configurations (aggregate and hot gases traveling in the same direction). Batch plants have counterflow dryers. The newer drum-mixers discussed in this chapter also utilize counterflow dryers.

Dryers have a burner and fan fitted at one end to create a hot gas stream that dries and heats the aggregate. Burner and dust control equipment allow dryers with fossil-fuel burners to function very effectively from both a particulate- and gaseous-emission standpoint.

Dryers are inclined and rotate. They function by picking up and drop- ping aggregate material into the hot gas stream. Inside the dryer are steel flights, or lifters. These flights pick up the aggregate material and drop it through the hot gas stream as the dryer rotates (see Figure 7.9). To prevent incomplete combustion and hydrocarbon contamination, the aggregate material never passes through the burner flame. Special burner flighting is installed in the dryer for the length of the flame. These special flights pick up the aggregate, trap it against the outside wall of the dryer, and carry the material over top of the flame. Gravity causes the material to advance slightly with each rotation because the dryer is installed on a slight incline. Retention time typically is three to five minutes but can be longer, depending on the dryer’s size, length, and flight configura- tion. Flights can be configured for different material and local conditions. Small aggregate particles heat quickly and easily, although they are typi- cally wetter and require a higher burner position and exhaust fan damper position to remove the additional water from the rock. Coarse mixes with large quantities of stone have more mass for each particle and often require a more aggressive flight pattern for maximum heat transfer into the stone. The equipment manufacturer can provide advice for adjusting flight configurations.

Flights also wear as they dig through the aggregate material on the bot- tom of the dryer. As their leading edges wear down, drying efficiency is reduced. If moisture is retained in the mix, quality and placement will be compromised and the long-term serviceable life of the pavement may be affected.

Moisture retention in the mix can be monitored simply by weighing produced mix, drying it, and then weighing it again. Any weight loss can be attributed to moisture in the aggregate material. Reducing the feed rate will usually solve the problem.

Elevated exit gas temperature is another indication that the flights are either worn or that the configuration is wrong for local conditions. Flights can easily be replaced if they are worn. The pattern of the flight can also be changed for more drying efficiency.

Another indication that the dryer is functioning inefficiently for project conditions is a significant drop in material temperature after production. If the aggregate in a batch plant—or the mix in a drum-mixer plant—loses temperature significantly between the time it leaves the dryer and is loaded into the truck during normal production Gmore than 25°-30°F/14°-17°C), the aggregate is either not completely dry or not heated all the way through. Mixes with a high percentage of large aggregate often require elevated dryer exit temperatures to compensate for the longer time it takes for the colder center of the stone to heat-stabilize, but a temperature drop might also indicate that the stone is not completely dry. When significant tem- perature drops are encountered, moisture retention tests are needed. If no moisture is present, the uncoated stone can be overheated to compensate for the eventual temperature drop.

Drying effectiveness should be monitored during production and over the life of the equipment. The flights in the dryer are subject to wear because they constantly dig through and handle aggregate materials. These flights will wear and must be maintained. Their maintenance is part of the inspection/monitoring process for plant operating efficiency. Recording the surface temperature taken with an infrared temperature gun along the length of the dryer shell is an excellent way to monitor the flighting arrangement and drying efficiency. 7.2.6 Dust Collection Equipment

The gas velocity in an aggregate dryer is quite significant and will remove fine aggregate fractions from the dried aggregate mixture. Dust collectors are installed on hot mix plants primarily to collect the dust for environmental reasons, but captured dust is often reused, depending on the type of equipment installed on the plant.

There are two stages of dust collection on plant systems—primary collectors and secondary collectors. Primary collectors gather the large dust/fine aggregate particles. Secondary collectors gather the smaller particles.

Most primary collectors are either the knockout-box type or the cyclone type. Figures 7.10 and 7.11 show these types of collectors installed on plant systems. The fines collected in primary collectors are returned 100 percent to the plant process, without any contemplation of partial return. Most people consider this dust to be sand-size particles, but in reality they also have a fairly high percentage of material passing the 200-mesh or 75-micron sieve. The same style of primary collectors are found on both batch and drum-mix facilities.

Secondary dust collectors fall into two categories—wet washers (or wet scrubbers) and baghouses (or fabric filter collectors). This equipment is positioned immediately upstream of the exhaust fan and is connected to the fan with ductwork. Figures 7.11 and 7.12 show these types of systems installed on hot mix plants.

Wet washers clean the dust from the gas stream by injecting additional water into the system. This makes the dust particles heavier, allowing them to fall out of the gas stream. Collected dust is very wet—basically a wet sludge—and so cannot be returned for use in the mix process. This effluent is pumped to a settling pond where the dust settles to the bottom. The particle-free water is then recycled from the surface of the pond and reused for dust collection.

A baghouse cleans with a dry process, allowing the gas to pass through a fabric filter supported by a wire cage. Dust is collected on the outside of the fabric filter while the dryer is running, and cleaning air periodically passes through the bag from the opposite direction, causing the collected dust to fall into the hopper of the baghouse. This collected dust can either be returned to the mix-production process or removed from the system. Figure 7.13 shows the internal components of a baghouse.

When dust is returned to the plant process, certain operational practices are important in order to keep the dust return consistent.

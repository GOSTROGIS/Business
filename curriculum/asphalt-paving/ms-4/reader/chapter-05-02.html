5.2.3 Field Test Procedures

Dynamic Cone Penetrometer

Field Test Methods

The Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP) is a field-testing device to quickly estimate the in situ strength and deformation characteristics of pavement subgrades. The test can be performed on native soils or on com- pacted materials. The DCP Index that results from completing the test can be readily correlated to a CBR for use in the empirical CBR design method. Developed in South Africa in 1975, the DCP has since found its application in many parts of the world because of its portability, speed, and ease of use in flexible pavement design.

For field evaluation of subgrade stiffness, the Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) is commonly used by highway agencies and airports throughout the United States. The test is conducted by raising and dropping a weighted package on the pavement surface and measuring vertical deflections on the pavement surface. Using principles of mechanics, the stiffness of the HMA layers, aggregate base, and subgrade layers can be back-calculated at the testing location. Due to the speed and nondestructive nature of the test, numerous tests can be conducted on a pavement feature in a relatively short amount of time.

General

The DCP is a testing device used to estimate the in situ strength and deformation characteristics of native soils or fills placed as part of a pave- ment subgrade; it generally is not useful to evaluate the properties of bases and subbases. If the test is performed on existing pavements, the asphalt concrete surface layer must be removed, typically by coring an appropriate-size hole in the asphalt concrete. It relies on correlations with various soil types and the relative compaction or density of soils. Though commonly used to estimate the structural capacity of soils, this test is now being considered as a quality-control tool for pavement subgrades during construction, in lieu of more common in situ density testing.

The test involves driving a cone-shaped probe (see Figure 5.7) into a soil using a dynamic load and measuring the advancement of the probe for each applied load. The strength of the soil being penetrated has a direct impact on the penetration observed. Thus, the strength of the soil is related to the depth of penetration achieved with each blow used to drive the DCP. As previously discussed, the strength of the soil depends on den- sity, moisture content, and soil type or classification.

The data recorded during the testing process include the number of blows and the depth of penetration. The data is presented as a plot of the number of blows versus the depth of penetration. The penetration rate has been correlated to engineering properties of soils or other derived measurements of strength and modu- lus. Specifically, studies have correlated CBR to the penetration rate for a wide variety of cohesive and granular materials. The penetration rate can be correlated to resilient modulus through correlations between CBR and resil- ient modulus; however, such secondary correlations should be used with caution. Several studies have also correlated penetration rate, or index, to the elastic modulus of soils.

Falling Weight Deflectometer

General

The FWD, as shown on Figure 5.9, is an impulse load-deflection device that delivers a transient impulse force to the pavement. A weight is lifted to a given height on a guide system and then dropped onto a buffering plate on the pavement. A transient impulse force, which can be changed by varying the magnitude of the falling weight or by varying the drop height, is generated in the pavement by the impact of the falling weight. A series of sensors located at various distances from the load plate are used to measure pavement deflections. Commercial impulse load-deflection devices include the Dynatest, KUAB, JILS, and Phoenix.

A major advantage of the impulse loading devices is their ability to more accurately model a moving wheel load in both magnitude and duration, thus producing a deflection that simulates the pavement deflection caused by a moving vehicle. The advantages of the FWD are the following:

¢ The ability to better simulate a moving wheel load

© The ability to measure deflections at varying loads over time

® The ability to record a deflection basin

° The speed, repeatability, and robustness of the equipment

Analysis of Data

The measured surface deflections are used as input to a back calculation procedure to estimate the stiffness of the pavement layers and underlying subgrade soil. The pavement is viewed as a multilayered elastic system, and deflections are measured at radial offset from the center of the load plate to define the deflection basin. The typical approach to back calculation for HMA pavements involves an iterative process in which an elastic layer program is used to determine theoretical deflections. In the first iteration, assumed layer moduli (i.e., “seed” moduli) are used to determine a theo- retical deflection basin, which is then compared with the measured deflec- tion basin. The moduli are then adjusted and the deflections recalculated until the calculated deflections match the measured deflections.

5.5 Drainage of Asphalt

Pavement Structures

5.3.1 Introduction

Adequate drainage of the pavement structure is considered the most important element in ensuring long-term pavement performance. The proper design of the drainage system for a particular pavement is of para- mount importance. This section of the manual is a brief overview of the issues to be considered in designing pavement drainage. For additional details, consult MS-15, Drainage of Asphalt Pavements or the resources listed at the end of the chapter.

Reason for Drainage

Highway engineers recognize the critical need for good drainage in designing and constructing pavements. Probably no other feature is as important in determining the ability of a pavement to withstand the effects of weather and traffic, and in providing trouble-free service over long periods of time. Reducing the moisture content of the granular base and subgrade through the use of effective drainage directly affects fatigue cracking of the HMA surfaces, and thus the life of the pavement.

Water in a subgrade is usually slow to evaporate or drain. Even in dry weather, the subgrade soils may remain damp or wet indefinitely. Often, voids in porous materials become subterranean reservoirs unless they are drained.

With a few exceptions, most subgrade soils contain silt and clay, which weaken when wet. This weakening may be hastened by vibrations from traf- fic. If the subgrade is not properly shaped and sloped for drainage, water may collect at the interface of the granular base or subbase and the subgrade soils. As this water saturates the subgrade, the subgrade soils tend to lose sta- bility and thus the strength required to support the loads on the pavement. Under traffic, wet soil may be worked into a fine-grained slurry that pushes up into voids in the granular base or subbase course, thus destroying the grain-to-grain interlock and weakening the granular materials.

Design In developing the various features of a drainage system, consideration should be given to the following principal purposes: 1. To collect and drain water from the pavement surface 2. To collect subsurface water in the granular layers and keep the subgrade moisture content as low as possible 3. To prevent or retard embankment erosion 4. To intercept water from surrounding areas and carry it from the area 5. To lower the groundwater table

Drainage problems are caused by either surface flow or subsurface flow. Each requires separate analysis and treatment.

Surface drainage includes the disposal of all water present on the pave- ment surface and nearby ground. If possible, surface water should be prevented from entering the subdrainage system. Inevitably, some water enters the pavement structure, but may be kept to a minimum by surface maintenance procedures (e.g., crack sealing, etc.).

Subsurface drainage deals with water in the granular layers and in the subgrade soils beneath the pavement. Such water may enter the pavement through open joints, cracks, and other openings in the surface, through adjacent porous material, or from the ground below.

Subsurface water may be present in several forms, including these:

° Water that is free to flow under the force of gravity, referred to as “free

water”

° Water that moves by capillary forces, referred to as “capillary water”

Important differences exist between surface and subsurface drainage. Surface runoff is usually a direct result of rainfall or melted snow. Water flow to a subdrain depends entirely on the permeability of the soil and the pavement courses through which the water must pass. The maximum flow of surface water into a subdrain usually occurs within hours of a rainfall event. In contrast, the maximum flow into a subdrain from ground- water sources may occur several hours or even days after rainfall events. Design requirements in the first case are based on the amount of rain or melted snow and, in the second case, on the permeability of the soil or aggregate.

5.3.2 Surface Drainage

Design for Roadways

Protecting pavement from the action of surface water is most easily accomplished with full-width asphalt paving. This means that the traffic lanes and the shoulders are paved with HMA to provide an impervious cover that prevents surface water from directly entering the pavement struc- ture during rainfall. The pavement surface should be free from holes and cracks, have a permanently tight joint with the shoulder or gutter, and be shaped and sloped (crowned) for adequate surface runoff. Surface water usually is collected at the edge of a paved surface in ditches or gutters, which discharge the runoff by gravity flow to a location remote from the pavement. To prevent erosion on embankments, water should be directed to spillways by way of curbs or dikes constructed on the extreme edge of the shoulder. In addition, if either the volume or the velocity of the flowing water is expected to be excessive, these features can be constructed using asphalt lining, or other impermeable materials, to reduce the potential for erosion.

For urban sections, the paved surface, including shoulders, is crowned and typically drained by a curb and gutter system leading to catch basins, which carry the surface flow to a system of storm drains. If asphalt gut- ters are used, it is usually possible to tack the surfaces to ensure that no leakage occurs at the joint between the shoulder and the gutter/curb. Where concrete curb and gutter is used, it is critical to ensure that the joint between the paved surface and the concrete curb is maintained over the life of the pavement because the two materials respond to temperature in different manners and thus the joint tends to open.

In addition, the sidewalk or sodded area beside the curb must be graded to drain into the gutter to prevent water ponding in this location. Ponded water on the backside of the curb will seep into and saturate the sub- grade at the curbline and may cause increased localized stress to the HMA layers.

Highway pavements are also built with a crown sloped to facilitate the flow of water from their surfaces into ditches at each side. The most com- mon design practice allows the surface water to flow across the shoulder into a ditch or gutter at the side. On a pavement uniformly sloped to either the outside or the inside (such as a superelevated curve), the upper and lower shoulder crossfall is generally the same as that of the driving lanes to address safety concerns.

Paved shoulders provide the most effective surface drainage solution. From a drainage point of view, a less-effective practice (but still acceptable) is to provide gravel shoulders with increased crossfall to promote surface flow and minimize seepage into the subgrade. Sodded shoulders tend to prevent ero- sion, but they may also prevent water from draining freely from the road surface, thereby increasing the amount of moisture entering the ditch and saturating the subgrade soils at the edges of the pavement. Because sodded surfaces are typically weaker than granular surfaces, they may create a traffic hazard if they are used adjacent to higher-speed traffic lanes.

In some cases—where profile grade is sufficient—a raised lip or curb 4 inches (100 millimeters) or more in height may be constructed at the out- side shoulder edge to intercept water and guide it to some convenient outlet. In this design, the edge of the pavement is sometimes shaped into a shallow channel which leads into storm sewer openings or other paved channels. Curbs and dikes should be located so that they do not interfere with the removal of snow from the pavement surface.

Drainage ditches receive water from pavement surfaces and subsur- face drains. When the surrounding area slopes toward the roadway, these ditches also intercept water that would otherwise reach the roadbed. It is usually preferable to widen rather than deepen a drainage ditch to increase its capacity. Side slopes usually vary from about 4 (horizontal): 1 (vertical) on the roadway side to about 8:1 or flatter for the outside slope. The ditch should be large enough to accommodate the estimated surface runoff. Generally, the ditch should be constructed on a continuous smooth grade without depressions or pockets that will collect water.

Water collected in ditches may discharge directly into natural channels or into storm sewer openings. In other cases, it may be directed from the roadway by special ditches constructed for that purpose.

The ideal velocity of flow in a ditch is one that is fast enough to pre- vent settling of suspended material but not so fast as to cause erosion or scouring. Generally, a figure of 2 feet per second (0.6 meters per second) for newly seeded loam or fine sand, to 6 feet per second (1.8 meter per second) for well sodded or coarse gravel linings is an acceptable range of safe velocities. Higher velocities require measures such as rock dams, rock lining, or paving to prevent erosion. Hot mix asphalt is a desirable material for ditch paving.

5.3.3 Design for Large Paved Areas

Surface drainage design procedures for parking lots are the same as those for airports. A recommended guide is the Federal Aviation Adminis- tration’s Advisory Circular 150/5320-5B, Airport Drainage.

After the parking area plan has been established and an efficient parking pattern worked out, drainage grades that obviate patrons stepping from cars into gutters are designed.

To prevent puddles from forming in rainy weather, ideally a minimum of 2 percent, or 4 inch per foot (20 millimeters/meter), grade is necessary. On sloping terrain, constructing a grade presents no problem, but in flat areas, built-in drainage valleys give rise to a gently undulating flow on the paved area. Flows to proposed inlets should be calculated for existing runoff conditions, and if necessary, spacing should be adjusted to give satisfactory results. For average conditions, inlet spacing of 200-400 feet (60-120 meters) is satisfactory.

Because only a fixed contour can be laid in one pass of a paving machine, designs requiring a meandering gutter or valley should be avoided. Such gutters require hand placing, the quality of which is inferior to machine laying. A layout should provide for drains that follow straight lines. When the mix is being placed by the paving machine, the direction of the paver should parallel these lines. For the most efficient paver use, these lines should parallel the long dimensions of the lot. Watertight longitudinal joints are essential to prohibit the intrusion of surface water and to mini- mize the possibility of erosion under traffic.

In some instances, local bylaws restrict the flow of water into the storm sew- ers during rainfall events. In these situations, the pavement surface is some- times used for short-term storage (tens of minutes) of precipitation. In these conditions, the pavement surface should be adequately maintained to prevent saturation of the pavement structure during the temporary storage period.

In order to drain the granular materials and subgrade, the use of stub drains 15-20 feet (5-7 meters) in length installed in a cruciform pattern at each catch basin is recommended. The stub drains should be installed in trenches with an invert below the underside of the granular layers and

5.3.4 Subsurface Drainage

Introduction

Subsurface water results from rainfall or melting snow soaking into the soil and percolating downward until an impervious layer is reached. At this point, the water saturates the void spaces of the soil. The boundary between the saturated and unsaturated soil is referred to as the ground- water table. Subsurface water tends to be in motion and will percolate long distances through a porous layer if confined by impervious layers or strata.

A porous or cracked pavement may permit rainwater or melted snow to enter the granular base or subbase and saturate the various layers in the pavement structure. This water may then flow downgrade through the granular layers and create problems in other areas. These problems may be avoided most effectively with proper attention to pavement mix design and construction. A dense asphalt-treated base is not as subject to weaken- ing and may be particularly effective. However, water infiltration into the subgrade soils is still a concern. The installation of subgrade drainage is fundamental to long-term pavement performance. If the structural section contains open-graded layers, these should be drained by pipes or other positive means to prevent water ponding.

Subsurface seepage, under certain conditions, may develop a hydro- static head sufficient to lift the pavement completely off the base, causing cracking, and in extreme cases, complete disintegration of the pavement structure. The problem is more acute when steep grades in high rainfall areas are involved. With steep gradients, the water may travel longitudi- nally in an untreated base course along the direction of the gradient and cause excessive hydrostatic pressures at sag vertical curves or on the low side of superelevated horizontal curves.

Most urban pavements would benefit from the installation of subdrains along both curblines. The subdrains should be installed in trenches with an invert below the underside of the granular layers and should be con- nected to permanent year-round frost-free drainage such as the catch basin system. When full-length subdrains cannot be installed, the use of stub drains 15-20 feet (5-7 meters) in length on the upstream side of the catch basins is recommended.

In many cut sections, longitudinal subdrains are needed to prevent water from higher ground accumulating under the pavement. If not intercepted, subsurface water from a cut may flow onto an adjacent fill section and cause slumping of the fill slope and cracking of the pavement. The choice of filter material and the design of the drainage system must be given care- ful attention, considering both the type of material to be drained and the quantity of water to be expected.

Filter Material and Drain Rock

The purpose of pavement subdrainage is to enhance the long term (10 to 15 years) performance of the pavement. Thus, it is necessary to design the drainage system for long-term performance. Such a system requires the use of filters around drainage pipes to ensure performance. The filter allows water to flow to the drain and, at the same time, to prevent the loss of the fine particles from the soil. The design of a granular filter for any particular soil was defined by Terzaghi and Peck as follows:

D15, filter < 5 * D85, soil (for soil retention) D15, filter > 4 * D15, soil (for adequate permeability)

where D,, refers to the diameter of the soil or filter particle at the stated percent passing.

The material that meets these criteria for most subgrade soils is washed mortar sand, which is a clean, essentially one-size sand. Unfortunately, the permeability of mortar sand, while greater than that of most subgrade soils by several orders of magnitude, is still low for high-flow situations. Con- sequently, for adequate drainage, a perforated pipe is typically required. Such pipes are usually wrapped in a geotextile sock to prevent the sand particles from washing into the pipe.

Drain rock, on the other hand, essentially consists of one-size stone material (i.e., greater than % inch [4.75 millimeters]), which results in very high permeability. It is apparent, however, that drain rock will never meet the retention criteria for most subgrade soils. As a result, the fine particles from the adjacent subgrade soils will wash into the drain stone and clog the material. This loss of soil will result in settlement above and/or beside the subdrain.

The permeability of a granular filter material is reduced very rapidly as the percentage of fine material is increased. To prevent these issues, a filter fabric is sometimes placed between the drain stone and the subgrade soils. The choice of geotextile and the proper installation are critical to the suc- cess of this type of construction. An inappropriate geotextile will still allow fines from the subgrade to pass and result in clogging and settlement.

Subsurface Drainage Schemes

The following approaches to subsurface drainage are suggested.

As noted previously, the installation of continuous subdrains under each curb in pavements designed with an urban cross section will extend the life of the pavement. If the expense of a continuous system is prohibitive, a system of stub drains could be used. For large paved surfaces, this same stub drain system installed in a cruciform pattern at the catch basins is also recommended.

For rural cross sections (e.g., shoulders and side ditches), adequate depth of the ditches is critical. The invert of the trench must be below the base of the pavement structure and typically at least 3 feet (1 meter) below centerline grade to promote adequate drainage. Drainage Related to Frost Action

When the frost depth exceeds the thickness of the pavement structure, a subsurface drainage system may be required to lower the water table to pre- vent the formation of ice lenses and the damaging effects of frost heaves.

Frost heaves are a serious problem. It should be noted that all saturated or partially saturated soils will expand in volume (heave) when the water in the pores expands by 9 percent on freezing. However, because the water content of most subgrade soils is less than 15 percent, the overall expansion is less than 2 percent. This likely applies to most subgrade soils in North America. True frost heave, from a geotechnical perspective, only occurs in a particular type of soil where the D,, of the soil is in the range of 30-60 microns and the uniformity coefficient (D,,/D,,) is less than about 6. This is a medium-size uniform silt. This type of soil has sufficient permeability to attract water in the capillary zone to the freezing front and is also fine enough to have a high capillary rise. This results in ice lenses forming in the soil. The ice lenses grow in a downward direction (as water in a pond freezes, being fed by water from below), as seen on Figure 5.10. In order for ice lenses to grow in the soil, there must be freezing soil tem- peratures, a water table close to the frost line, a soil favorable to the rapid movement of capillary water, and a soil capable of retaining water. When freezing temperatures are prolonged and the frost line penetrates suscep- tible soil, the ice lenses that form cause a heave at the surface.

To avoid damage to drainage structures, determining the depth of frost penetration is necessary for design purposes. The most reliable informa- tion is obtained from local records of depth of frost penetration in the area and for the soils concerned. When such local information is not available, frost penetration can be estimated from charts obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration National Climatic Data Center, (www.ncdce.noaa.goy).

An alternate, but less effective, method of construction is to remove, mix, and recompact a suitable depth of subgrade to promote uniformity. This method will eliminate differential heave, although the whole road still will rise and fall. Even if every care is taken, a generalized form of heave can still occur. Frequently, ice accumulates between the granular base and the asphalt layer. This is probably caused by the migration of water vapor from the warm areas deep in the structure toward the colder surface lay- ers. The sudden change in permeability at the asphalt layer restricts the flow of vapor causing it to condense and freeze.

Another aspect of frost susceptibility is thaw weakening of subgrade soils. This behavior is exhibited by most subgrade soils when the amount of material finer than no. 200 sieve (75 microns) is greater than about 30 percent. Because the majority of subgrade soils are affected, thaw weaken- ing is generally the most serious aspect of frost susceptibility. The greatest loss of subgrade support is encountered when a wet autumn is followed by a winter with many freeze-thaw cycles and then by a rapid spring thaw. As frost advances into the soil, the available water in the voids freezes and the saturation of the soils reduces; as a result, water from below the freezing front is attracted to the freezing front and accumulates. In the spring, the frozen subgrade thaws from both above and below. The result is a period where the additional water drawn into the subgrade soils is liquid but trapped over an impermeable frozen zone. The abnormally elevated moisture content significantly weakens the soil. Soil strength will only increase after the frost is completely out of the subgrade and normal downward drainage occurs. The effects of spring thaw can be minimized by using adequate depths of granular soils or full-depth asphalt pavement and by maintaining proper drainage.

5.3.5 Geosynthetic Fabrics

The design of subsurface drainage systems should give consideration to geotextiles as alternative designs. Geosynthetic fabrics (geotextiles) are manufactured from a number of different materials, including polypropyl- ene, polyester, nylon, polyethylene, and polyvinyl chloride. The function of geotextiles, when used as filters, is to allow groundwater removal without the buildup of excessive seepage forces or water pressures. The geotextile must also prevent piping or subsurface erosion of the soil which leads to settlement. In these applications, water flows through the geotextile into a water-conducting medium, usually a trench filled with a selected drain- age aggregate and a slotted or perforated pipe, which quickly removes the water.

The use of geotextiles for subsurface drainage may be categorized in accordance with the following three general applications.

° Geotextiles are used as an envelope surrounding the single-size, clean coarse aggregate (i.e. less than 20 percent retained on a No. 4 sieve (4.75 millimeters)) in a trench. The geotextile is intended to prevent subgrade soil from moving into and eventually clogging the stone while still allowing water to pass through the geotextile. A more efficient sys- tem is to install drainpipes in the aggregate-filled trench rather than relying solely on the porous aggregate to carry the runoff. Typically, a 4- to 8-inch (100- to 200-millimeter) perforated flexible plastic pipe is installed within 5 feet (1.5 meters) of the surface.

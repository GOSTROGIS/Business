Like particle shape, the surface texture influences the workability, com- pactability, and strength of asphalt paving mixtures. A rough, sandpaper- like surface texture (as opposed to a smooth glass-like surface) tends to increase the strength of the paving mix and requires additional asphalt to overcome the loss of workability. Voids in the compacted mineral aggre- gate are also typically greater, a characteristic that provides additional space for the required increase of asphalt.

Asphalt paving mixes that carry heavy traffic loads and are highly resistant to rutting and deformation usually contain rough-textured, crushed, and angular aggregates, both coarse and fine. These mixes require greater care and effort to place and compact.

Many asphalt mixes in pavements for medium to low traffic contain both angular, rough-textured aggregate particles and smoother, rounded aggregate particles. The coarse aggregate fraction is usually crushed stone or crushed gravel, and the fine aggregate is usually all natural sand (rounded particles) or a combination with stone screenings. Such mixes rely principally on crushed aggregate for strength and rounded sand par- ticles for workability and ease of compaction.

Rounded particles, such as most natural gravels Cuncrushed) and sands from streambeds, have been used successfully in light duty asphalt pave- ments, such as parking lots and walking trails. However, because a higher density is possible with round aggregate particles, the amount of asphalt in the mix is usually a more critical factor in such dense-graded mixes. Toughness

An aggregate’s toughness is measured by its ability to resist wear and degradation. Aggregates are subject to additional crushing and abrasive wear during manufacture, placing, and compaction of asphalt paving mixes and so must exhibit a certain degree of toughness. Aggregates in HMA layers at or near the pavement surface are also subject to abrasion under traffic loads and require more toughness than aggregates in the lower layers where loads have dissipated or are not as concentrated. Rela- tively high resistance to wear, as indicated by a low percentage of abra- sion loss, is a desirable characteristic of aggregates to be used in asphalt pavement surface layers. Pore Structure, Porosity, and Absorption

The pore structure of an aggregate refers to the characteristics and con- figuration of voids in an aggregate particle. Pores or voids can be isolated or encapsulated within aggregate particles and they are thus imperme- able, or they can be interconnected and open to the surface, making them permeable.

Porosity is the total volume of voids in the aggregate particle. Higher porosity can relate to lesser aggregate strength and lower density and spe- cific gravity. Absorption is the intake or permeation of liquid into the pores of an aggregate particle. Higher porosity may not mean higher absorption if most of the pores are impermeable. However, high absorption does indicate a large volume of permeable voids.

An aggregate with permeable voids will absorb asphalt. Additional asphalt must be incorporated into the paving mix to compensate for the absorption of asphalt by the aggregate. This is noneffective asphalt. Such aggregates usually are not used unless less-absorptive aggregates are not available or the aggregates possess other qualities or properties that make them desirable in spite of their high-absorption rate. Blast furnace slag and many synthetic or manufactured aggregates are lightweight materials that are also highly absorptive. Their lightness and wear-resistant properties frequently outweigh the high-absorption rate consideration for pavement construction use.

AASHTO T 85 (ASTM C 127) is commonly used to determine absorption for coarse aggregates. For fine aggregates, AASHTO T 84 (ASTM C 128) is the common test standard. In these tests, absorption is expressed. as the amount of water as a percentage of the total dry aggregate mass that penetrates into the pores only and does not include water that adheres to the surface of the aggregate. The following section on specific gravity provides a description of the two test procedures.

Specific Gravity

The specific gravity of an aggregate is the ratio of the weight of a unit volume of material to the weight of the same volume of water at 73°F (23°C). This property is primarily used in mix volumetric calculations for voids determination. Also, bulk specific gravities (defined in the following paragraphs) are used in the computations for adjusting quantities of the aggregate components that are to be used in an HMA mix because of the differing specific gravities of various aggregates.

The three generally accepted types of specific gravities for aggregate, depending on the defined volume of the particle, are the following:

As illustrated in Figure 3.8, apparent specific gravity considers the volume as being the volume of the aggregate itself. It does not include the volume of any pores or capillaries that become filled with water after a 24-hour soaking. Bulk specific gravity considers the overall volume of the aggregate particle, including the pores that become filled with water after a 24-hour soaking. The effective specific gravity considers the overall volume of the aggregate exclusive of the volume of pores that absorbs asphalt. Whereas bulk and apparent specific gravities can relate to individual aggregates or combined aggregates, effective specific gravity relates exclusively to the total combined aggregate structure in a mix of HMA.

Air-voids in the compacted asphalt pavement course appear throughout the mix as small pockets of air between the asphalt-coated aggregate particles. The air voids content cannot be directly measured but is back-calculated from other test results. The choice of specific gravity for the aggregate used in asphalt mix calculations can have a substantial effect on the calculated amount of air-voids in the compacted mix. The actual specific gravity of the aggregate in the mixture depends on the degree to which the aggregate absorbs asphalt. When apparent specific gravity is used, asphalt is assumed to be absorbed by all the water-permeable pores. If bulk spe- cific gravity is used, it is assumed that the asphalt is not absorbed by the water permeable pores. Except in rare cases, neither is true. The concept of effective specific gravity more nearly approaches the correct value for deter- mining the voids in a compacted asphalt paving mix. Bulk specific gravity may be used, however, only if an allowance for the asphalt absorbed by the aggregate is taken into account.

Equipment and procedures for determining the bulk and apparent specific gravities of coarse aggregates are outlined in AASHTO T 85 (ASTM C 127). Equipment and procedures for determining the apparent and bulk specific gravities of fine aggregates are detailed in AASHTO T 84 (ASTM C 128).

Freeze-Thaw/Weathering Resistance

Freeze-thaw resistance is an aggregate’s ability to resist degradation caused by cycles of freezing and thawing when the aggregate is in a satu- rated condition. Internal expansive forces develop in the pores of the aggregate each time the absorbed water transforms into ice. Weathering resistance can include the freeze-thaw resistance factor, but it also refers to the aggregates’ ability to withstand the volume changes and degradation that occur with cyclic wetting and drying. An aggregate’s ability to resist the effects of wetting and drying cycles and freezing and thawing cycles can also be called the aggregate’s durability or soundness property.

In moist and freezing climates, aggregates must exhibit a certain level of durability or soundness to resist deterioration in stockpiles in the interim stage (after aggregate processing and before HMA production). However, once the aggregates are introduced into HMA production, the need for weathering durability is debatable. Moisture within the aggre- gates is driven out in the dryer, and the aggregates receive a coating of impermeable asphalt binder.

The test most commonly used to determine an aggregate’s durability is the sulfate soundness test—AASHTO T 104 (ASTM C 88). In this test, an aggregate sample is sieved into specified size fractions. The separated fractions are then immersed for 16 to 18 hours in a saturated solution of either sodium or magnesium sulfate for absorption of the solution into the aggregate pores. The samples are then removed from the solu- tion, allowed to drain for a short period, and then dried in an oven until they reach constant mass. The samples are then cooled to room tempera- ture, and the cycle of immersion and drying is repeated. Typically, five cycles are specified. At the end of the required number of cycles, each size fraction sample is washed to remove the salts, and then dried and sieved over the specified screen. The mass loss of each fraction sample is determined and expressed as a percentage loss. A weighted loss for the composite aggregate is calculated based on the original aggregate grada- tion. The applicability of this test to durability is given in ASTM C 88: “The internal expansive force, derived from the rehydration of the salt upon re-immersion, simulates the expansion of water on freezing.”

Typical standards and agency specifications set maximum allowable loss values in the 10-20 percent range for test durations of five cycles.

Aggregate cleanliness is one way of describing the level of surface coatings on an aggregate. Aggregate coatings can affect the bond or adhesion of binder to the aggregate. Aggregate cleanliness of coarse aggregate is often determined by visual inspection, but a washed sieve analysis generally provides positive proof. The sand-equivalent test, developed by the California Division of High- ways and described in AASHTO T 176 (ASTM D 2419), is the most common method of assessing the level of cleanliness of fine aggregates by determining the relative proportion of detrimental fine dust or claylike materials. The test method summary describes the procedure: “A measured volume of soil or fine aggregate and a small quantity of flocculating solution are poured into a graduated plastic cylinder and are agitated to loosen the claylike coatings from the sand particles in the test specimen. The specimen is then ‘irrigated’ using additional flocculating solution forcing the claylike material into suspension above the sand. After a prescribed sedimentation period, the height of floc- culated clay is read and the height of sand in the cylinder is determined. The sand equivalent is the ratio of the height of sand to the height of clay times 100.” Cleaner aggregates will have higher sand equivalent values. Common specification minimum limits for this property range from 40 to 50.

Determination of liquid limit and plasticity index properties is another method sometimes required by specifications for assessing the clay content in fine aggregate. Procedures for determining these properties are contained in AASHTO T 89 and T 90 (ASTM D 4318). These procedures define liquid limit as “the water content, in percent, of a soil at the arbitrarily defined boundary between the semi-liquid and plastic states.” Plasticity index is defined as “the range of water content over which a soil behaves plastically.”

Samples for both the liquid limit and the plastic limit tests use the portion of the material that passes the No. 40 (0.425 millimeter) sieve mixed with varying amounts of water. In the liquid limit procedure, a prepared sample is spread in a brass cup and cut with a grooving tool, forming a notch through the sample. The brass cup is then repetitively raised and dropped until the divided sample sections flow together. The liquid limit is the corresponding water content at which the sample flowed together at 25 drops of the cup.

In order to determine the plasticity index, the plastic limit of the material must be determined. According to the test method summary of D 4318, “the plastic limit is determined by alternately pressing together and rolling into a ¥%-inch (3.2 millimeters) diameter thread a small portion of plastic soil until its water content is reduced to a point at which the thread crumbles and can no longer be pressed together and re-rolled. The water content of the soil at this point is reported as the plastic limit. . .. The plasticity index is calculated as the difference between the liquid limit and the plastic limit.”

3.2 Composite Aggregate

Analysis

3.2.1 Introduction

HMA is normally composed of a blend of individual aggregates that make up the composite aggregate structure, or matrix, of the mix. These individual aggregates are stockpiled separately, are typically different in size and grada- tion, and might even come from different sources. Particularly during the processing stages, it is important that the individual aggregate components possess certain qualities. It is also important that the composite of the blended aggregates possess key characteristics critical to the HMA. These qualities and characteristics are reflected in most specifications that stipulate requirements for both the composite aggregates and the individual aggregate components. One of the key characteristics of the composite aggregate is gradation.

A uniformly graded aggregate structure consists of particles that are primarily of one size or their range of sizes is very narrow. The amount of particle-to-particle contact is low, and the interlock of the aggregates is low. The amount of voids between the particles is high; thus, permeability of the aggregate structure is high. Uniformly graded aggregates are not usually desired in asphalt mixtures.

Some asphalt mixes have an aggregate structure that is called open-graded. This is actually a uniformly graded aggregate structure with a very small amount of fine-aggregate particles. Open-graded mixes are most often used for their characteristics of high permeability and high surface friction. Surface courses made with these mixes are also called “porous friction courses.”

A well-graded aggregate structure has an extensive distribution of particle sizes ranging from fine to coarse. The smaller aggregate particles fill in gaps and voids between larger particles. The grading is considered dense in that the concentration of aggregate particles is high and the amount of voids between aggregate particles is small. Permeability of the aggregate structure is low, and interlock between aggregate particles is high. Paving mixtures with such aggregate structures may be easily compacted but tend to tightly pack and have air-void contents that are too low.

Gap-graded aggregates refer to the gradation characteristic in which there is a “gap” in the particle-size distribution. There is a range of particle size fractions (typically the mid-size range) that is either devoid of particles or the amount of particles in that range is low. Gap-graded aggregates are not as dense in particle concentration as well-graded aggregates. In turn, the amount of voids between aggregate particles and the permeability are higher than that of well-graded aggregates. Many times, asphalt mixtures must necessarily have the greater voids content of a gap-graded aggregates so that the mix can contain an adequate amount and coating thickness of asphalt binder. Gap-graded aggregate structures typically have good inter- lock between particles.

Two other dense-gradation types that are often mentioned are “coarse- graded” or “fine-graded.” Fine-graded aggregate structures usually contain a greater amount of fine aggregate, generally resulting in HMA mixtures with higher asphalt content, a lesser compactive effort needed during placement, a more closed-surface texture, and an increased potential for surface defor- mation. Coarse-graded aggregate structures, on the other hand, contain a lesser amount of fine aggregate. HMA mixtures with a coarse grading tend to have a lower asphalt content, require more compactive effort, have a more open-surface texture, and have a lower potential for surface deformation.

Those gradations that generally curve below the maximum-density line are consid- ered coarse gradings, and those that generally curve above the line are con- sidered fine gradings. See Figure 3.11 for examples of plotted gradations.

The combined percentages (P, in equation 1) for each sieve size should closely agree with the percentages of the desired composite aggregate grading. None of the percentages should fall outside the specified grad- ing limits. Obviously, there may be several acceptable combinations. An optimum combination is achieved when the blended or composite per- centages match as closely as possible to the original desired percentages. If the blended gradation falls outside the specifications, the responsible proportion in the blend should be modified, with the other proportions also modified to total 100 percent. Sometimes, it is necessary to delete a proposed aggregate and obtain replacement material if a satisfactory blend cannot be developed with the aggregate initially suggested.

Mathematical methods have been developed that will directly determine an “optimum” combination of aggregates. To accomplish this calculation, various optimization techniques have been used, such as the closest to the middle of the specification range. The speed of these methods varies with the number of stockpiles being blended. Many designers use computer spreadsheet programs to quickly evaluate a number of alternative blends. The Asphalt Institute’s SW-2, Mix Design Program computer program can also be used to visually evaluate the gradation plot of numerous blends very rapidly. Regardless of which method is used, a trial-and-error approach guided by reasoning, is usually the easiest and best procedure to determine and refine a satisfactory combination of aggregates. A computer-generated blend should not be used without further evaluation and checking.

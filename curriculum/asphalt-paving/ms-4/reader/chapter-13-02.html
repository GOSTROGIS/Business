Asphalt-rubber is defined by the (ASTM) D8 as: “a blend of asphalt cement, reclaimed tire rubber, and certain additives in which the rubber component is at least 15 percent by weight of the total blend and has reacted in the hot asphalt cement sufficiently to cause swelling of the rubber particles.” Information about the production and qualities of asphalt-rubber can be found in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt,” and from the Rubber Pavements Association (www.rubberpavements.org).

Asphalt-rubber is viscous and includes a dispersion of particulates that require application at higher rates and temperatures than other asphalt binders. Specially modified distributors that have larger and more pow- erful circulating pumps, larger heating systems, and larger spray nozzle openings are used.

Construction Process

The chip-seal construction procedures discussed in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance” also apply to the construction of a SAMI. The hot rubber modified asphalt is sprayed with a properly calibrated distributor. It is spray-applied on an existing pavement at the rate of 0.6-0.8 gallon/yard? (2.7-3.6 litre/meter’). The binders should be placed when the air tempera- ture is above 70°F (21°C) and rising and the pavement surface is dry.

The liquid membrane is then covered with %-inch (4.75 millimeter) or %4-inch (12.5 millimeters) one-size aggregate at the rate of 30-35 pounds/ yard’. As with any hot-applied chip seal, it is imperative to use clean, dry cover stone to promote adhesion between the binder and aggregates. The cover stone can be precoated with 0.5-1.5 percent by mass residual asphalt to improve adhesion by removing the surface dust and keeping moisture from being absorbed into the chips. Alternately, some agencies require that the chips be delivered hot to the project. Table 13.3 provides the aggregate gradation for the construction of a SAMI.

13.3 Mill and Inlay

Cold milling a pavement consists of removing the surface to a specified depth with specially designed equipment. Cold-milling equipment is avail- able in many sizes. The equipment will range from large high-capacity machines (see Figure 13.7) capable of milling up to a width of 16 feet. (4.9 meters) and a depth of 12 inches (300 millimeters) to smaller machines that can mill around manholes, valves, etc. (see Figure 13.8).

Cold milling is used to:

e Remove a deteriorated pavement to a desired depth, eliminating failed or faulty materials;

Restore the pavement surface profile;

e Restore or maintain the drainage flow and curb reveal along street

curbs;

° Provide a textured surface for skid resistance and improved bonding

of an asphalt overlay; and/or

¢ Remove material under overhead structures to maintain clearances for

bridge structures, traffic signals, and overhead utilities.

The byproduct of the milling operation, Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP), can be readily recycled into future asphalt pavements, saving both asphalt cement and aggregates. Processes for recycling RAP are described elsewhere.

Milling is conducted using a cutting drum, which is available in multiple widths. The cutting drum uses replaceable tungsten carbide teeth to cut the pavement surface (see Figure 13.9). These drums generally operate in the “upcutting” direction to facilitate breaking and removal of the existing material. The teeth are oriented in a helical or a spiral pattern to move the milled material to the center of the cutting drum, where it is forced onto a loadout conveyor for removal. The service life of the tungsten teeth depends on the nature and the depth of the material being milled. A small amount of water is used to control the amount of dust created by the milling process and to cool the cutting teeth to extend their life. The size of the reclaimed material can be adjusted by changing the rotational speed and/or reducing the forward speed of the milling machine.

Micromilling (also called fine milling) is a variation of the standard milling procedure. The micromilling process uses more cutting teeth to pro- duce a much finer textured surface (see Figure 13.10). A conventional milling machine has teeth spaced % inch (15 millimeters) - 3% inch (20 millimeters) apart. The teeth on a micromilling drum are typically spaced about % inch (8 millimeters) apart. This results in almost double the number of teeth for micromilling versus conventional milling. It is also possible to have the teeth spaced even closer at about 4 inch for micromilling, but this is not very common. Micromilling can provide a very fine, uniform texture that can serve as the final riding surface with or without an overlay. It can also be used to correct some minor pavement distress or to remove pave- ment markings because of changing traffic patterns. 13.3.1 Specifications

The end-result specification for cold milling is generally simple and

includes provisions that address the following details:

° A general requirement about the cold-milling machine. A typical specification requires the contractor to use a machine with automatic grade and slope controls, capable of cold milling an existing pave- ment surface to the depth or profile specified.

¢ The milling width and depth. Whether to specify a depth of cut or a nominal depth depends on whether or not the milling operation is considered to be an opportunity to improve the profile, or ride qual- ity, of the pavement. The smoothness, or ride quality, of the milled surface can only be obtained if a nominal depth of cut is specified

¢ The maximum particle size of the reclaimed material. This will generally be a maximum particle size of 14-2 inches (37.5-50 millimeters).

Hot In-place Recycling (HIPR) is an on-site, in-place method for rehabilitating pavements. The process is used to correct surface distress such as surface cracking, raveling, low friction values, and distortion con- fined to the wearing course such as corrugations and shoving. It cannot be used to correct structural failures. By eliminating the cost of transport- ing, processing, and stockpiling the RAP and new HMA, HIPR can be very economical compared with alternative treatments.

The process softens the existing HMA pavement structure with heat, removes or scarifies the surfacing material, mixes the material with a soft asphalt or a recycling agent and additional aggregate or HMA (if required), and places and compacts the new recycled asphalt surface on the roadway. One advantage of this process is that the thickness of any new overlay pavement can be reduced because the surface distress in the existing pavement is eliminated. Thus, current elevations and the overhead clearances are preserved.

The three categories of HIPR are surface recycling, repaving, and remix- ing. The first two processes involve heating, scarification, rejuvenation, and replacement of the top 1-1% inches (25-37.5 millimeters) of the exist- ing pavement. The repaving process combines surface recycling with the placement of a new HMA lift directly on the recycled but yet uncompacted material. The new material is not mixed with the recycled material but is compacted as a single layer. The recycled depth in the repaving process is still 1-1% inches (25-37.5 millimeters) and the new overlay is placed up to a depth of 2 inches (50 millimeters). The remixing process involves blend- ing virgin HMA directly with the scarified existing material in a pugmill and placing it to a depth of 2 inches (50 millimeters).

13.4.1 Pavement Assessment

HIPR is used to correct surface defects in an HMA pavement surface. It is not the appropriate treatment for a pavement that has base-course or subgrade failures. The thickness of the existing HMA layer should be at least 3 inches (75 millimeters) and should not contain a geotextile inter- layer within the recycling depth. The presence of surface treatments, rub- berized crack sealants, thermoplastic paint stripes, and specialty mixes (such as an open-graded friction course or an SMA mix containing an asphalt-rubber binder), may present problems and require approaches to surface rehabilitation other than HIPR.

13.4.2 Material Evaluation

The mix design of an HIPR project is developed using samples obtained from the existing roadway. The frequency of the sampling program will vary with the size and uniformity of the project. Samples should be obtained at a minimum of two sampling locations per lane-mile (or about one per lane-kilometer). The amount of material obtained from each sam- pling location will vary according to the amount of laboratory testing to be done. The sample for a mix design test should be larger than that needed when only thickness, gradation, and asphalt content are needed. Both core samples and chunk samples (obtained without water) should be taken. Large core samples are preferred (greater than 6 inches [150 millimeters] diameter). The samples should be tested to determine the in situ moisture content.

The core samples should be evaluated to determine the thicknesses of the various layers, the presence of paving geotextiles, and evidence of stripping or anything that may lead to delamination. After inspection, the cores should be trimmed to the depth of the recycled mix and the follow- ing properties should be evaluated:

° Bulk specific gravity

° Asphalt binder content

° Properties of the aggregate—gradation, coarse-aggregate angularity,

fine-aggregate angularity

° Recovered binder properties determined using the PG binder testing

procedures

¢ The maximum theoretical specific gravity of the mix, Gam

° The volumetric properties of the mix—Va, VMA, and VFA

Binder Evaluation and Blending Charts

The first step in developing a mix design for an HIPR mix is to deter- mine how to rejuvenate the asphalt binder in the existing pavement. As the existing pavement has been exposed to the elements, the asphalt binder in the surface mix has been exposed to air, water, and sunlight, resulting in the oxidation and hardening of the asphalt binder. One of the benefits of the HIPR process is the ability to rejuvenate this binder and return it to its original properties. This can be accomplished by adding a recycling agent, a soft asphalt, or a combination of the two.

The effect of adding a recycling agent or soft asphalt can be determined by measuring the physical properties of the recovered asphalt binder. The asphalt binder is tested to determine the penetration, viscosity, or G*/sin stiffness using a dynamic shear rheometer. This information is used with a blending chart. Mix Design

When the amount of asphalt, new aggregates, and new HMA has been determined from the blending processes previously described, they should be combined and compacted using appropriate mix design procedures for the traffic volume of roadway for which the mix is being designed. The volumetrics of the mix (air-voids, voids mineral aggregate, and voids filled with asphalt) should be determined and the properties of the combined asphalt binder should be checked to ensure that the needs of the project are being met. If these properties do not meet established criteria, the mix proportions should be adjusted.

A wearing course of a surface treatment or a thin layer of HMA may be added on top of the surface recycled layer.

The drying and heating of the existing pavement surface is accom- plished by one or more preheating units (see Figure 13.12). These units use indirect radiant or infrared heaters generally fueled with propane. The newest units use high velocity, super heated air that passes through small holes in a plenum passing near the pavement surface. This super-heated air is then captured and circulated through the heating unit to re-heat the air. Multiple units operate close to one another to maximize the penetra- tion of the heat into the pavement. The preheating units should increase the temperature of the pavement without hot spots or scorching the exist- ing pavement. The temperature of the scarified layer should be at least 230°F (110°C) and less than 300°F (150°C). The effectiveness of the pre- heating process depends on the temperature of the heat source, the ambi- ent conditions (air and pavement temperatures), moisture content, and the length of time the pavement surface is exposed to the heat. If the ambient conditions are such that the pavement is not being properly heated, the time the surface is exposed to the heat source must be increased. This can be done by slowing the rate at which the source moves across the surface or by adding heating units.

After the road surface has been preheated, a heating/scarification unit passes over the surface (see Figure 13.13 for a close-up of the scarifying tines). If necessary, a recycling agent is added to the scarified layer. The control system for adding the recycling agent is linked to the forward speed of the scarifying unit. (2 litre/ meter’). The scarified material is struck off with a heated, vibrating screed using manual grade control.

Compaction is generally accomplished by using a pneumatic roller as a breakdown roller, followed by a static steel-wheeled or double-drum steel vibratory roller for finish rolling. Static rolling is preferred where very thin lifts are being compacted to avoid damaging the mixture.

The compacted recycled surface can be left as the wearing course on a low-volume roadway, or it can function as a pretreatment for an HMA overlay or surface treatment. Lower costs and higher production generally result if the placement of the wearing course and the scarifying operation are separate and independent operations that do not delay each other.

Remixing

Remixing consists of heating the roadway to a depth of 1%2-2 inches (7.5-50 millimeters); scarification and collection of the softened material into a windrow; mixing the material with virgin aggregates, a recycling agent, and/or new HMA, if required, in a pugmill; and laying the recycled mix in a single homogeneous HMA layer. This method is used when the repaving procedure is not sufficient to make the necessary corrections to an existing roadway. It can be used to make changes in aggregate gradation, improve skid resistance, and adjust the mix to improve rutting resistance.

Remixing can be either a single-stage or a multistage process (see Figure 13.16). The treatment depth for the single-stage process is typically 1-2 inches (25-50 millimeters). The treatment depth of the multistage method is 1%4-3 inches (37.5—75 millimeters).

The heating of the existing pavement is done in much the same way as the surface recycling procedure. Scarifiers and/or hot milling drums (Figure 13.18) loosen the softened material, which is then augered to the center of the unit. These units have a moldboard for collecting the RAP. Depth control is accomplished by automatic sensors operating off a short reference ski or matching shoe.

In the single-stage method, the loose material is directed into a mixing chamber consisting of a pugmill or a mixing drum.

The multistage process (Figure 13.16) consists of one or more pre-heater unit(s), two heater-milling units, a pugmill mixer, and a standard paving machine and compaction equipment. The pre-heater provides initial heat- ing of the surface and removes the surface moisture. Then, a heater-mill- ing unit heats and mills the existing pavement. The loosened material is windrowed in the center of the lane (see Figure 13.18). The typical depth for this first pass is 1-1% inches (25-37.5 millimeters).

The second unit follows directly behind the first unit and continues the heating and removal process. The distance between these two units must be kept to a minimum to ensure the least amount of heat loss. The second unit picks up the milled material from the first unit as it mills 1-inch below the windrow. This unit lifts and removes the loose material. If required, new HMA is dropped into a hopper on the second unit. From the hopper, it is dispensed evenly over the windrow from the first unit. This material is carried over the heating chamber by a slat conveyor that has a variable-speed control that matches the speed of the second unit. It is dropped into the windrow created by the final milling head. This milling head mills the second 1-inch layer of the softened material into a windrow.

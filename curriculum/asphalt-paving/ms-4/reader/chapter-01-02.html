This procedure is called stage construction. It avoids extensive investment in the beginning; when a new asphalt pavement layer is added, the wearing surface is equal to or better than the original.

When road-building funds are limited, a strong all-weather asphalt base can be placed and opened to traffic for a year or more. When additional construction funds are available, the asphalt surface course can be added. Similarly, in new housing developments, asphalt base can be placed on all new streets to serve until the development is finished and heavy construc- tion traffic ceases. The asphalt surface is then added after first repairing any damage or settlement suffered by the base while supporting the con- struction traffic. The streets are then at full strength and in an attractive condition appropriate to a new living area.

Many examples of planned stage construction are evident in the inter- state highway system. Asphalt pavements have been built with sufficient thickness to serve for the first few years, with funds programmed to add strengthening thickness when required by increases in traffic.

The handbook is divided into the following five sections:

° Chapters 2, 3, and 4 cover the materials used to build HMA pavements and how to design those materials.

* Chapters 5 and 6 cover the design of an HMA pavement, starting with the subgrade or foundation soils and then the actual thickness design of the HMA layer.

° Chapters 7, 8, 9, and 10 cover the construction of the asphalt layer, starting with production and moving through to quality control of the HMA.

* Chapters 11, 12, 13, and 14 present information on the management of the HMA pavement, including maintenance and rehabilitation.

* Chapter 15 covers special uses of asphalt and HMA.

Chapter 2—Petroleum Asphalt

This chapter discusses the manufacture of the asphalt binder to obtain desired properties. It also discusses the historical tests that have been used, and are still used in many parts of the world, to describe the proper- ties of asphalt cement. It describes in detail the Performance Graded (PG) system for specifying asphalt binders and the testing associated with that system. It also describes the manufacture and testing of emulsified and cutback asphalts. A brief discussion of air blown asphalts is provided. Finally, the steps that must be taken to ensure that those working around asphalt cements do it in a safe manner are also provided. ,

Chapter 3—Mineral Aggregates and Mixture Additives

This chapter discusses aggregate processing and aggregate properties. Included in this discussion are sources of aggregates and the properties of those aggregates as they affect the quality and performance of an HMA mixture. The tests used to evaluate the quality and properties of vari- ous aggregate sources are also discussed. The tools needed to analyze a composite aggregate blend are presented, and example problems are pro- vided. The properties and handling of recycled asphalt pavement (RAP) materials are discussed, along with the proper procedures for sampling and testing RAP. Recent developments in the construction of HMA pave- ments have introduced the use of mixture additives. The use of these materials is discussed.

Chapter 4—Hot Mix Asphalt Mix Design

This chapter presents the basic concepts of HMA mix design and the steps to calculate the volumetric properties of an HMA mixture. It covers the Superpave mix design procedure in detail and introduces the back- ground of the Marshall and Hveem mix design procedures. This chapter also covers the use of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) in an HMA mix

Chapter 5—Soils and Drainage for Asphalt Pavements

This chapter presents a background on the characteristics of soils as they relate to the design and construction of HMA pavements. The test proce- dures used to evaluate the strength of soils are reviewed, and the develop- ment of a proper drainage system for an HMA pavement is covered.

Chapter 6—Asphalt Pavement Structural Design

This chapter presents an overview of the various Asphalt Institute pavement thickness design methods and how those methods relate to other thickness design methods used for the design of HMA pavements. This description includes a discussion about the required inputs, such as environmental (temperature, rainfall), traffic (type, mix of traffic, and traffic volume), and subgrade strengths. It also discusses how to handle very heavy wheel loads in the development of a pavement design for a specialized use such as container yards or airfields.

Chapter 7—Manufacture of Hot Mix Asphalt

This chapter presents a discussion of the HMA manufacturing process. It covers the components of an HMA manufacturing facility, how aggre- gate and RAP are fed in a controlled manner into the HMA plant, how it is dried, and how the dust generated by the production process is controlled. It details the operation of both a batch plant and a drum-mix plant, and it provides a detailed discussion of troubleshooting problems that might develop during the production of the HMA.

Chapter 8—Hot Mix Asphalt Paving

This chapter presents the process of actually constructing an HMA pavement including: subgrade and base course preparation for a new pavement; placement of the HMA layers; and pavement surface preparation for an HMA overlay over an existing pavement. The operation of the paving machine is covered in detail, along with steps that can be taken to prevent problems during the placement of HMA mixtures.

Chapter 9—Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction

This chapter covers the concepts behind the proper compaction of an HMA pavement. The principles of compaction that are discussed include the following: the effect of HMA properties; environmental effects; layer thickness; subgrade and subbase as it affects compaction of the HMA; and the time available for compaction. A discussion of the different types of compaction equipment is provided, along with how that equipment should be used to properly compact an HMA pavement.

Chapter 10—Quality Control and Acceptance of Hot Mix Asphalt

Chapter 11—Pavement Management

This chapter discusses how the pavement management process works to ensure an adequate pavement performance over time. The components of a pavement management system and how that system can be used by an agency as a tool in the development of a pavement preservation sys- tem are presented. The steps for conducting a pavement evaluation of an existing pavement and the procedures to accomplish a pavement distress survey are covered.

Chapter 12—Maintenance of Asphalt Pavements

This chapter presents a detailed discussion of the materials and proce- dures that can be used to properly maintain an HMA pavement. It includes a discussion about what is needed to maintain a pavement and why vari- ous procedures are used. The chapter covers patching, cracking sealing, and asphalt surface treatments (fog seals, slurry seals, micro-surface, and chip seals).

Chapter 13—Rehabilitation Methods for Asphalt Pavements

This chapter discusses the proper methods for rehabilitating an HMA pavement. It contains a detailed discussion of the steps that should be taken before placing an overlay including patching, interlayers milling and inlay procedures, hot-in-place recycling, cold-mix recycling, and full-depth reclamation.

This chapter presents the steps that can be taken to rehabilitate a failed portland cement concrete pavement. A review of the process of reflection cracking is provided. The use of saw-cut and seal, crack/break and seat, and rubbilization are discussed in detail.

Chapter 15—Special Uses of Asphalt and HMA

This chapter is a brief discussion about the ways asphalt is used for other than highways. For example, the HMA is used for hydraulic structures, recreational facilities, leaching pads, and racetracks.

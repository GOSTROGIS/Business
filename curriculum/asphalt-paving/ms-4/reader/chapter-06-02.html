Relationship to Other Design Methods

The Asphalt Institute recognizes that other flexible-pavement design methods are available to engineers. These other design procedures fall into two basic categories: empirical and mechanistic-empirical. A separate discussion on Perpetual Pavement design is included in this section.

6.2.1 Empirical Methods

Empirical methods are based entirely on the experience of evaluating well-performing and poorly-performing pavements along with specifically constructed test sites such as the AASHO test road. The commonly used CBR method is an empirical method developed in the 1920s by members of the California Department of Highways from experience with its pave- ments. It was validated with field testing and improved for use on airfields during World War I and is still in wide use today.

The 1993 AASHTO Guide is an empirical method derived from the AASHO Road Test of the 1950s. While the method is widely used and highly respected, the AASHO Road Test pavements were constructed of relatively uniform mixes, built on a single subgrade soil, and exposed to a single environment—Illinois. These and other facts anchor the validity of the data to the specific mixes, soil types, and climatic conditions experi- enced in Illinois in the 1950s and open the door to criticism of its validity in other conditions.

Empirical methods are limited by the depth and breadth of the data set. For example, it is well established that HMA stiffness varies with pavement temperature, yet the CBR method makes no allowance for HMA stiffness variation due to monthly or seasonal climate variation. Likewise, the AASHTO method has similar difficulty in transferring the experience of one climate to that of another. For these reasons, empirical methods, while convenient, may not make the best predictions for pave- ment thickness in other locations or for other conditions.

computer-based M-E analytical model, DAMA, which was used to create the Asphalt Institute design curves published in MS-1 and other manuals. These efforts established the Asphalt Institute as a pioneer in M-E pave- ment design methods at a time when the concept was relatively new.

In the past few decades M-E design methods have become widely accepted. It can be said that M-E-based flexible-pavement design is on the path to becoming the standard rather than the exception. Several state high- way departments have developed their own mechanistic-empirical design systems including WINFLEX, MnROAD, and EverPAVE, the official design methods of the Idaho, Minnesota, and Washington State Departments of Transportation, respectively. Air-carrier airport-pavement designers are able to tap the power of M-E methods in LEDFAA, a computer program devel- oped by the FAA. PCASE is the M-E design method applicable to military airfields operated and maintained by the U.S. Department of Defense.

At the time of this writing, a new national M-E Pavement Design Guide (MEPDG) has been developed under sponsorship of the National Cooper- ative Highway Research Program and is in the process of being introduced to the state highway departments by the Federal Highway Administration. It is widely expected that the MEPDG will be adopted by AASHTO as its pavement design guide at some time in the future.

As discussed in the next section, the Perpetual Pavement design con- cept, based entirely on M-E principles, is a pavement design strategy that is gaining support across the country.

The Asphalt Institute M-E design method is now part of an increas- ingly populated field of powerful pavement design tools available to civil engineers worldwide. The reader is referred to Tables 6.1 and 6.2 for a comparison of some of the features of a select group of M-E methods for highway and airport applications.

6.2.3 The Perpetual Pavement Method

The basic concept of a Perpetual Pavement is that through careful mix type and material selection together with high-quality construction, a pavement structure can be built that is resistant to permanent deformation and fatigue cracking. Surface-initiated distress, such as top-down cracking and weathering/raveling, may still occur, but through a regular process of milling and overlaying, the pavement structure never deteriorates. Like the roof on the typical residential structure, the surface layer will need to be replaced periodically, but the underlying pavement structure remains solid and strong.

The Perpetual Pavement design concept consists of the following layers:

1. An unyielding subgrade forms the pavement foundation.

2. The base layer consists of a flexible and fatigue-resistant HMA mix—possibly a “rich” mix with a slightly higher binder content.

3. Intermediate layers consist of stiff, rut-resistant mixes such as Superpave or stone matrix asphalt (SMA).

4, The wearing course is a high-quality Superpave, open-graded friction course, or SMA intended to be milled and replaced at regular intervals.

The thrust of Perpetual Pavement design is to combine a rut-resistant, impermeable, and wear-resistant top structural layer with a rut-resistant and durable intermediate layer and a fatigue-resistant and durable base layer. Though a Perpetual Pavement is focused at high-volume traffic roadways, the justification may be made for medium- and low-volume roads as well.

Essential to the Perpetual Pavement concept is the assumption that the subgrade soils form a strong foundation. Improvements to drainage, soil stabilization, or over excavation and replacement may be required to cre- ate the proper foundation for a successful Perpetual Pavement.

The HMA layers in a Perpetual Pavement may include a “rich” bottom layer with a slightly higher-than-typical binder content to create additional fatigue resistance. The intermediate layers are intended to be stiff rut-resistant mixes, such as the Superpave mixes that are currently used in the United States. The mixes used for the wearing course can vary depending on the specific needs of the area. Superpave mixes provide additional rut-resistance and a smooth riding surface. SMA mixes might be selected for exceptional durability on high-volume or heavily loaded streets and highways. An open-graded friction course (OGFC) may be chosen to reduce splash and spray and/or reduce pavement noise while providing a smooth-wearing surface. In a Perpetual Pavement, the wearing course is intended to be milled and overlaid at regular intervals to remove surface distresses and maintain smoothness.

At the core of the Perpetual Pavement design method is the concept of an “endurance limit.” This concept holds that there is a level of strain below which fatigue damage does not occur for any number of load repetitions. For the condition of fatigue at the bottom of the lowest HMA layer, an endurance limit of 70 microstrain has been established. Long-term research studies will validate this limit. In the life of a Perpetual Pavement, horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the lowest asphalt-bound layer does not contribute to fatigue failure if it does not exceed the 70 microstrain threshold.

Similarly, a threshold of 200 microstrain is currently understood to be the limit for vertical compressive strain in subgrade materials. It is held that a strain level below 200 microstrain does not contribute to subgrade rutting in Perpetual Pavement design.

Perpetual Pavement design is conducted using M-E design principles for layered elastic materials using the PerRoad Perpetual Pavement Software. The cumulative-damage factor concept is applied to both fatigue and rut- ting conditions, but rather than allow pavement damage to reach 1, dam- age in a Perpetual Pavement design is never allowed to exceed 0.1.

6.3.1 SW-1 Asphalt Pavement Thickness Design Software

SW-1 is the Asphalt Institute’s Windows-based asphalt pavement thickness design software that provides users a single computer-based design solution for highways, airports, heavy wheel applications, and other applications such as parking lots. It includes the Asphalt Institute pave- ment design curves for highways, airports, heavy wheel loads, and other applications, which have been digitized for use in SW-1. Also included is the DAMA analysis tool for advanced structural analysis. Tying it all together is a graphical user interface to ease handling of user inputs, pavement design reports, and management of data files.

One of the benefits of SW-1 is a versatile and powerful user interface, as shown in Figure 6.6. From this interface, users collect input data, report output, and manage data files for asphalt pavement design for all types of applications. SW-1 uses a tabular format to clarify the type of input required and to aide the user in stepping through a pavement design.

A design wizard is automatically invoked on the initial setup of a new problem. The wizard steps the user through the process of inputting all the required data—climate, traffic, subgrade, materials. The final step through the wizard leads to a design solution and the report page.

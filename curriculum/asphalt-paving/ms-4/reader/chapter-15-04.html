15.7.3 Industrial Floors

General

Asphalt floors are particularly adaptable for use in many factories and warehouses. Above ground level, they are ordinarily laid on PCC decks. At or below ground level, they may be designed and constructed similarly to roadways or parking lots. Asphalt floors are waterproof, highly resistant to wear, and dustless. The use of a highly siliceous mineral aggregate in the mix may make it resistant to certain dilutions of acid.

Asphalt Mastic Floors

Originally, an asphalt mastic was prepared using crushed-rock asphalt. The term now generally applies to a mixture of graded sand, mineral filler, and asphalt cement quite similar in character to sheet-asphalt paving. Fac- tory and warehouse floors are frequently subjected to very heavy unit loads, carried on small-diameter narrow metal wheels. When confronted by this condition, the regular sheet-asphalt mixture is modified by using a harder asphalt binder (such as a PG 70-22 or PG 76-22) and a higher proportion of very fine mineral filler. A maximum thickness of less than 1 inch (25 millimeters) is typically recommended.

Asphalt Floors

Dense-graded asphalt such as that used for paving highways and air- ports is particularly well adapted for paving warehouse and factory floors. Asphalt itself is unaffected by acids, but the mineral aggregate should be acid-resistant. Care should taken to design and compact the mixture to produce high density and waterproofing.

General

The roofs of many modern structures serve as parking areas. Asphalt paving is particularly adaptable to roof parking lots for reasons similar to those for bridge-deck paving.

The importance of proper drainage cannot be overemphasized. To ensure rapid runoff of water to drains, the surface should be pitched or graded at a 2 percent slope or % inch per foot (20 millimeters per meter).

Asphalt pavement transfers large moving and static loads to the roofing membrane. Any lateral movement in the membrane causes the pavement to move. Any differential heaving under an asphalt pavement under load causes distress or failure to the underlying membrane. Also, any excess or free asphalt in the membrane is absorbed into the wearing surface and causes it to soften and possibly bleed.

If an asphalt pavement is placed over a deck area constructed of prestressed concrete beams, reflection cracks are likely to come through the pavement at the junction between successive beams. To minimize reflection cracking, some type of stress-absorbing membrane can be placed over each joint. Such a membrane also helps provide a tighter seal against the passage of water.

The two types of roof-deck paving are (a) slab and pavement with a built-in slip plane and (b) slab and pavement bonded together.

Free-Movement Plane Construction

Free-movement plane construction provides a distinct, free-movement plane between the structural roof slab and the pavement.

A positive bond breaker, such as a lime or fine sand, is spread over the con- crete deck to allow freedom of movement between the concrete deck and the pavement. A conventional built-up roof consisting of four or five plies of light- weight roofing felt is placed on the roof. However, the final flood coat and aggregate cover is not applied. In its place, a very light tack coat of diluted SS-1h or CSS-1h asphalt emulsion is applied to the top felt layer. On top of this, an HMA layer is placed and compacted. A minimum thickness of 2 inches (50 millimeters) is recommended, and a very densely graded mix having a maximum aggregate size of about % inch (9.5 millimeters) should be used.

Bonding Roof Deck and Pavement

To bond pavement to a roof deck after cracks and joints have been sealed, a conventional four- to five-ply built-up roof is applied. Each layer, except the surface of the top layer, is spot mopped with asphalt. No mop- ping is done within 15 inches (375 millimeters) of a PCC slab joint. The top layer is then covered with 2~3 inches (50-75 millimeters) of a dense- graded HMA. Another method is to use an asphalt membrane that contains mineral filler and fibers for body and strength. The membrane is carefully spread to ensure a uniform thickness. Often, a protective board %—4 inch (3-6 millimeters) thick is placed over the membrane. The membrane or board is overlaid with one or two lifts of HMA having an asphalt content higher than normal. The total thickness of the mix should not exceed 3 inches (75 millimeters). The aim is to obtain an impervious mixture that weathers well and has high stability or strength.

Small rollers or sidewalk rollers are often used because of the restricted working area and the maximum allowable loads for the building. Small hand-operated electric or gasoline-powered vibratory compactors are used to work around areas inaccessible to rollers.

15.8 Leaching Pads

Because asphalt is an inert material, it can be used to construct all sorts of structures for handling very corrosive materials.

15.8.1 Uses of Leaching Pads

Leaching pads are used to extract metals from low-grade ores, such as

copper, gold, etc. Acid solutions are sprayed over stockpiles of the ore, and

‘the solutions leach out the metals. The metals are then recovered from the leachates.

15.8.2 Design of Leach Pads

Leach pads are designed using hot mix asphalt with a high binder con- tent of a soft grade to assure a flexible pavement capable of withstanding some subgrade settlement.

To ensure that the pad pavement is impermeable, leach pads are sealed with a high softening point (polymer-modified) asphalt.

Also included in the design are dikes, channels, and retention ponds for gathering and holding the leachate.

15.8.3 Materials

Mix Binder—The binder selection should be one or two grades softer than what would usually be used for asphalt pavements in the climatic area where the pads are to be constructed. Example: If a PG 70-10 is the normal paving grade used, then select either a PG 64-10 or a PG 58-10.

Mix aggregate—Use a %-inch (19 millimeters) nominal-size aggregate, with a dust-to-binder ratio of at least 1, and design for 0 air voids.

Membrane seal—Use a 170/200 pen or high softening point material that is applied at the rate of 0.35 gallon per square yard (1.10 liters per square meter).

15.8.4 Construction of Leaching Pads

The following steps should be followed in constructing a leaching pad. 1. Prepare the subgrade and compact to a uniform density. 2. Place 4 inches (100 millimeters) of the hot mix asphalt and compact to 96 percent of maximum specific gravity.

15.9 Environmental

Applications of Asphalt

15.9.1 General

For decades, many civil engineering solutions to environmentally sensi- tive projects have incorporated asphalt. It has been used because it is flex- ible, strong, chemically stable and nonreactive, impermeable, recyclable, economical, and readily available. All sorts of asphalt materials are used in environmental applications: sprayed-on membranes of emulsified asphalt, asphalt cement, and modified asphalt, dense-graded HMA, open-graded HMA, hydraulic mixes, sand mixes, and mastic asphalt liners.

Contrary to the fears of some environmental groups, scientists have established that asphalt, in the form of an emulsion or incorporated into a mixture of aggregates, does not leach into water, contaminate soil, or kill fish. The results of leachability tests of new HMA show very low levels of leachable compounds, well below any current Environmental Protection Agency guidelines. Scientists have proved that asphalt is an environmen- tally safe material.

15.9.2 Examples of Environmental Uses of Asphalt

1. Fish hatcheries. Oregon and Washington have more than 35 asphalt-lined fish hatchery ponds. These ponds are used to raise fingerling fish until they are large enough to be released. A typical hatchery pond is 5-7 feet (1.5-2.1 meters) deep and covers about a half-acre (0.2 hectares). The pond liner is constructed of 2 or 3 inches (50-75 millimeters) of HMA over 8 inches (200 milli- meters) of aggregate base course. Most liners are sealed with an asphalt emulsion so that bacteria cannot inhabit the tiny surface irregularities in the HMA liner.

2. Water reservoir liners. Throughout the world, many asphalt- lined water reservoirs have been constructed over the past 100 years. The design of a reservoir liner must address permeability, durability, flexibility, resistance to weathering, the ability to absorb stresses during filling and drawdown cycles, long-term loading stresses caused by settlement in the supporting subgrade, and the weight of maintenance vehicles when the reservoir is periodically cleaned. 15.10 Uses of HMA for Race

and Test Tracks

15.10.1 Introduction

Whether a racetrack or a test track, a track almost certainly will be paved with HMA—the only material that can consistently provide the smooth surfaces necessary for vehicles traveling at such high speeds (see Figure 15.16). HMA pavements can be constructed to smoothness stan- dards unmatched by any other surfacing type.

Each track has its own unique characteristics as to length, shape, and bank- ing angles. Racetracks are usually oval in shape but can be quad- or tri-ovals and have banking angles that range from 12 to 36 degrees. Test tracks, on the other hand, usually are oval; however, some older tracks are circular in shape. Slopes on test tracks can range from the normal 2-percent cross-slope typi- cal of a normal pavement, to as much as a 70-degree cross-slope. The greater slopes are needed to provide neutral steer though the turns while vehicles are tested at high speeds.

Test facilities not only have high speed tracks but generally incorporate road courses, stability testing pads, and/or segments of pavements that duplicate existing pavements. These test facilities are a way to compare how vehicles react to different pavement conditions.

Pavements for test tracks and racetracks are constructed in all sorts of climate conditions, and these conditions must be considered during pave- ment design and construction. These tracks provide very wide, paved sur- faces, which make them susceptible to thermal cracking. The pavement is subjected not to heavy loads but to the scrubbing action of the side thrust of the tire as the vehicles go through the turns of the track. Therefore, track pavements need to be stable and durable.

Drainage is another important factor. The pavement design should include a drainage layer within the pavement structure. Special attention must be given to the areas behind the crash wall and under the stands. Any water from these areas must be intercepted.

15.10.2 Materials Requirements

Binders

The selected binder should be at least two PG grades higher than that commonly used for the area where the track will be constructed. The binder should have a minimum softening point of 180°F (82°C).

The selected binder should be two PG grades higher on the high- temperature side and can be one grade less to minimize thermal cracking; the low-temperature grade should be one or two grades lower than that usually selected, compared with a normal highway binder grade in that climate. For instance, instead of using a PG 64-22, a PG 76-28 or PG 82-28 should be considered.

Binders for the underlying layer of the pavement structure can be the same unmodified binder as would be selected by the local DOT for that region of the state where the track is located. ,

Aggregates

The aggregates used in track surfacing must be a hard, durable material that provides good surface friction. Granite and slag make good aggre- gates; however, other sources of hard, non-polishing, durable aggregates can be used. Very hard, angular aggregates should be avoided because they tend to shred rubber from tires, resulting in slower races with more frequent pitstops for tire changes.

The same aggregates used for highway construction can be used for the underlying layers of the pavement structure.

Mix Design

Mix designers should consider high stability and high density as their main objectives. Stone matrix asphalt mixes with their high binder content and thicker mortar should be considered when the local paving industry has experience using this type of mix regularly. The Indianapolis Speedway used an SMA mix.

45.10.3 Construction Practices

Race and test track construction is very complex construction work. This is especially true for the paving contractor. The contractor not only deals with closer tolerances, a smoother surface, and higher densities, but also must accomplish those tasks while working on steep slopes. Construction work certainly tests the ingenuity of the contractor’s personnel.

Anyone contemplating being involved in race or test track design or construction should consult with a person who has previously built a track.

For slopes 18 degrees or less, paving procedures are similar to normal highway paving. On slopes between 18 and 24 degrees, the rollers need lateral support, which can usually be accomplished from the lower side (Figure 15.17). Care must be taken to ensure that the lower portion of the roller does not dig into the mix. Material transfer vehicles are needed to deliver the mix to the paving machine. Adjustments must be made to the paving machine, to ensure proper delivery of the mix to the screed. Once the slope is steeper than 20 degrees, all the equipment from the top or bottom of the track must be anchored. Crawler tractors, backhoes, or cranes make good deadmen that can move along the top of the track at the paving speed. Heavily weighted crawler-mounted machines con- nected by I-beams of different lengths can be used to anchor the paver from the bottom of the slope. Large crab-steering, shoulder-widening machines can be used in the same configuration to hold up compaction rollers.

Test tracks pose some added problems. To maintain neutral steer, they require parabolic cross sections. Normal screeds on paving machines can- not be configured to the required shape. This limitation necessitates the use of a screed with several sections. These special screeds usually have a break at 2-foot (0.6-meter) intervals. Compaction of the pavement on these parabolic sections also poses a problem. Either a roller with split drums or a series of smaller, narrow rollers are required.

Consideration should be given to diamond grinding the underlying layer before placing the surface course, eliminating the need to solve roughness problems with just the final surface.

Joints are very important and must be properly constructed to ensure good smoothness and proper density. The number of transverse construc- tion joints should be minimized, and where production allows, a single paving lane should extend one full lap around the track. All joints should be tack-coated.

15.11 Porous Pavements

15.11.11 Introduction

A porous asphalt pavement can be used to reduce the runoff from parking lots and paved roads. A porous asphalt pavement consists of a permeable asphalt surface placed over a granular working platform on top of a reservoir of large single size stone. The asphalt surface is an open- graded friction course designed using the principles in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.” The lower reservoir of large single-size stone has sufficient voids to create adequate storage capacity. A properly designed porous asphalt pavement can provide a solution to storm-water runoff problems as well as groundwater table recharge. The National Asphalt Pavement Association’s Design, Construction, and Maintenance Guide for Porous Asphalt Pavements, Information Series 131, contains detailed information on the design and construction of porous asphalt pavements. The following summarizes that information.

15.11.2 General Design Principles

A porous asphalt pavement consists of a porous asphalt course (usually an OGFC), a top filter course (of %-inch [12.5 millimeters] aggregate), a reservoir course of aggregate (114-3 inches; 37.5-75 millimeters thick), a filter fabric and the existing soil or subgrade material. Figure 15.18 shows a typical porous pavement. The site conditions, such as soil characteristics, local topography, climate, and traffic are the key items in determining the applicability of a porous pavement for a specific site. In addition, local storm-water regulations must be followed. The porous pavement should be designed for a 25-year/24-hour storm event. Other environmental factors must be considered. Frost penetration must be evaluated in determining the reservoir course thickness. Porous pavements are not recommended for barren areas subject to moderate to high winds because of the pres- ence of blowing dust in those areas. Dust, sand, and salt used for snow and ice removal should be kept off a porous pavement because they can clog the voids in the porous pavement surface.

Soil and Subgrade Conditions

The subgrade on which the porous system is placed is a major fac- tor in the success of a porous pavement. A thorough soil investigation must be conducted, including determining infiltration measurements per- formed at the approximate bottom of the porous pavement structure. These measurements can include simple percolation tests. These tests should be

15.11.3 Construction

Constructing the porous pavement near the end of any other construc- tion work prevents other construction debris from clogging the porous surface. The site area should be protected from excessive heavy equip- ment operating on the surface, which compacts the soil and reduces per- meability. The following is a summary of the recommended construction sequence:

1. Excavate the subgrade using either track equipment or vehicles with over-sized tires to reduce the amount of compaction of the subgrade.

2. Place a filter fabric on the uncompacted subgrade.

3. Place 1% to 3 inches (37.5 to 75 millimeters) of rock on the filter fabric in lifts to the design thickness, keeping construction equip- ment off the fabric. It should be lightly compacted with plate compactors or light rollers.

4, Place a thin 1- to 2-inch (25 to 50 millimeters) layer of %-inch (12.5 millimeters) crushed aggregate over the rock layer.

5. Place a 2- to 4inch (50 to 100 millimeters) layer of an OGFC. The OGFC should be designed as discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.” A polymer-modified asphalt should be considered.

15.11.4 Maintenance

The porous pavement should be inspected periodically to check for surface ponding, which indicates possible clogging. The porous surface can be vacuumed, flushed or jet washed to help maintain the pavement’s porosity.

15.12 Colored Asphalt

Although not widely done, asphalt pavements can be colored something other than the usual shades of black and grey. The first coloring method is to use a natural-color aggregate and the wearing (surface) course. As the asphalt binder wears way from the surface with traffic, the aggregate is exposed, revealing its natural color. A second way to color asphalt is to use an additive in the asphalt binder such as an iron oxide (rust) to give it a reddish tint. Green, yellow, and orange are also possible with different ores. Other colors require different metal compounds. A spe- cial synthetic binder (resin) that contains no asphaltenes has been used because it colors more readily. By tinting the asphalt binder, the hot mix asphalt pavement is colored full depth, so there are no surface wear-off concerns. A third way to color asphalt is to coat the surface with a material that penetrates the voids and bonds well to an asphalt pavement, such as an epoxy-fortified acrylic emulsion. Many colors are available with these types of surface coatings. Care should be taken that these surface coatings do not negatively affect surface friction if the pavement is used for vehicu- lar traffic. One possible disadvantage of these systems is that they may wear off with time and need to be renewed. The second and third options require the use of specialty products, and more information about them can be obtained by contacting individual manufacturers.

Warm Mix Asphalt Pavements

New technologies have been developed that allow asphalt mixtures to be produced and placed at significantly lower temperatures. These technologies are called warm mix asphalt (WMA). With these technologies, temperatures can be reduced by as much as 30 percent but still allow the asphalt binder to adequately coat the aggregate during mixing at the plant and achieve the desired workability at the paving site. These methods reduce the viscosity and increase the workability of a given asphalt binder at a given temperature.

15.13.1 Potential Benefits

The following benefits in reducing mix production and paving tempera- tures through WMA are most often mentioned:

° Decreasing the energy and fuel consumed to make HMA

° Reducing possible emissions and odors from plants

¢ Improving the working conditions at the plant and paving site

° Allows for cold-temperature paving

Reducing emissions is especially critical in and around nonattainment areas such as large metropolitan areas and cities that have tight air quality restrictions.

Other potential benefits of WMA are less recognized. Some contractors and agencies are exploring the use of WMA in order to extend the paving season in cool weather. Depending on location and climate, paving proj- ects are typically prohibited after a certain calendar date because colder temperatures lessen the time available for the rolling operation to compact the mix to the desired in-place density. Through WMA technology, mixes can remain workable at cooler temperatures, increasing the window of opportunity for compaction. This may make WMA a feasible option for those end-of-season projects whose completions before winter without compromising quality are so important.

Early indications are that WMA technology can provide all these benefits without sacrificing the long-term performance of the asphalt pavement. However, before its widespread adoption, WMA technology must prove that is provides the same long-term performance as regular HMA.

Organic (Wax-Like) Additives

Special organic wax-like additives are mixed with the binder to reduce viscosity at temperatures above about 212°F (100°C) and then increase the viscosity at temperatures below about 212°F (100°C). The shift from viscosity reduction to viscosity increase in the binder occurs near the con- gealing point of the wax.

Mineral Additives

A number of minerals that have water bound internally have been used as additives in order to introduce water into an HMA mixture. The water is liberated gradually over time, resulting in an almost continuous foam- ing action. This foaming action of the liquid binder acts as an extender and lubricant that enables the mix to be workable at lower temperatures. Production temperatures can be reduced to the 266°-293°F (130°-145°C) range. The materials are added in a manner that produces up to 0.6 percent water by weight of total mix.

Low-Energy Asphalt

Another WMA process, developed in France, uses wet fine aggregate. This process is called Low-Energy Asphalt (LEA). A key to the energy say- ings in this process is that it takes five times more energy to turn water into steam than it takes to heat the aggregate from 32°F to 212°F (0°-100°C). Fine aggregates tend to have the highest moisture contents. In the LEA process, the coarse-aggregate fraction is dried and heated to a tempera- ture of approximately 300°F (149°C). A specially formulated surfactant is then added to the binder. The coarse aggregates are then coated with the binder, which results in a thick film coating because the fine aggregate has not yet been added. The wet fine aggregate is then mixed with the asphalt-coated coarse aggregate, resulting in a foaming action that aids in the coating of the fine aggregate.

Foam Processes

Foamed asphalt is created by injecting cold water, typically 1-5 percent, into the hot asphalt binder using special equipment. The water rapidly evaporates, producing a large volume of foam, which slowly collapses. Foamed asphalt differs from foaming mineral additives in that the water and asphalt binder are directly mixed together in the presence of com- pressed air. The expanded foamed binder is then injected directly into the mixing chamber containing the aggregate. In a variation of the foamed process, the aggregates are heated and coated with a very soft binder. A very hard asphalt binder is then foamed into the mixing chamber. The combined soft and hard binder produces the intended binder grade.

Stationary Mixing Facilities

Mixes using emulsified and cutback asphalt prepared at a stationary mix- ing facility offer certain advantages over road-mixing methods. Weather conditions are not as much a factor in a stationary mixing facility. Mixing that would otherwise require hours of windrow manipulation is accom- plished in a few minutes.

The stationary mixing facility provides good control over both the pro- portions of materials in the mix and the mixing time. Uniformity of mixes is practically assured. Processing in a central plant allows a more viscous asphalt to be used. Often, enough aeration has been accomplished in the mixing operation—dumping and spreading mix in the roadway—to permit immediate compaction with rolling equipment.

Often, these advantages make facility-mixing methods more economical for producing and placing mixes in the roadway than road-mixing methods.

Cold mix facility setups may vary depending on the quality and the type of mix being produced. At the very least, the setup should consist of a mixer, an asphalt storage tank, an asphalt metering pump, piping and spray bar equipment, a spray bar for feeding water and additives, con- trols for adjusting and monitoring the various components, a conveyor for feeding the aggregate, and a power source. It may also include one or more aggregate bins, proportioning aggregate feeders, a scalping screen, an aggregate load-sensing device, and a surge bin or storage silo. Batch- type pugmills can be used. However, this mix production is ideally suited for continuous mixers, which are used almost exclusively.

The production of high quality cold mixes for heavy duty pavements requires a well controlled facility setup to ensure success. In addition to carefully monitored and controlled blending of aggregate, asphalt, and in some cases water, the mixer should be a type that permits variation in mixing time. Surge hoppers or storage silos are highly desir- able because they minimize facility shutdowns and improve mix unifor- mity. Good stockpiling and loader techniques can also be used in lieu of surge bins or silos. A typical cold mix facility is shown in Figure 15.5. Aeration/Spreading/Compaction

Aeration of open-graded aggregate mixtures is usually not critical. However, as the presence of fine aggregate increases and the mix becomes dense-graded, ample aeration is necessary. Proper. aeration for cutback asphalt is usually reached when the volatiles have been reduced to about 50 percent of that contained in the original asphalt and the moisture content does not exceed 2 percent by weight of the total mixture. For emulsified asphalt mixtures, the moisture content should be reduced to not more than 2-5 percent by weight of the total mix. In the case of sands and sandy soils used as a base course and surfaced within 30 days, volatiles in the mix should be reduced by at least two-thirds.

The point at which adequate aeration is achieved is often based on experience and judgment. Experience with the particular mixture being prepared and trial rolling have practical value for determining the pres- ence of moisture or volatiles when test data are not immediately avail- able. (AASHTO T 110) Standard Method of Test for Moisture or Volatile Distillates in Bituminous Mixtures can also be used. This test uses a metal still (similar to the one used for the distillation of emulsified asphalt), condenser, and a glass trap. A light solvent is added to a sample of the uncompacted mix in the still and then heated.

Therefore, lift thickness must be limited by the rate at which the mixture loses its fluids. The type of emulsified asphalt, the water content of the mix, the gradation and temperature of the aggregate, wind velocity, ambient temperature, and humidity all affect evaporation.

Although each job has its own combination of these factors, experience has shown that under the best conditions, a dense-graded mix should be placed in compacted thicknesses no greater than 3 inches (75 millimeters). Open-graded mix may be placed in layers up to 6 inches (150 millime- ters). When multiple lifts are required, some curing time must be allowed between successive lifts. Curing time varies with the rate of evaporation. However, a second lift can usually be placed after two to five days under good curing conditions.

A delay in initial rolling may be necessary, especially with dense-graded mixes. Rolling seals the pavement as it reduces the voids in the mix. Pre- mature rolling retards evaporation of the water in the mix and greatly extends the time required for the mix to reach required density. The mix must be allowed to develop sufficient cohesion to support the rollers. If rolling is delayed too long, however, compaction will be difficult. In some cases, the developing asphalt-aggregate bond will be irreversibly broken, therefore, timing is important. Rollers may be selected as indi- cated in Table 15.6.

Because the tires of a motor grader compact the freshly spread mix, the tracks will appear as ruts in the finished mat unless there is adequate rolling between each successive lift. Therefore, the roller should follow directly behind the motor grader in order to eliminate wheel tracks.

If the asphalt mixture exhibits undue rutting or shoving at any time during compaction, rolling should be stopped. Compaction should not be attempted until there is a reduction in moisture content, either naturally or by mechanical aeration.

After one lift is thoroughly compacted and cured, another lift may be placed on it. The operation should be repeated as necessary to bring the road to required grade and cross section. For a smooth riding surface, the motor grader should be used to trim and level as the rollers complete compaction of the lift.

After the mat is shaped to the final cross section, it is rolled with a steel- wheel roller until the roller marks are eliminated.

15.2 Hydraulic Structures

Asphalt has been used in hydraulic applications for several thousand years. Archaeological excavations show that asphalt was used as water- proofing material for temple baths, aqueducts, and water tanks as long as 5,000 years ago.

The use of asphalt for the control, conservation, and storage of water has expanded in past decades.

Areas of growing importance include the following:

e Water conservation, to meet the increasing demand for water supplies

° Irrigation, to open up new lands for cultivation

¢ Flood control, to protect lives and investments in personal property

and industry

° Beach erosion, to preserve beaches and protect waterfront property

15.2.1 Application/Uses for Hydraulic Structures

The following list is a compilation of some applications and uses of asphalt in hydraulic structures.

° Breakwater—a structure protecting a shore area, harbor, anchorage, or basin from waves

° Canal—a watercourse cut through a land area and used for either irrigation or navigation

° Catch basin, pond, or reservoir—a large, shallow holding area designed to collect rainwater. It usually discharges the collected water into a storage tank (see Figure 15.7)

¢ Dam—an artificial barrier of earth, rock, masonry, or other material across a channel to impound water or to obstruct its flow

¢ Dike—a wall or mound built around an area to impound water or to prevent flooding. If used to protect from flooding, it is usually called a levee

° Embankmeni—an artificial bank, mound, or dike built for a specific purpose, such as to hold backwater, or to carry a roadway

° Groin—a shore-protective structure (such as a pier or structure of stone, rubble) extending out from and perpendicular to the shoreline to trap lateral drift sand and retard shore erosion, or to protect a har- bor or to influence water currents or tides

° Levee—a dike or embankment to protect a land area from flooding

Asphalt Materials and their Functions

Many types of asphalt materials are used in hydraulic applications. Each type can be classified in one of the following distinct categories:

° Impermeable asphalt mixes

° Porous asphalt mixes

° Asphalt mastics

° Asphalt cement

These materials can be used in various forms to perform one or more of the following functions:

° To waterproof a structure

° To protect a structure

° To reinforce a structure

Table 15.7 shows how each type of asphalt material may be used to perform these functions.

Standard roadway construction equipment is usually modified for this type of paving. In some instances, special paving equipment has been devised. Linings of impermeable asphalt mixes consisting of well-graded aggregates provide an erosion-resistant, durable surface. Even when the waterproofing of an embankment is not required, impermeable asphalt revetments or fac- ings resist scouring, weed growth, and other damage.

Porous Asphalt Mixes

There are two types of porous mixes: permeable and open-graded. Per- meable asphalt mixes are used to line reservoirs that are made watertight by compacted layers of earth or clay. The asphalt linings protect the earth embankment from erosion and at the same time permit the free flow of water through the lining, thereby eliminating the danger of hydrostatic back pressures developing under the lining. Open-graded mixes are used to provide drainage layers beneath watertight linings. Drainage layers are designed to dispose of seepage or leakage that may occur in the water- tight lining. They are also used to drain away groundwater seepage that would otherwise build up hydrostatic pressures underneath the watertight lining. Open-graded mixes, in addition to providing a subsurface drainage layer, add to the stability and overall strength of the lining.

Asphalt Seals

An asphalt membrane lining consists of a continuous layer of asphalt cement sprayed over the surface being waterproofed. It is usually covered with a layer of soil, gravel, or other material to hold the lining in place, to prevent weathering (oxidation) of the asphalt, and to protect it from mechanical damage. 15.2.2 Hydraulic Mix Design Requirements

There are certain basic requirements for HMA mixes to be used for waterproofing hydraulic structures. They must be workable while placing, and impermeable, flexible, and stable in service. To ensure impermeability (ess than 1 X 107 centimeters per second), the void content of an asphalt concrete lining should not exceed 4 percent. A compaction level of 96 percent of the maximum specific gravity is required.

The lining will usually be placed on a sloped surface. Therefore, the mix- ture should be quite workable to allow efficient placement by both mechani- cal and hand methods. Usually, the desired compaction must be achieved using lighter compaction equipment. While operating on the slope, cables are used to winch the rollers up and down.

The lining should be flexible and able to withstand, without cracking, long-term loading stresses caused by settlement in the supporting sub- grade or foundation. On the other hand, it must also be resilient under short-term loading, such as wave impact.

Increased asphalt content helps satisfy the foregoing design require- ments. However, after a certain point, increased asphalt content results in decreased stability. Although this mix does not need the high stability usually associated with highway paving mixes, it should be stable enough to support its own weight on relatively steep slopes while hot. In addition, it should not creep after the lining has been compacted.

Permeable Surface Mix Composition

Permeable asphalt mixes for hydraulic structures are characterized by the absence or reduced amount of fine aggregate or sand in the mix. As a conse- quence, the asphalt content is also reduced. The mixes have interconnected voids that permit passage of water. The void content of these mixes must be a minimum of 18 percent to assure easy passage of water through the mix.

A harder, or more viscous, grade of asphalt cement is desirable in these mixes to allow sufficient film thickness and to prevent drainage from the aggregate during construction. This choice of asphalt also provides addi- tional cohesion in the mix between the aggregate particles.

The selected grade should be two PG grades higher on the high-temperature side compared with a normal highway binder grade in that climate. For instance, instead of using a PG 64-22, a PG 76-22 should be considered.

Sub-liner Drainage Layers

Porous drainage layers, with higher void content than a permeable lin- ing, serve as drainage layers under an impermeable lining while at the same time contributing to the structural strength of the lining. In either case, the purpose is to provide free drainage to prevent hydrostatic pres- sures from building up in the embankment or within the lining itself. As a surface lining, water is allowed to flow to and from the embankment through the lining. As a drainage layer, the subsurface water should be collected and carried off by drains.

To be effective, porous drainage layers must have 20-22 percent void contents to assure proper drainage of water through the layer.

Asphalt binder selection and content is accomplished with the same procedures used for permeable mixes. The suggested mix composition for the porous layers is listed in column E of Table 15.8.

15.2.3 Construction Considerations

impermeable Surface/Lining

Hydraulic linings of asphalt concrete should be prepared and placed in accordance with established construction practices. However, some spe- cial aspects of construction are mentioned here.

Asphalt concrete linings are placed on side slopes, on curved sections, in angles and corners, and on level surfaces. Conventional highway spread- ers, modified pavers or spreaders, spreader boxes, special slip-form pav- ers, hand methods, or some combination of these methods may do this. In most instances, conventional highway spreaders are used. They are often modified as necessary for slope operations. When side-slope paving and floor paving are separate operations, experience has shown that best results are obtained when the slopes of the reservoir or channel are paved before paving the floor.

Conventional highway spreaders, (sometimes winched up the slope), usu- ally perform spreading on side slopes; often they are modified to facilitate the work. In some cases, especially with large shallow reservoirs, asphalt mixes are spread with specially designed finishers operating on rails within a steel-frame structure up and down the slope. Also, side slopes of shallow reservoirs have been paved with pavers moving along the slope longitudi- nally. During the operation, the paver is held with cables attached to equip- ment operating on the crest. This method presents compaction problems, however, because rollers operating this way apply nonuniform rolling pressures and the drum tends to cut into the mix on the downslope side.

Outrig- gers with wheels attached to the side of the rollers have also been used successfully. The outriggers can be hydraulically adjusted to keep the surface of the steel drum parallel and in uniform contact with the mat being laid.

When a spreader operates up and down the slope, a sloped ramp at the crest may be used to permit the paver to pave all the way to the top. Cable winches attached to the ramp framework raise and lower the paver on the slope. Upon completion of a paved strip with the paver on the ramp, the entire assembly is then moved over for the next paving pass.

When the paving operation proceeds down the slope, the ramp is not nec- essary. Recently, this has become the preferred method of paving the slope.

Several methods can be used to supply the paver with hot mix for slope paving. Probably the most common is to load the paver at the bottom of the slope with enough mix for one complete trip up the slope. Another is to load the paver at the top of the slope and then lower it to the bottom for the paving pass. When slope distances are too great for paving a strip without recharging, a front-end loader with a side-dump bucket has been used to supply the paver partway up the slope. Cranes have also been suc- cessfully used to supply the paver on the slope. Material transfer vehicles at the bottom of the slope, feeding conveyors equipped with belting material approved for handling hot materials, have also been used successfully.

Light steel-wheel rollers and vibratory steel-wheel rollers are often used for compacting the freshly spread mix on the slope. Power winches and cables are usually required for maneuvering the roller up and down the slope. The rolling is done independently of the paving, but it should begin as soon as possible without interfering with the paver. The initial rolling pass is usually made up the slope. Depending on the stability of the mix, the vibrator is not desired on the initial pass but on the following coverages.

When the lining is to be placed in two or more courses, the paving joints should be staggered or offset. Emphasis should be on a tight bond for impermeability.

A tack coat is usually required whenever an asphalt mix is placed over another paved surface. For bonding impermeable asphalt layers together, a tack coat of SS-1, SS-Ih, CSS-1, or CSS-1h emulsified-asphalt diluted 1:1 with water and applied at a rate of 0.05-0.15 gallons per square yard (0.2-0.7 liter per square meter) over the surface to be paved will usually be adequate. Tack coats of emulsified asphalt should be allowed to cure fully before paving; otherwise moisture will be trapped between the two layers at construction.

Tack coats can be critical when used to bond two impermeable layers together. An excessive tack coat application combined with warm tem- peratures can cause a slippage plane between two layers on a sloped surface. On the other hand, unless the two layers are bonded together so that there are no voids or pores in the interface, seepage water from a crack or opened joint may get between the layers. If this happens, hydrostatic pressures can develop and eventually cause layer separation.

Permeable Surface/Lining

Essentially, the procedures for placing permeable surface lining mixes are the same as those for impermeable asphalt mixes. However, they are generally placed in one layer 3-6 inches (7.5-15 centimeters) thick, rather than in two or more courses.

There is no real effort to obtain density in open-graded drainage layer courses; the intent is only to firmly seat the aggregate particles. Exces- sive rolling can be harmful because of aggregate degradation or by the breaking of the asphalt bond between particles, particularly if the mix temperature is below 150°F (66°C). For this reason, generally, only a static steel-wheel roller is used.

Porous Drainage Layer

The same construction for permeable linings is used for porous drain- age layers, with the expectation that the mixing temperature at the hot mix facility should not exceed 250°F (121°C). Higher temperatures may cause the asphalt to drain from the aggregate particles and settle in the bottom of the truck bed as it is hauled to the paving site. This problem is not usually encountered with mixes such as mix composition D.

Asphait seals

An asphalt seal may be applied to an asphalt surface for a number of reasons. It may be designed to make the surface more watertight, or to protect it from abrasion by waves, water currents, or even by ice. It may also be used to protect the surface from mud curl or the curling of drying algae along the waterline, or to reduce the rate of oxidation of the exposed asphalt surface. These seals are generally of two types.

° Sprayed asphalt sealers/asphalt cement. (It should be noted that emulsified asphalt or cutback asphalt cannot be used as a seal for reservoirs that are used for drinking water, to avoid the residual emulsifying agent or cutter stock from contaminating the water.) The asphalt should be sprayed over the surface of an asphalt lining at the rate of 0.25 gallons per square yard (1 liter per square meter). This will provide a film coating as much as 0.04 inch (1 millimeter) thick. A continuous film coating will fill and seal any exposed pores and increase the water-tightness of the asphalt lining. It will also tend to fill and seal small cracks in the surface that may have been caused by improper rolling procedures in compacting the lining.

The surface should be clean, dry, and free from loose material. A sloped surface usually necessitates hand spraying. This should be done in a back and forth sweeping motion to build up the film and to keep the asphalt from flowing down the slope.

° Asphalt mastic seals. Asphalt mastics applied to the surfaces of asphalt linings provide water proofing and extra surface protection from mechanical abuse. Asphalt mastics permit a heavier coating than sprayed asphalt applications, and well-designed mixtures can make the surface resistant to abrasion by waves, or scouring by waterborne sands.

Asphalt mastic mixtures for this purpose are essentially blends of mineral filler and asphalt cement. A small percentage (about 2 percent) of inert fibers is sometimes added to the mix to increase stability. The composition of the mastic for sealing can vary widely. However, it should usually fall within these ranges:

Mineral filler (typically lime) 50-70 percent Asphalt Cement 30-50 percent

Such mixes require the use of a squeegee. Depending on the consistency of the mix, the surface thickness will be 3% inch (3 millimeters) or less. Fine sand may be added to give a gritty surface texture. When this is done, asphalt contents below 30 percent may be desirable.

These mixes usually are too liquid to prepare in a conventional HMA plant; special mixing equipment may be necessary.

The same grade of binder should be used in the mastic seal as was used in the mix for the liner, but lower viscosity grades can also be employed. Asphalt mastic seals are not easy to produce or to apply.

15.3 HMA Utilization in Railway Track Beds

15.3.1 Introduction

Since deregulation in 1980, the U.S. freight railroad industry has expe- rienced unprecedented growth in traffic volumes, revenue ton-miles, and wheel loadings. This growth has necessitated the application of new technologies to provide stronger and longer-lasting track and support structures.

The railroad companies and the asphalt paving industry have developed designs and applications for utilizing a support layer of HMA to strengthen the track structure. HMA is being used in new mainline tracks, yards, and terminals. It is also being used as a solution to specific trackbed (mainte- nance) instability problems in existing trackbeds. These include the recon- struction of turnouts, railroad crossings, bridge approaches, hump tracks, tunnel floors and approaches, highway crossings, and loading facilities where conventional trackbed designs have not performed satisfactorily. The use of HMA in railway track beds results in an optimal track modulus, which significantly reduces the rail and rolling stock wear.

Two designs are used to incorporate HMA in the track structure as depicted in Figures 15.8 and 15.9. The most common design used on U.S. railroads is known as HMA underlayment in which the HMA layer is placed directly on select subgrade or old roadbed and the typical layer of ballast is subsequently placed between the HMA layer and the ties. This design requires little change from normal trackbed designs because the HMA layer merely replaces the typical granular subballast layer.

The HMA layer is typically 5-8 inches (125-200 millimeters) thick and 12-14 feet (3.7—4.3 meters) wide. It is similar to a paved lane of a highway, but is contained within the track structure to form a structural layer or hardpan between the ballast and subgrade or existing roadbed.

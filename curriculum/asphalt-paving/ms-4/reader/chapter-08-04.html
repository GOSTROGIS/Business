When shoulders are added to an existing paved roadway, the same pro- cedures and precautions for roadway widening are observed. Also, the vertical face of the existing pavement should be given an ade- quate application of tack coat to seal the joint.

When paved shoulders are part of the initial construction of a roadway, procedures for paving travel lanes should be followed.

Superelevation

Paving curve sections with superelevated cross slopes on new construc- tion does not generally pose a problem because the prepared foundation material is usually superelevated or sloped the same as the new pavement. This allows the same uniform thickness that is being placed on the tangent or straight sections to be placed on the curved or superelevated sections. As the paver proceeds through the transition to the superelevated section and out again, the paver and the screed tilt accordingly on the prepared foundation, and little if any adjustment in the screed controls is required.

If the superelevation is not built into the prepared base or subgrade, then it must be built into the HMA pavement with variable thickness layers. Today’s pavers are equipped with automatic slope controls, allowing the transverse cross slope to be dialed in as the paver moves through the superelevated curve. Sensors riding on an erected or traveling stringline maintain grade control for the end of the screed nearest the sensor. The depth at the other end of the screed depends on the transverse slope setting. When the paver reaches the point where the superelevated slope begins to change, stakes or other markers are placed on adjacent lanes with the required cross-slopes settings so that the change is slowly dialed in as the paver proceeds through the transition. The change in slope is gradual, and small errors in dialing do not affect the riding quality of the pavement. Once the paver enters the full superelevated cross slope, the slope setting will remain the same through- out. Then, as the paver exits the fully superelevated section, the cross slope is gradually dialed out using the same procedure, but in reverse.

Asphalt Ditches and Spillways

Ditches lie parallel to the highway at the foot of large cut sections or between dual-lane pavements having a depressed median strip. In many locations in rolling terrain, a hard impervious surface is required to prevent costly erosion. In deep-cut sections, it is often desired to construct ditches over the tops of slopes and around the ends to prevent slope erosion. Spill- ways to carry off water collected by curbs or dikes are subject to high water velocities and may need to be paved. In all cases, such channels should be sufficiently wide and deep so that the heaviest anticipated runoff is carried away smoothly. In some instances, very large outfall ditches may be neces- sary to carry runoff completely away from the right-of-way.

HMA makes an excellent ditch or spillway-lining material. As a rule, highway-surface course HMA need only be modified by increasing the binder and mineral filler contents as necessary to ensure a watertight and durable lining. The resulting lower stability is not a concern; it actually adds to the capacity of the lining to adjust to minor settlement without cracking.

Special slip-form paving machines are available for placing at least part of the ditch linings. Spreader boxes, paving machines, and even hand-placing pro- cedures may be used. Linings are made as thick as necessary for the use and stresses involved, but practical experience indicates that a finished thickness of 1% inches (40 millimeters) of HMA should be the minimum thickness specified. Compaction of HMA in ditches and spillways is accomplished by hand tamps, weighted slip forms, small vibrating plate compactors, or small rollers.

Roadway Shoulder Rumblie Strips

Rumble strips were first used on New Jersey’s Garden State Parkway in 1955. They provide a warning for drivers when their vehicle begins to run off the road. Rumble strips are continuous bands of raised material or indentations that are formed or grooved in the shoulders. They alert driv- ers by transmitting sound and vibration through the vehicle.

The two most widely used methods for installing rumble strips in an asphalt pavement are milled-in or rolled-in.

Rolled-in rumble strips are created by using a steel-wheel roller (see Figure 8.30) to which half-sections of metal pipe or solid-steel bars are welded. The compaction equipment presses the shape of the bar into the HMA shoulder surface. The indentation is about 1 inch (25 millimeters) deep, 2 to 2.5 inches (50 to 63 millimeters) wide and 18 to 35 inches (450 to 900 millimeters) long, transverse to the travel direction. The indentations are usually set 8 inches (200 millimeters) on center and offset from the travel lane edge by 6 to 12 inches (150 to 300 millimeters). See Figure 8.31 for a picture of rolled-in rumble strips.

7. Foreman’s/inspector’s Check Sheet

The following list includes important points to keep in mind for the project foreman, or inspector, who is responsible for placing an HMA pavement.

To build a smooth pavement:

e Never run the hopper empty between loads (The level of material in the hoppers should never be allowed to fall below the bottom of the flow gates.)

Establish and maintain a continuous paving speed. (Establish the speed by balancing the delivery of material with the compaction process.) Control the head of material in the auger area to within +1 inch.

Do not allow trucks to bump the paver.

Do not dump excess material on the grade (When dumping directly into the paver hopper or cleaning truck tailgates.)

Do not fold the hopper wings into an empty conveyor system. (Fold hoppers often enough while moving forward to keep HMA at place- ment temperature.)

Practice good starting and stopping techniques of the paver.

Watch for overcorrecting of the depth screws.

Make sure the edger plate is adjusted properly.

To make good longitudinal joints:

Strive to minimize segregation at the outside edges of the new HMA. Steer a straight line on the first lane.

Utilize the proper overlap of the cold HMA pavement being matched. The depth of the HMA pavement being laid should be approximately 25 percent greater in depth than the HMA pavement being matched for rolldown.

Watch that material is not allowed to flow out or under the edger plates. (This excess material can make getting a good joint impossible.)

If luting is required, push material back to the hot side of the joint only and do not cast material across the mat.

Use proper rolling procedures for compaction. (See Chapter 9.)

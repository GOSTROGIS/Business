14.1 Introduction

Many miles of Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavement are rehabilitated each year by placing an asphalt overlay on them. According to the FHWA’s “Highway Statistics 2005,” there were more than 40,000 more miles of composite pavements than rigid (PCC surface) pavements in the United States. Chapter 6, “Thickness Design” discusses the thickness design procedures for those overlays. This chapter discusses techniques used to either manage the reflection cracking in those overlays or elimi- nate the occurrence of reflection cracking.

14.1.1 Reflection Cracking

Reflection cracking is the result of horizontal and vertical movements at the joints and cracks in the underlying pavement, resulting in a pattern of cracking in the surface that reflects the joints or cracks in the underly- ing PCC pavement. Temperature changes in the pavement cause the slabs to contract and expand. This movement is most pronounced at joints and cracks, which open and close in response to the volume changes in the PCC slabs. Opening of joints and cracks due to cooling causes tensile stress directly over these locations in the HMA overlay (see Figure 14.1). Reflection cracking may occur after an extreme cold temperature event or after repeated opening and closing.

Traffic loading may produce differential vertical movement across the joint or crack, particularly when load transfer is inefficient. This causes shear and bending stress in the overlay. As the wheel (see Figure 14.2) approaches, the shear stress will reach a maximum at point A, and as it passes over the joint, it will reverse at point C. As the wheel load passes over the joint at point B, the bending stress will reach a maximum.

The key to delaying or avoiding reflection cracking is to reduce or eliminate the stresses and deformations produced in the overlay. Some treatments include the placement of an interlayer to reduce or dampen the movement at the joint or crack caused by thermal contraction of the PCC. Some of the techniques are discussed in Chapter 13, “Pavement Rehabilitation Methods for HMA Pavements,” including the use of geo- textiles, asphalt-rubber “stress-absorbing membrane interlayers” (SAMIs), etc. Single- and multiple-surface treatments have also been used as an interlayer for rehabilitation of HMA- and PCC-surfaced pavements, as have specialty asphalt paving mixtures that are extremely ductile and resistant to cracking. Another widely used approach is to control reflection crack- ing by sawing and sealing the overlay directly over the joints. This will be discussed in more detail later in this chapter.

14.2 Saw-and-Seal

Saw-and-seal has been used for many years to slow the rate of deterioration of reflection cracks in relatively thin overlays of jointed PCCP. The technique consists of carefully referencing all the joints in the existing pavement before overlay and then sawing and sealing contraction joints in the HMA overlay directly over the underlying joints. When properly done, a weakened plane is formed directly above the joint defining the ultimate cracking pattern in the overlay. The saw-and-seal procedure will do the following:

° Provide maintainable joints

e Reduce or eliminate the spalling associated with reflection cracking

° Provide for good ride quality

° Provide a neat, well-maintained appearance

Saw-and-seal has most commonly been used on jointed-reinforced con- crete (JRPC) pavements on highway and street projects in New England and Louisiana, as well as on airfield pavements. JRPC pavements gener- ally have longer joint spacing than jointed-plain concrete (JCPC) pave- ments, resulting in longer slabs and thus greater opening and closing movement at the joints. It should only be considered for jointed concrete pavements that are in good structural condition, have good joint load transfer, and have intact panels (no working cracks) and minimal spalling. Thought should be given to how the joints can be practically referenced before selecting this approach, because missing the joint by more than one inch usually results in a jagged, irregular reflection crack alongside a neat sawed and sealed crack.

This technique should not be considered for the following:

¢ Overlays on JCPC that have a high frequency of mid-panel cracking

(more than 20 percent) or badly deteriorated cracking. Panels with working cracks should either be patched or replaced with a new con- crete panel.

¢ Overlays on JCPC with badly deteriorated joints and/or significant

patching at or near the joints

* Overlays where there is insufficient load transfer across the joints as

evidenced by %-inch (0.0625 millimeters) faulting and/or pumping. When tested using nondestructive testing equipment, joints should have a minimum 60 percent load-transfer efficiency.

° Overlays of pavements with wide joints—greater than 34 inches

(19 millimeters)

14.2.1 Design Considerations

Minimum Thickness

Saw-and-seal is commonly considered when the overlay thickness that can be placed is limited. Examples include urban streets with curb and gutter sections, intersections and driveways, and highways with vertical clearance restrictions. As previously suggested, an overlay is best applied in two lifts to provide the greatest opportunity to improve ride quality and surface drain- age, but there have been numerous examples where a single lift of HMA has been placed, has been sawed and sealed, and has provided excellent performance. The minimum recommended thickness for a single lift of HMA is 2 inches (50 millimeters). For two lifts, the minimum thickness is 3% inches (90 millimeters).

Joint Configuration

Many state DOTs have specifications for this process. The engineer should check with the local DOT to ensure that his or her specification follows their requirements. The typical requirement for the joint is that the HMA layer be cut to one-third to one-half of the thickness of the HMA layer. This can range from % inch wide Gf a reservoir for the sealant is used) to % inch wide if no reservoir is used. Figure 14.3 presents the procedure most DOTs utilize, which is the % inch cut with a reservoir for the joint sealant material.

Urban and Other Areas

When using saw-and-seal in urban areas where curb and gutter sections are present, the saw cuts should line up with the control joints in the gut- ter sections, even if a slightly skewed saw cut results. The saw cuts should not be spaced more than 40 feet (12 meters) apart, and for thin JPCP, it may be beneficial to have the cuts spaced closer, such as 30 feet (9 meters) apart. For parking lots, airports, and other large areas of pavement, saw-and-seal has been shown to be effective when the saw cuts are made so that the pavement is cut into 50-foot (15-meter) squares.

14.2.2 Construction

Preparation Before Overlay

Before placing an HMA overlay, the existing concrete pavement should be repaired as needed. Concrete panels with working cracks, corner breaks, moderate-to-severe spalling, and joints exhibiting poor load trans- fer should be repaired before placing the overlay. Referencing the existing joints in the concrete pavement is critical. Fixed reference markers should be used. 14.3 Fractured Slab Techniques

Two procedures have been developed to fracture PCC slabs, thereby reducing the stresses and deformations that occur in an asphalt overlay— crack/break-and-seat and rubblization. The crack/break-and-seat procedure reduces the effective size of the PCC slabs and seats them firmly onto the underlying layers with heavy rollers. The rubblization process eliminates slab action by reducing the PCCP to aggregate-size pieces, resulting in a layer that functions in a similar way to an aggregate base course.

14.3.1 Crack/Break and Seat

Crack/break and seat minimizes joint and crack movement by shortening the effective slab length and firmly seating the broken PCC pieces onto the underlying layer (see Figure 14.7). The PCC is broken using a drop hammer, and then seated with a large, ballasted rubber-tire roller. The intent is to create slabs that are small enough to reduce the movement at the cracks in the PCC layer that were caused by low temperatures and daily temperature variation, while still being large enough to develop flexure under loading.

Cracking and seating is used on JCPC pavement, while breaking and seat- ing is used when the PCC pavement is JPCP. The broken slabs are seated to re-establish firm support between the underlying layers and the slabs, reducing the amount of differential vertical movement under traffic.

Historically, cracking and seating has performed very well, but this has not been the case with breaking and seat- ing because of the difficulty in ensuring that the steel has been ruptured. This problem has led many agencies to use rubblization instead of breaking and seating when rehabilitating joint reinforced concrete (JRC) pavements. Breaking and seating should only be considered for relatively thin (less than 8 inches; 200 millimeters) or variable thickness (often called “thickened- edge”) JRC pavements.

Some agencies have used various types of interlayers or specialty mix- tures in conjunction with cracking and seating. The interlayer provides added insurance against reflection cracking, and some can help seal the pavement structure. When a fabric or a SAMI is used, it is often recom- mended to place these directly on a leveling course of HMA rather than directly over the fractured PCC layer. If an open-graded crack relief layer is used, though, it should be placed directly on the fractured PCC, and provision must be made for removing any water collected within this layer by daylighting or using longitudinal drains.

Project Selection and Overview

Cracking/breaking and seating has been shown to be an effective tech- nique when the following distresses occur in the PCCP pavement:

° Seriously faulted transverse joints and cracks

° Rocking of slabs due to the presence of voids

¢ Presence of working transverse and longitudinal cracking

* Uneven slab settlement

* Corner breaks

* Blowups, shattered slabs

The quality of the underlying layers should be thoroughly investigated dur- ing the design process. It has been determined that the performance of the crack-and-seat process is not very good when the PCC was placed directly on a fine-grain subgrade, particularly in wet conditions. It is important that a thorough sampling and testing program be conducted during the design pro- cess. The thickness of the concrete pavement must be determined by cores. The properties of the subgrade should be determined by cutting test pits in the existing PCC pavement to determine the type, thickness, and strength of the base and subgrade materials. These underlying materials should be evaluated in the laboratory to determine their strength, using R-Value, CBR, or elastic modulus testing. The base materials should be evaluated to determine their drainability if a subdrainage system is to be used. The subgrade materi- als should also be evaluated for potential swelling problems.

Design Considerations

The overlay should be designed according to the procedures outlined in Chapter 6. If the existing PCC pavement shows signs of pumping, installa- tion of a subdrainage system to drain the water away from the pavement structure should be seriously considered. The Asphalt Institute recommends a minimum overlay thickness of 5 inches (125 millimeters) of HMA over cracked or broken and seated PCC.

14.3.2 Construction

The objective of the crack-and-seat process is to break the pavement into slabs ranging in size from 18 to 48 inches (0.5 to 1.5 meters). Breaking the pave- ment into the smaller sizes has shown to provide better performance. The PCC slab must be cracked full depth. A test strip should be established to ensure that the equipment and processes used are sufficient to thoroughly crack the PCC pavement. Cores should be drilled directly over cracks to verify that the slab is being cracked throughout the entire depth of the layer.

Cracking and Seating JCPC Pavement

Various types of equipment have been used to crack JCPC, including pile drivers, guillotine hammers, whip hammers, and impact hammers. Production rates will vary with the equipment type and the thickness of the pavement. The most common method used is the guillotine hammer (see Figure 14.8), which has high productivity and covers the full width of a lane.

After the JCPC has been cracked, the pieces must be firmly seated onto the underlying layer to prevent rocking under traffic. Seating is accom- plished with a large pneumatic roller that forces the broken slabs into full contact with the foundation. Two passes of a 50-ton (450 kilonewton) roller are specified for the seating operation. Excessive rolling should be avoided because it can result in a loss of interlock between the cracked pieces or in shear failure of the existing subbase or subgrade.

Breaking and Seating JRCP Pavement

As previously defined, breaking and seating is conducted on existing JRCP pavement, and the process must rupture the reinforcing steel to be effective. This process requires that the slabs be broken into pieces smaller than those required for crack and seating. For breaking and seating, it is recommended that the slabs be broken into pieces measuring 12-18 inches (300-460 millimeters). The steel should be cut by transverse sawing of the pavement at routine intervals to ensure debonding in the transverse direction. The seating process is accomplished with a heavy pneumatic roller in a manner similar to that used in the crack-and-seat process, except that additional passes are needed to ensure rupturing of the steel. Five to seven passes of a 35-50 ton (315-450 kilonewton) rubber tire roller are recommended. Any soft spots or local failures identified in the seating process require careful removal and replacement. It is important to address drainage or unsuitable foundation conditions during this process. The broken PCC should be replaced with a full-depth HMA patch. The areas to be replaced should be sized to accom- modate the equipment necessary to properly place and compact the new material.

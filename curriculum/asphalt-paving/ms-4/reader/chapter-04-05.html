4.8.2 Open-Graded Friction Course

Description and Usage

Open-Graded Friction Courses (OGFC) provide an open mix with permeable voids that provide drainage on the surface of an HMA pavement under wet conditions. The water drains vertically through the OGFC to an impermeable, underlying layer and then laterally to the day-lighted edge of the OGFC. In addition to minimizing hydroplaning and providing high friction resistance on wet pavements, an OGFC layer will also provide the following advantages:

° Reduce splash and spray (see Figure 4.28)

° Reduce the potential for hydroplaning

° Enhance visibility of pavement markings

° Reduce nighttime surface glare in wet weather

° Reduce tire-pavement noise While open-graded mixes provide many advantages, they have the following limitations:

¢ Snow- and ice-control procedures may need adjustment when they

are used,

° They may ravel and shove when used at intersections, locations with

heavy turning movements, and airfield ramp terminals.

° They may require special patching and rehabilitation techniques to

maintain adequate drainage.

Open-graded mixes have been used throughout the United States since 1944. In many states they have been used extensively for many years. In the early 1970s, many states experienced problems with OGFC layers. These problems were raveling, delamination, loss of permeability after a few years of service, and stripping of the underlying layers. These problems have been addressed by one or more of the following: use of polymer-modified asphalt binders, relatively high asphalt binder contents

Mix Design Characteristics and Procedures The two published mix design procedures for the design of open-graded friction courses include the procedure outlined in the FHWA Technical Advi- sory T 5040.31 and a procedure based on research by the National Center for Asphalt Technology (NCAT). Most OGFC surface layers placed in the United States are designed by the specifying agency, using its own internal techniques and experience to accomplish the mix design. It is recommended that a designer check with the specifying agency about its procedure when designing OGFC mixes. The mix design consists of four steps: 1. Materials selection 2. Selection of the design gradation 3. Determination of the optimum asphalt content 4. Evaluation of moisture susceptibility

Materials Selection

The materials used for OGFC include aggregates, asphalt binders, and additives (such as fibers). The aggregates must be high-quality, polish- resistant aggregate materials. The NCAT procedure requires the coarse aggregate used in an OGFC to have a Los Angeles Abrasion value of less than 30 percent. Soft aggregates should be avoided due to the poten- tial for excessive breakdown during mix production and compaction in the field. The aggregate should consist of 100 percent fractured faces to provide high internal friction. If crushed gravel is used, it should have at least 95 percent of the particles with two fractured faces and 100 percent with one fractured face. The fractured faces on gravels should be produced by mechanical fracturing of the rock. The percentage of flat and elongated particles should not exceed 5 to 20 percent corresponding to ratios of 5:1 and 3:1. The fine aggregate used in the mixture should have a fine aggregate angularity of at least 45 percent.

The asphalt binder used should be based on the environment and traffic levels of the roadway. A stiffer binder than those recommended based on the procedures outlined in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt” is recommended for use. It is suggested that a binder two grades stiffer than the grade recommended for dense-graded mixes be used and that they be made with polymer-modified asphalts. The addition of fiber may be desirable to reduce the amount of draindown during construction. Either a cellulose fiber is added at about 0.3 percent of total mix or a mineral fiber added at about 0.4 percent of total mix. The dosage rate for the fibers depends on the results of the draindown test.

4.8.3 Asphalt-Treated Permeable Base

General Description

An asphalt-treated permeable base (ATPB) is built as a base for an HMA pavement. The purpose of this layer is to intercept the water that enters through the pavement surface before it can reach the unbound layers, result- ing in lowering the strength of those layers. The water enters the pavement through cracks in the surface and/or a poorly compacted and therefore permeable HMA layer. The layer should be designed to provide sufficient air-voids to make the layer permeable (approximately 10 percent). The per- formance and maintenance of these systems is discussed in more detail in Chapter 5 of this handbook.

Mix Design Characteristics and Procedures

The mix design of an ATPB is very similar to that used for an OGFC layer. The goal is to provide a free-draining layer with sufficient asphalt binder to cement the particles together and to prevent stripping of the asphalt binder from the aggregate.

Table 4.11 presents gradations being used by different agencies for an ATPB layer.

The asphalt binder can be either the standard grade of binder used by the agency (such as a 64-22) or a modified binder. The primary concern in the choice of asphalt binder is insuring the coating of the aggregate and minimiz- ing the amount of draindown during construction. Coating is evaluated by visually observing the mix in the laboratory during mixing. Different binder contents should be evaluated. The draindown test discussed in the sections on SMA and OFGC can be used to evaluate the draindown characteristics of the mix. If the draindown exceeds 0.3 percent by weight of aggregate, steps should be taken to reduce the draindown, such as using fibers or a polymer- modified asphalt binder.

The air-voids in the mix can be evaluated in the same manner as with the OGFC mixes. The mix should be compacted using 50 gyrations with a SGC and the bulk specific gravity of the ATPB determined using the CoreLok® procedure.

4.9 Performance

Testing of HMA

4.9.1 Introduction

Performance testing of HMA mixes is conducted to predict the perfor- mance of HMA prior to use on a transportation facility and to evaluate new materials and design tools to improve the performance of HMA pavements. HMA mixture testing can be divided into five general categories:

* Modulus—shear, dynamic and resilient modulus

° Creep—static and dynamic creep

° Flexural beam fatigue

¢ Empirical

* Wheel-tracking tests

4.9.2 Modulus Testing

The modulus of a material is used to calculate the expected deformation from a specified load. The modulus of elasticity is determined by applying increasing loads at a specified rate and measuring the resulting deformation. Loads are converted to stress by dividing by the cross-sectional area of the specimen section under load. Deformation is converted to strain by dividing the change in length (deformation) by the original (non-deformed) length. In shear testing, the shear modulus (G) of a material is determined by dividing the shear stress (7) by the shear strain (y). In axial testing, the modulus of elas- ticity (E) is determined by dividing the axial stress (o) by the axial strain (e).

Shear Modulus

The specimens used for shear and dynamic modulus testing are gener- ally manufactured using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC). An HMA sample is obtained by mixing the materials in the laboratory or from a hot mix asphalt mixing facility. Mixtures prepared in the laboratory are subjected to short-term oven aging (STOA). For shear modulus testing a STOA of four hours at 275°F (135°C) is used. This is longer than the two hours used in Superpave for mix design. Stiffening of mixtures and binder frequently occurs in any HMA facility; therefore the STOA procedure is not used for plant-produced mixtures.

The shear modulus test is used to describe the viscoelastic behavior of an asphalt mix. It is described in AASHTO TP 7. A sinusoidal shear strain of 0.01 percent (peak-to-peak) is applied to the specimen at ten frequencies (0.01 to 10 Hz). The shear stress and strain are then used to calculate the complex shear modulus (G*) of the asphalt mixture. 4.9.3 Creep Testing

Creep testing is a simple version of a controlled stress test. There are two types of creep tests—static creep and dynamic creep. In the static creep test a constant axial load is applied to the test specimen throughout the duration of the test. In the dynamic creep test, a large number of repeated axial load applications are applied to the specimen. It is called “dynamic” because the specimen is being continually loaded and unloaded throughout the dura- tion of the test.

Static creep

In the static creep test an axial load is rapidly applied (within 0.05 sec) to the test specimen until a desired axial stress is achieved (usually between 69 and 207 kiloPascals). This axial stress is then maintained until the total axial strain reaches approximately 2 percent (20,000 microstrain) or tertiary flow occurs in the test specimen. At any time during the test, the applied stress can be divided by the observed strain to arrive at a creep modulus or stiffness. Because the strain changes with time, so does the creep modulus. In creep testing, many engineers prefer to compute creep compliance rather than creep stiffness. Creep compliance can be portrayed as the inverse of creep stiff- ness (although it is not strictly correct, theoretically). In other words, creep compliance is the strain at any time during the test per unit of stress. At the conclusion of a creep test, the test specimen will partially rebound to its original shape. For many materials, however, some permanent deforma- tion remains in the test specimen.

The rate of change of creep compliance versus loading time is then calculated and plotted as shown in Figure 4.31. The minimum point of the curve occurs where the rate of creep compliance changes as a function of time is zero. 4.9.4 Flexural Fatigue Test

The flexural fatigue test is conducted to evaluate the fatigue charac- teristics of an HMA mixture. Fatigue cracking of pavement is considered to be more a structural problem than simply a material problem. Several external factors influence the fatigue cracking in pavements, such as poor subgrade drainage, time of placement, and method of compaction and placement of the asphalt mix.

The specimens for this test are 2.5 inches (62.5 millimeters) by 2 inches (50 millimeters) by 15 inches (375 millimeters) beams. The test is conducted in accordance to the procedures in AASHTO T 321. In this method, repeated haversine loads (0.1 second load and 0.4 second unload) are applied at the third points of the specimen. 4.9.5 Empirical Tests

Empirical tests have been used to relate the performance of an HMA pave- ment to a standardized test. The results from the test do not provide funda- mental engineering properties that can be used in calculations of stresses and strains in an HMA pavement. Rather, they have shown through field per- formance to be indicative of performance. The three most commonly used empirical tests for HMA pavements are the Marshall test, the Hveem test, and the indirect tensile test; these were described earlier in this chapter.

4.9.6 Wheel-Tracking Tests

Hamburg Wheel-Tracking Device (HWTD)

This machine was developed by the Germans in the 1970s. The test pro- cedure is AASHTO T 324. The HWTD measures the combined effects of rut- ting and moisture on an HMA mixture. A steel wheel rolls across the surface of the HMA core, which is immersed in hot water. The steel wheel moves through a crank connected to a flywheel. The steel wheel has a diameter of 8 inches (204 millimeters) and is 1.85 inches (47 millimeters) wide with a 154-pound load applied. The testing is done with the samples in a hot water bath at 50°C. The test is run for 10,000 cycles (20,000 passes) or until a deformation of 0.787 inch (20 millimeters) is reached. The machine is shown in Figure 4.35.

Figure 4.36 is a sample of the typical data obtained from the HWTD. Three points are identified on the curve: the creep slope, the stripping slope, and the stripping inflection point. The creep slope occurs before the onset of strip- ping and is the inverse of the rate of deformation in the linear range of the deformation curve. This portion of the curve is where rutting will occur due to consolidation and plastic flow. The stripping slope is related to the severity of the damage due to moisture. It is the inverse of the rate of deformation in the linear region after the onset of the stripping to the end of the test. The stripping inflection point is related to the resistance of the HMA tested to the effects of the moisture. This point is determined at the number of passes at which the creep slope and the stripping slope intersect.

In its executive summary on performance testing of HMA mixes, NCAT suggested rut-depth criteria of less than 0.393 inch (10 millimeters) after 10,000 cycles (20,000 passes) for conventional dense-graded HMA mixes.

Asphalt Pavement Analyzer (APA)

The Asphalt Pavement Analyzer (APA) is a modification of the Georgia Loaded Wheel Tester (GLWT), which was developed in a cooperative study of the Georgia Department of Transportation and the Georgia Institute of Technology in 1985. It has been used primarily to evaluate the rutting resistance of an HMA mixture. Studies have been conducted to evaluate the use of the equipment for evaluating fatigue and moisture resistance of HMA mixtures. The APA is shown in Figure 4.37.

A loaded wheel is placed on a pressurized hose, which sits on the test specimens, and then tracked back and forth to induce rutting. Generally, the specimens are tested to 8,000 cycles. Samples can be tested dry or while submerged in water.

The test specimens for the APA can be either beam or cylindrical specimens. The beam samples are generally compacted using the Asphalt Vibratory Compactor (Figure 4.38). The cylindrical specimens are made with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor. The beams are compacted to 7 percent air-voids, and the cylindrical specimens are compacted to both 4 and 7 percent air-voids. Cores from an in-service roadway can also be tested.

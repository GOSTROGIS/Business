13.1 HMA Overlays over

Asphalt Pavements

The most popular method of pavement rehabilitation for both hot mix asphalt (HMA) pavements and Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements is the asphalt overlay. This chapter discusses the rehabilitation of HMA pavements, and Chapter 14, “Rehabilitation of Concrete Pavements with HMA” covers the use of HMA overlays for PCC pavements. Other options for resurfacing, such as surface treatments, are discussed in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance”. Properly designed and constructed, an asphalt overlay is a cost-effective method of correcting surface deficien- cies, restoring user satisfaction, and increasing the load-carrying capacity of the roadway.

The first step in developing an asphalt pavement rehabilitation project is to conduct a detailed project-level pavement evaluation using the proce- dures detailed in Chapter 11, “Pavement Management.” This information is needed for identifying available rehabilitation alternatives and for design- ing the project. In addition, the designer needs data to assess the structural adequacy of the pavement. This data is collected by sampling and testing the materials in the pavement, by conducting in situ testing, or by conduct- ing deflection testing. In some situations, the project’s physical constraints may require pavement reconstruction rather than overlay.

The three purposes for overlaying an asphalt pavement are the following:

° Heavy structural overlay. A heavy structural overlay is more than 5 inches (125 millimeters) thick. These overlays considerably improve the load-bearing capacity of the pavement and usually have the same design period as a newly constructed pavement.

¢ Structural overlay. A structural overlay is 3-5 inch (75-125 millime- ters) thick. These overlays add strength to an old pavement and last longer than functional or maintenance overlays.

° Functional or maintenance overlay. A functional overlay is 0.75-3 inches (20-75 millimeters) thick. Functional overlays are intended to address ride quality, surface friction or other minor problems for pavements that are structurally sound. Thin HMA overlays are also a popular choice for preventative maintenance. A functional overlay may be placed after the surface has been cold milled to replace the material that was removed. This technique is commonly called “mill and fill,” or referred to as an inlay.

Pavement Condition

Pavement condition is the most important consideration when deciding which rehabilitation process to use. A discussion of the different pave- ment distress types and mechanisms is provided in Chapter 11, “Pave- ment Management,” and MS-17, Asphalt Overlays for Highway and Street Rehabilitation. A pavement in good condition—little surface distress and no evidence of any structural failure problems—is a good candidate for a functional rehabilitation, which might include a surface treatment, mill and fill overlay or hot-in-place recycling. A pavement in poor to failed condition requires an extensive rehabilitation, which might include cold- in-place recycling or full-depth reclamation prior to an overlay.

Construction Feasibility

An HMA overlay requires less traffic control than a reconstruction project because in most cases the overlay can be placed one lane at a time. But some pre-overlay techniques, such as full-depth reclamation, require that the roadway be closed or that its usage be significantly restricted. Therefore, some techniques in urban areas or on mountainous roadways with limited width may not be appropriate. In some situations, the lack of specific equipment in the project locality may limit the feasibility of a particular rehabilitation method. For example, it may not be economically feasible to mobilize specific equipment for a small project, such as a hot- in-place or cold-in-place recycling train. Overlay placement that increases the thickness of the existing pavement has the disadvantage of decreasing overhead clearances under bridges or utilities. Also, the overlay placement in an urban area may cause loss of curb reveal or problems at adjoining driveways and intersections. Any possible clearance problems must be considered in the initial design, and steps must be taken to solve them. Possible solutions are to reconstruct the section under the bridge or utility or to mill the existing roadway below the level of the curb line and cross streets. These steps must be taken before placing the overlay.

Performance A number of factors can lead to diminished asphalt overlay performance, including the following: ¢ Insufficient overlay thickness ° The use of the wrong type of HMA surface for the overlay ° Insufficient preparation of the existing pavement before the overlay ° Insufficient consideration of the potential for reflection cracking

Overlay Thickness

The overlay thickness must be designed for the anticipated traffic over the design period. It should be designed using the principles and procedures discussed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design.” The selection of the proper material for the overlay is discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt CHMA) Design.”

Pre-overlay Preparation

Overlay performance is influenced by the amount, type, and severity of distress in the existing pavement. Extensive repair of the existing pavement will maximize the performance of the overlay. For example, replacing all areas that have medium-to-severe alligator cracking and improving all areas with poor drainage on a project will significantly increase the life of a pavement rehabilitation project. This is one approach to pre-overlay preparation. The other approach is to place an overlay of sufficient thick- ness to protect all the weak areas in the existing pavement structure. If an existing base or subbase layer has deteriorated, the designer increases the thickness of the overlay to account for the decreased strength of the underlying layers, instead of removing that layer. As the overlay thickness is increased, the applied loads are distributed over a larger area in the lower layers, thereby decreasing the stresses and deflections on the weak layer. The thickness required to adequately protect weak layers usually makes this approach economically unfeasible. Figure 13.1 shows the total repair cost and the total overlay cost plotted against the percentage of area repaired. When the percentage of the total area repaired increases, the cost of the overlay will decrease. But when these two costs are added together, the total cost of the project reaches a point at which it is mini- mized. When the total cost begins to increase relative to the percentage of the area repaired, the designer should consider reconstructing or using full-depth reclamation as more cost-effective design alternatives.

Reflection Cracking Control

Reflection cracking is the appearance of the pattern of joints or cracks in the old pavement at the surface of an overlay, and can be a serious problem to consider when choosing an approach for asphalt pavement rehabilitation. 13.2 Preparing Pavements for Overlays

13.2.1 Local Weak Areas (Patching)

Any structurally inadequate areas should be removed and replaced using proper patching techniques before placing the new overlay. Detailed visual inspection should be used to identify any load-associated distress such as alligator cracking. “Proofrolling,” or observing the amount and the extent of deformation in the existing pavement while driving a loaded truck over the pavement surface, is a very quick and simple method of defining the structur- ally inadequate areas. These areas should be removed and patched following the procedures detailed in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance.”

13.2.2 Leveling Courses

If milling is not used to correct surface distresses, the placement of one or more leveling courses may be required to restore proper line and cross-section. A leveling course can be either a scratch course or a wedge course. Both are designed to fill in low spots on the existing pavement surface and to restore cross-slope.

Scratch courses can be placed with either a paving machine or a motor grader (Figure 13.2). They are thin courses of HMA used to fill in small undulations in the pavement surface. The HMA mix used for a scratch course generally has a small nominal maximum particle size (many times a sand mix or a 98 inch [4.75 millimeters] mix). Care should be taken that the aggregate in the mix used in a scratch coat not be so large that drag- ging of the mix with the motor grader or the paving machine results.

Leveling wedges are courses of HMA used to level depressions or correct cross-slope issues (for example, adding superelevation to an otherwise flat horizon curve) in an old pavement before additional layers of HMA are placed. Leveling wedges should be placed in lifts of not more than 3 inches (75 millmeters). Figure 13.3 illustrates several design considerations for constructing leveling wedges. The HMA can be placed with either a motor grader or a paving machine. Care should be taken in choosing the mix for a leveling course. The thickness of the leveling course should be at least three times the nominal maximum particle size for the mix being used.

13.2.4 Widening

Pavement widening includes adding paved shoulders to an existing pavement or adding additional lanes. The thickness for the widened section should be designed and constructed to match the existing pavement plus the overlay pavement. The structural number (see Chapter 6 “Thickness Design”) should be equal or exceed the structural number of the mainline pavement. The materials and the construction procedures used for widening are similar to those used for new HMA pavement construction. A geotechnical engineer should be consulted to evaluate the need for a subsurface longitudinal drain- age system, and, if necessary, design one.

13.2.5 Drainage

If drainage problems are a contributing factor to the need for the overlay, they must be addressed. A frequent cause of failure is inadequate pavement and subgrade drainage. An assessment of the drainage-related pavement damage is critical in the development of the overlay design. Correcting a drainage problem can be as simple as cleaning or deepening the side ditches to permit rapid runoff of surface water, or the correction may be as complex as installing a longitudinal drainage system. Chapter 5, “Soils and Drainage for Asphalt Pavements,” contains a discussion of the design and construction of a longitudinal drainage system.

13.2.6 Interlayer Techniques

Function of an interlayer An interlayer is a layer of material placed between an existing pavement (either HMA or PCC) and the overlay. The purpose of the interlayer is to minimize the reflection cracking in an overlay. Reflection cracking is the result of horizontal and vertical movements in the cracks in the underly- ing asphalt pavement. Reflection cracking results in increased roughness and infiltration of surface water into the underlying layers. This increase in moisture results in a weakening of the support for the pavement. The use of an interlayer will not always prevent reflection cracking, but it has been found that the proper use of an interlayer will: ° Retard the occurrence of the reflection cracking (delay the onset of the cracking) ° Reduce the severity of the crack when it does occur ¢ Provide a waterproofing membrane for the underlying layers ° Reinforce the asphalt overlay to reduce horizontal stresses in the asphalt layer

Geotextiles

Woven or non-woven synthetic geotextiles made of polypropylene, poly- ester, fiberglass, nylon, and combinations of these are used as interlayers for HMA pavements. There are many of these products on the market, and the engineer is encouraged to contract the local DOT and various suppliers for information on a specific product. They are placed directly on the existing pavement surface after a heavy tack coat is applied. The geotextile is rolled or brushed into the tack coat and an overlay is placed on top of the geotex- tile. Many agencies place the geotextile within the overlay. This requires two lifts of HMA and a minimum overlay thickness of 3 inches (75 millimeters).

After surface preparation is complete, an asphalt membrane is applied using a calibrated distributor truck. See Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance” for a discussion of the proper operation of the distributor. Selection of the proper type and application rate, along with a uniform distribution of the membrane, is essential to the performance of the inter- layer and thus the overlay. The manufacturer’s recommendations should be carefully followed regarding the type and amount of asphalt that should be applied with a specific geotextile.

Special care must be taken during application of the membrane in the overlap zone when two passes of a distributor are used to obtain cover- age over the entire width of the geotextile being placed. The membrane should be placed over a sufficient length such that the distributor can place a uniform quantity. Care should be taken to avoid placing the membrane too far ahead of the paving process. Small, variable-width areas should be applied with the hand spray boom on the back of the distributor to ensure proper coverage. Before placing the membrane, the surface should be swept to remove debris. If weather or equipment breakdown cause paving operations to be suspended, the surface should not be opened to traffic because of safety concerns. Uncovered geotextile can have very low skid resistance.

Paving Nembranes

Several manufacturers produce a composite membrane that consists of a paving geotextile saturated with asphalt and coated with a rubber- ized asphalt adhesive. The top surface is saturated with asphalt cement. Beneath the geotextile is a second layer—rubberized asphalt adhesive. The adhesive is protected by a release sheet that is removed just before placement. The paving membranes are basically a peel-and-stick type of system. It may be necessary to apply a primer to improve adhesion before placement of the membrane. This step is needed whenever problems with adhesion may develop, such as on a surface that is wet or dusty, where temperatures are cool, or on an irregular surface (for example, a milled surface). The prime coat should always be used before installing a pav- ing membrane on a bridge deck or when the paving membrane will be exposed to moisture combined with traffic before placement of the over- lay. Before a paving membrane is installed, the manufacturer should be consulted for proper installation procedures.

Strip interlayers

Strip interlayers (Figure 13.5) are membrane materials designed to repair localized pavement cracking and pavement joints. As with surface-wide interlayers, these materials absorb and dissipate movement (strain) in the pavement, reduce reflection cracking, and minimize water infiltration. Strip interlayers usually consist of modified asphalt sandwiched between non-woven paving geotextile and high-modulus woven geotextile. The non-woven geotextile layer ensures a strong bond with the pavement sur- face. The center modified-asphalt layer provides stress relief and resistance to water infiltration. The top woven geotextile adds modulus and strength to resist cracking in the overlay. Strip interlayers come in 12- to 20-inch rolls that are 65 feet long.

Chip Seals

Chips seals are used as interlayers to help seal the underlying pavement and help bond the overlay to it, as well as a means to delay reflection cracking. These applications range from “conventional” single chip seals to multiple course treatments that have a similar effect as an open-graded crack relief layer in delaying reflection cracking. A chip seal may be placed on an existing pavement or on a milled surface. Placement procedures are described in Chapter 12, “Asphalt in Pavement Maintenance”.

Stress-Absorbing Membrane

An asphalt-rubber stress-absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) is a chip seal that consists of a heavy, hot-applied [G25-425°F) (162-218°C)] crumb rubber (Figure 13.6) modified asphalt binder membrane, into which a relatively large cover aggregate is imbedded. The SAMI is very flexible and has a low modulus, allowing it to dampen movement at underlying cracks, reducing tensile stress in the HMA overlay. The membrane will also provide a waterproofing layer to reduce moisture intrusion into the underlying layers.

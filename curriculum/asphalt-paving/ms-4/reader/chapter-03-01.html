3.1. Aggregates for Hot ooo Mix Asphalt (HMA)

3.1.1 Introduction

The amount of aggregate in asphalt paving mixtures is generally 90-95 percent by weight or 75-85 percent by volume. Aggregate is primarily respon- sible for the load-supporting capacity of pavement; accordingly, performance of a hot mix asphalt (HMA) mixture is heavily influenced by the aggregate.

The general term aggregate refers to any hard, inert mineral material used for mixing in graduated particles or fragments. ASTM D 8 defines aggregate as “a granular material of mineral composition such as sand, gravel, shell, slag, or crushed stone, used with a cementing medium to form mortars or concrete or alone as in base courses, railroad ballasts, etc.”

Aggregates can be either natural or manufactured. Natural aggregates are generally fragments extracted and processed from rock formations or rock deposits. Manufactured aggregates are derived from raw materi- als that have been physically or chemically changed or from industrial by-products that have been processed.

3.1.2 Origin of Rocks

The purpose of this section is not to describe in detail the origin of bed- rock, from which most aggregate material is derived. A brief discussion. is in order, however, because knowledge of the origin of rock is important for understanding the production and performance of mineral aggregates.

Rocks are divided into three general classifications: sedimentary, igneous, and metamorphic. These classifications describe how rocks are formed. Table 3.1 is a breakout of aggregate types and families for these three classifications. Table 3.2 shows some typical properties of four of the more common aggregate families.

Sedimentary Rocks

Sedimentary rocks are formed by the accumulation of sediment deposited by water or wind. With sufficient overburden, the sedimentation re-solidifies more or less to solid rock. The sediment may consist of rock or mineral frag- ments or particles of various sizes (conglomerate, sandstone, shale), the remains or products of animals or plants (certain limestone and coal), the product of chemical action or evaporation (salt, gypsum), or mixtures of these materi- als. Some sedimentary deposits consist of particles blown from volcanoes and deposited on land or in water. A characteristic feature of sedimentary depos- its is a layered structure. This stratification is a direct result of the method of formation—the material having been deposited over some lake or sea bottom.

igneous Rock

Igneous rocks are formed by the cooling and solidification of hot molten materials (magma) that erupt from or are trapped beneath the earth’s crust. These rocks are further classified as extrusive or intrusive rocks. Extrusive rocks are formed by material that has poured out on the earth’s surface, such as that produced by volcanic action, and are distinguished in general by their glass-like structure (rhyolite, andesite, basalt, etc.). Intrusive rocks result from cooling and solidification at great depths within the earth’s crust. They are wholly crystalline in texture (granite, diorite, gabbro, etc.). Although originally formed beneath the earth’s surface, they are often exposed today as a result of earth movements and erosion processes.

Metamorphic Rocks

Metamorphic rocks are generally formed by the modification of sedimentary or igneous rocks because of intense pressures caused by movement within the earth’s crust and by excessive heat and solutions. Factors causing such modifications are complex, and the original form of the altered rock is often difficult to determine. One distinguishing feature of metamorphic rocks is the parallel planes in which the minerals that form the rocks are arranged. This arrangement is called foliation. These planes are zones of weakness along which the rocks may be split more easily than in other directions. Examples of foliated rocks are gneisses and schists (altered from igneous material), and slate (altered from shale). Marble (altered from limestone) and quartzite (altered from sandstone) are common types of metamorphic rocks having a massive texture, usually without foliation.

3.1.3 Sources of Aggregates

Aggregates for asphalt paving are generally classified according to their source and means of preparation. Figure 3.1 shows the sources of aggregates throughout the United States. They include pit or bank- run aggregates, quarried or crushed stone, and synthetic and lightweight aggregates.

Pit or Bank-Run Materials

Gravel and sand are natural aggregates existing in loose or unconsoli- dated alluvial deposits and are typically pit or bank-run material extracted without drilling and blasting. Gravel is usually defined as aggregate par- ticles ranging in size from 3 inches (75 millimeters) to No. 4 (4.75 millime- ters). Sand consists of particles ranging in size from No. 4 (4.75 millimeters) to No. 200 (75 microns). Sizes No. 200 and smaller include the silt fraction. Many pit or bank-run alluvial or glacial sources also contain cobbles with aggregate particles as large as 10 inches (254 millimeters) or more.

Exposed rocks are eroded and degraded by many processes of nature, both physical and chemical. The products of the degradation processes are usually moved by wind, water, or moving ice, and deposited as a soil mineral in various landforms.

Gravels are widely distributed, but deposits are rarely found without some proportion of sand and possibly silt. Sandy soil mixtures frequently contain some clay and silt. Beach sands, some of which are now far inland, are a uniform-size material, but river sands often contain large amounts of gravel, silt, and clay.

Transporting agents also influence the particle shape. Rounded boulders and pebbles included in deposits in glaciated areas are one example. Another is the smooth, rounded gravel and sand particles in flowing streams.

Pit or bank-run material is usually screened to proper size, separating sand from gravel and even further separating gravel into different sizes. The larger particles of natural gravel are often crushed to make them more suitable for asphalt paving mixtures. Crushing typically improves the particle shape by making the rounded particles more angular; it also improves the distribution and range of particle sizes. Many times, pit or bank-run material must also be washed to remove dirt (excessive amounts of silt and clay) before using it for asphalt paving.

Synthetic and Lightweight Aggregates

Aggregates resulting from the modification of materials, which may involve both physical and chemical changes, are sometimes called synthetic, manufactured, or artificial aggregates. They may be industrial by-products or the result of a specially produced or processed raw material for ultimate use as aggregates. Typically, synthetic aggregates are lightweight and have

excellent resistance to wear. Historically, their preferred use has been in bridge-deck or roof-deck paving and in surface layers of asphalt pavements where a high degree of skid resistance is required.

Slag is a by-product of the iron- and steel-making processes. Slag that is coproduced with iron in blast furnaces is known as Blast Furnace Slag (BFS). Slag that is generated during the production of steel in basic oxy- gen furnaces or electric arc furnaces is called steel furnace slag or steel slag. For aggregates used in asphalt paving applications, both types of slag are processed by the air-cooled method in which the molten slag is placed into beds and cooled slowly under atmospheric conditions. The hardened, crystalline material is subsequently crushed and screened.

Expanded Shale, Clay, and Slate (ESCS) are often called structural light- weight aggregates. These aggregates are derived from the intense heat treatment of the naturally occurring materials of shale, clay, and slate. The raw materials are subjected to temperatures that exceed 1800-2100°F (980-1150°C) in a rotary kiln, which causes them to expand. These aggre- gates have a rough texture and are angular. They have a strong affinity for asphalt cement and provide good skid resistance in asphalt paving surfaces. Because of their insulating qualities, underlying subgrades of pavements that contain lightweight aggregates show some evidence of reduced frost depths. Because these aggregates may have densities as low as one-half of conventional aggregates, adjustments are needed in mix-design calcula- tions to accommodate for differences in specific gravity when these aggre- gates are used in mixes in combination with conventional aggregates.

Many synthetic aggregates are made from reclaimed materials. Some have been used for some time but on a limited basis, while others are in developmental stages. Reclaimed materials used in these synthetic aggre- gates include recycled plastic and glass, processed sludge and sediment, and by-products from coal combustion.

Recycled glass has been used as an aggregate substitute in HMA paving. The recycled glass is crushed and screened, yielding fine aggregate—typically minus No. 4 sieve (4.75 millimeters) aggregate particles. Coarse glass particles typically contain flat and elongated particles, which need to be avoided. The quantity of flat and elongated particles depends on the crushing equipment and the amount of crushing to which the glass is subjected. Specific grav- ity of the material is 2.0-2.5, and absorption is obviously insignificant. When recycled glass is used in HMA, glass processing must include the removal of ferrous and nonferrous metal, plastic, and paper. The term glasphalt has at times been used to describe pavements containing recycled glass.

There has been evidence in the past of poor bonding with asphalt binder that has resulted in stripping and raveling.

Two other waste materials that have been used as aggregate substitutes in HMA mixtures are ground rubber and roofing shingles. These materials are discussed in Section 3.4, Aggregate Additives.

3.1.4 Aggregate Production

The overall objective in aggregate processing is to economically pro- duce aggregates that are appropriate for the end use. Particularly for the structural and functional needs of the more demanding asphalt paving mixtures, aggregates must have particular particle shape, surface texture, and grading or particle-size distribution characteristics in addition to the inherent strength and durability characteristics.

Extraction

The first stage in the production of aggregates is the extraction or min- ing of the source material from its deposited condition. The material must be accessed through an open or “working” face of the deposit. Many times, overburden (unwanted or unusable material) that lies above the aggre- gate deposit must be removed or stripped before the working face can be established. In cases where overburden is primarily soil, removal is typically accomplished with common earth-moving equipment such as scrapers. Removal of overburden containing more highly consolidated soil and rock fragments may require bulldozers, front-end loaders, and haul trucks.

In their natural state in a dry pit, gravel and sand are typically in a rela- tively unconsolidated condition. For this condition, mining of the working face is usually accomplished with front-end loaders or power shovels. For pits with larger and taller working faces, a bulldozer is often employed to push the material down the face to then allow for the use of loaders or shovels. The extracted material is then loaded into haul trucks or placed on a conveyor for transportation to the processing plant for screening, crushing, and washing. ;

Some deposits of sand and gravel lie beneath bodies of water, such as rivers and lakes. Extraction of these materials usually requires dredges, draglines, or large suction pumps.

Quarry stone is commonly extracted by drilling and blasting the rock formation. Mechanical extraction tools, such as rock hammers for further rock fragmentation after blasting, are sometimes necessary. Equipment and methodology for handling and transporting the rock fragments or shot rock is essentially the same as that for gravel and sand.

Crushing

Most HMA mixtures use a maximum aggregate size of 1% inches (37.5 millimeters) in diameter or less. In addition, they require some minimum level of aggregate surface roughness and shape for developing the neces- sary aggregate structure to resist pavement loads and deformation. Hence, crushing of the aggregate is usually necessary to further reduce gravel and rock fragments to proper particle sizes and to create appropriate shape and surface characteristics.

In impact crush- ers, rock particles are impacted against other rock particles and against surfaces inside the crusher at high speeds. The rock particles shatter in impact crushers.

The amount of reduction in particle size, the shape of the aggregate par- ticles, and the amount of fines or minus No. 4 (4.75 millimeters) material vary with the different sizes and kinds of crushers, as well as with the rock type and rate at which the rock is fed into the crusher. In order to yield the most appropriate end products, multiple crushing stages with optimized crushing equipment for each particular stage coupled with appropriate screening sequences are common.

Screening

The screening operation is the part of the processing procedure in which aggregates are separated according to size. Material may be passed through single-, double-, or even triple-screening applications in order to make the various needed sizes of aggregate. Before or during screening applications, material can be routed to crushing operations. Additionally, material can be conveyed to washing operations before, during, or after screening applications.

Several types of equipment are used for screening, but the most com- mon is the vibrating inclined screen. This equipment allows the slope, vibration speed, stroke, and direction of rotation of the screen surface to be adjusted as needed. Typically, units with multiple-deck screens are used in production facilities. Screen surfaces are available in various types and materials and with a variety of sizes and shapes of screen openings.

Washing

Aggregates may need to be washed during production to remove dust generated during crushing. Depending on the nature of sand and gravel deposits, washing may also be needed to remove silts, clays, and other deleterious substances such as coal and soft or lightweight fragments.

The various types of washing equipment include high-pressure spray nozzles, screw washers, log washers, and rotary scrubbers. Sprayers mounted above the screens might adequately rinse some unwanted mate- rials, such as dust, from aggregates. Other materials, such as more highly plastic clays and cemented aggregates, may need the rigorous agitation of a log washer.

Handling and Stockpiling

The handling and stockpiling of aggregates is an integral component of aggregate processing for producing a quality product. The methods used for the pickup or loading of material by shovels and loader buckets should avoid segregation. Feed bins, chutes, gates, and conveyors should be sized and operated in such a way that a proper and consistent rate of feed is maintained to crushers, screens, and washers.

Each stage of conveying and storing aggregates must minimize contamination, degradation, and segregation. Degradation can mean not only the breakdown of aggregates into smaller pieces but also the creation of dust. Excessive dropping of aggregates from conveyors, pushing of aggre- gates with bulldozers, handling of aggregates by front-end loaders, and buildup of stockpiles to excessive heights all contribute to degradation.

Segregation is the separation of larger particles from smaller particles, leaving a nonhomogenous and unrepresentative blend of particle sizes. Because movement and vibration cause segregation, every stage of han- dling and processing can contribute to it. On conveyor belts and in haul trucks, smaller particles migrate downward. More segregation takes place at the conveyor end and as the material is dumped from the truckbed. In these cases, larger particles flow away from the belt or the truckbed and the finer particles drop more vertically and under the belt or truckbed (see Figure 3.3). The worst segregation occurs in single-cone stockpiles where material is dropped from a tall, stationary conveyor belt to build the stockpile. Segregation can be controlled with better stockpiling tech- niques, such as using radial stackers. This equipment allows the formation of a continuous layered stockpile. Each layer will have segregation, but it occurs in a more finite volume because load-out of the multiple layers negates the individual-layer segregation. Another way to control or reduce segregation is to screen aggregates into a greater number of fractions with fewer particle sizes in each fraction.

Sampling

Material sampling is a key component of aggregate quality-control, yet so often it is overlooked or taken for granted. It is imperative that samples be obtained in such a manner that they are representative of the aggregate product. Too often, samples are manually taken from the faces of coarse- aggregate stockpiles where, due to the common segregation that occurs in the building of stockpiles, the samples are not representative of the whole pile but are assumed to be. In actuality, they represent only a small volume of aggregate in the particular sample locations.

The methodologies of AASHTO T 2 (ASTM D 75) should be followed when sampling aggregates. Typically, the best sampling locations are wherever the full material flow-stream can be sampled, such as from discharge streams, from bins or conveyor belt ends, or from the full cross section of conveyor-belt surfaces. If sampling must be done from stockpiles, the procedures detailed in the appendix of AASHTO T 2 should be followed.

Maximum Particle Size and Grading

Two key properties of an aggregate that go hand in hand are maximum particle size and grading. ASTM D 8 defines the maximum size of an aggregate as “the smallest sieve opening through which the entire amount of aggregate is required to pass.” AASHTO MP 323 refers to the maximum size of an aggregate for Superpave mixes only as “one size larger than the nominal maximum aggregate size.”

AASHTO MP 323 defines it as “one sieve size larger than the first sieve to retain more than 10 percent of the material.” Care must be exercised when using and referring to the terms. For good paving results, the lift thickness of HMA should be at least 2 to 2% times the maximum aggregate size in the mix or 3 to 4 times the nominal maximum aggregate size in the mix.

The grading or gradation of an aggregate is the distribution of the vari- ous particle sizes. Gradation is typically determined by both a washed sieve analysis and a dry sieve analysis. ASTM C 136 (AASHTO T 27) details the procedure for dry sieving.

In this procedure, the mass of a sample of dry aggregate is determined and then the sample is passed through a series of sieves with progressively smaller openings (see Figure 3.4). Once separated, the mass of material retained on each sieve is measured and compared with the total sample weight. The aggregate gradation is ultimately expressed as the cumulative percentage passing each sieve size. Figure 3.5 gives an example of sieve analysis data for an aggregate that contains both fine and coarse particles. A graphical plot of the particle distribution is also shown.

Dry sieving alone may be satisfactory for general checks during aggregate production but does not appropriately determine the amount of fine particles smaller than the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. Therefore, before performing the dry sieve analysis, a washed sieve analysis should be made as detailed in AASHTO T 11 (ASTM C 117). They describe the procedure in this way: “A sample of the aggregate is washed in a prescribed manner, using either plain water or water containing a wetting agent, as specified. The decanted wash water, containing suspended and dissolved material, is passed through a 75 microns (No. 200) sieve. The loss in mass resulting from the wash treat- ment is calculated as mass percent of the original sample. . . .”

Particle Shape and Surface Texture

Particle shape refers to the form and contour of the individual aggregate particles (angular versus round, cubic versus flat or elongated). Surface texture refers to the extent of roughness of the surface of the aggregate particles.

Although two separate characteristics—particle shape and surface texture of aggregates—are often considered collectively in their roles in HMA workability, compaction, and deformation resistance (see Figure 3.6), both fine- and coarse-size fractions of crushed stone and gravel have irregular or angular shapes as well as rougher surface textures. Natural Cuncrushed) gravels and sands have generally rounded shapes and smoother textures.

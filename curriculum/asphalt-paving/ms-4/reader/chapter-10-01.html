10.1 General

Long-term performance of hot mix asphalt GAMA) pavements is achieved by using quality materials and placement techniques during construction. The life of a pavement and the cost of maintenance can be very sensitive to seemingly minor variations in materials properties, such as aggregate gradation, asphalt content, mixture volumetrics, and compaction.

Highway agencies and other owners of asphalt pavements use physi- cal test results to evaluate the quality of materials and the methods used in their projects. Each agency’s overall quality-assurance program deter- mines testing requirements. These programs generally include three types of tests: quality-control, acceptance, and independent assurance.

10.1.1 Quality-Assurance Programs

The definitions used in this chapter have been adapted from the TRB Transportation Circular, Glossary of Highway Quality Assurance Terms. The following terms are presented relative to quality-assurance programs. This chapter focuses mainly on these terms as they relate to sampling and testing, and is limited to aggregates and HMA mixtures.

Quality Assurance. All planned and systematic actions necessary to provide confidence that a product or facility will perform satisfactorily in service. :

Quality Control (also called Process Control). Actions and consider- ations necessary to assess and adjust production and construction pro- cesses in order to control the level of quality being produced in the product. Quality-control practices are usually provided by contractors and/or mate- rials suppliers to monitor the product manufacturing process.

Acceptance. Sampling and testing, or inspection, to determine the degree of compliance with contract requirements. Acceptance practices are usually provided by the owner or his designated representative to determine whether specific terms of the plans and specifications have been met.

Independent Assurance. Management tool that requires a third party, not directly responsible for process control or acceptance, to provide an independent assessment of the product and/or the reliability of test results obtained from the process-control and acceptance testing. This third-party testing and inspection is usually provided by the owner but accomplished by a laboratory that is not involved in the quality-control or acceptance.

10.1.2 Job-Mix Formula

For quality-control and acceptance of hot mix asphalt, specification lim- its are often based on a job-mix formula (JMF) established as part of the mixture design. The mix design will produce a JMF that establishes the tar- get values for production of the HMA for the project. The JMF is the com- bination of aggregate and asphalt binder materials proposed for use on a project that, when tested using the required mix design procedures, yields results that meet all the established design criteria. At a minimum, the JMF should include the following target parameters from the mix design:

° Asphalt content

° Aggregate gradation

° Laboratory air voids

° Stockpile blend percentages

° Hot-bin blend percentages (for batch plants)

° Other parameters specified for quality-control such as mixture volu-

metrics or aggregate angularity

* Specific materials used (that is, sources for asphalt cement, admixtures,

ageregate descriptions, etc.)

The JMF that results from the mix design becomes the “recipe” for producing that mixture in the hot plant. Specifications often dictate how closely the HMA supplier must adhere to this recipe.

The specifications may require, for example, that the binder content of the material produced shall not vary by more than 0.4 percent from the binder content in the JMF (established in the approved mix design). The concept is that the mix design provides evidence that a mixture made with given aggregates, at a certain binder content and gradation, and com- pacted with a specific amount of energy will comply with specific quality indicator tests. These indicators can include parameters such as aggregate absorption, tensile strength ratio (TSR), volumetric properties, and strength parameters such as Marshall stability. The premise is that if the mixture is controlled to be reasonably compliant with the fundamental mix-design properties (that is, gradation, binder content, and air voids), then the mix- ture produced will essentially comply with the quality properties (TSR, Stability, and VMA) established in the mix design. This assumption allows fewer tests for quality-control and acceptance than were required in the mix design. The assumption also requires a revised mix design if any of the quality-control or acceptance parameters change significantly.

More information on mix designs and their procedures can be found in Chapter 4, Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design or in Asphalt Institute’s publica- tions MS-2, Mix Design Methods for HMA and Other Hot-Mix Types or SP-2, Superpave Mix Design.

10.2 Sampling Methods

The methods used for sampling HMA can have a significant effect on the outcome of the test results. It is critical that good sampling methods are used and that the specified procedures are carefully followed to get an accurate representation of the materials being produced.

10.2.1 Representative Sampling

The primary goal is to obtain samples that are representative of the products being evaluated. Since test results are used to adjust asphalt mixing plants, it would be counterproductive to overall quality to obtain a sample from a particularly “bad” or a particularly “good” portion of the work. Consider as an example an asphalt plant quality-control sample taken from a nonrepresentative portion of the asphalt mixture with an unusually low binder content. The proper response from the plant opera- tor to a low asphalt content test result would likely be to increase the amount of asphalt binder being incorporated into the mix. After such an adjustment, he would inadvertently have the asphalt plant set too high for asphalt binder content.

Representative samples of the average production are critical for the effective use of quality-control and acceptance samples. Inspection is more appropriate for identifying non-uniform portions of the work. Once inspection has identified a portion of the work that is non-uniform (dif- ferent from the rest of the work), or for which defective materials are sus- pected, additional samples representing that specific element of the work should be taken. Test results from such samples should never be used as part of the lot averages, and certainly not for lot acceptance. They only represent the specific problem areas identified by inspection.

As an example, consider an asphalt pavement that contains a 50-foot (15.2 meters) length of roadway that is littered with manholes and valve covers that do not allow the regular compaction equipment into that iso- lated area. By visually observing the entire pavement, an inspector or technician would be able to recognize that the isolated area will not have density values that are representative of the pavement compaction for that day’s placement. Density tests taken there would represent that area only.

Regardless of the methods being employed, good samples will be obtained if a common-sense approach is combined with proper sampling technique. Visual observations of the entire element to be sampled can be valuable in selecting sample locations or number of sample collection points, and in verifying that representative samples can be retrieved.

It is also important to get a sufficient quantity of material when sampling. The sample size is important not only to obtain enough mate- rial to complete the tests but also to achieve a representative sample. As an exaggerated example, consider a graded gravel material that contains 15 percent of rock that is retained on the #4-inch (19 millimeters) screen and passes the l-inch (25 millimeters) screen. Each of these +%-inch (19 millimeters) rocks weigh about 250 grams. Assume that a sample size of only 500 grams is obtained. If the sample taken happens to contain one large rock, then the gradation test will yield 50 percent retained on the 34-inch (19 millimeters) sieve. If the 500-gram sample scoop does not happen to capture a large rock, then the gradation test would yield 0 percent of that size. In reality, the gradation is somewhere in between 0 percent and 50 percent. As the sample size increases, the test result is no longer impacted by the size of the sample—it is only impacted by the true gradation of the materials being tested. This is the point that establishes minimum sample sizes. Typical minimum sample sizes for aggregates and for HMA are presented in Table 10.2.

Production Lots

The quantity of HMA produced on a given project is usually divided into defined portions called “lots.” Quality assurance and quality-control plans usually require samples to be taken at defined intervals within these lots. The intervals are generally measured in any of the following two ways.

1. Time intervals. Typical lot sizes when sampling by time interval would include either a half day’s production, or a full day’s production.

2. Quantity intervals. Typical lot sizes when sampling by quan- | tity interval would include 5,000 or 10,000 tons (4,536 to 9,071 tonnes), depending on anticipated production rates. Lots based on linear feet (meters) of paving have also been used.

Lot sizes are normally established by project specifications. It is important to have lot sizes defined in advance of construction. The size and method of measurement of a lot will be used to determine locations and quantities of quality-control and quality assurance tests. The lot will also be used to define what portion of the work is represented by a given set of test results. The limits of the lot are critical if corrective action or removal is required.

10.2.2 Random Sampling

Use of random sampling techniques is an important part of obtaining un- biased representative samples. Random sampling methods can be simple to use if good forms and/or other tools are used. With widespread availabil- ity of personal computers, simple spreadsheet documents can be used to easily generate sample locations and make implementation less daunting.

Random sampling can be applied to any of the sampling interval methods (time, quantity, and length). Random sampling is achieved by taking the number of samples to be taken that day and distributing the times, quantities, or locations selected for each sample in a random way. For example, assume that the production of 5,000 tons (4,536 tonnes) of material is planned and a sample frequency for every 1,000 tons (907 tonnes) has been selected. Nonrandom techniques may lead planners to take the first sample at about 1,000 tons (907 tonnes) and the second sample at about 2,000 tons (1,814 tonnes) and so on until all five of the samples for that day are collected. Using random sampling, a random number (numbers between 0 and 1 obtained from a chart) would be assigned to each of the five samples. Each of the random numbers is multiplied by the total ton- nage (5,000 or 4,536) to determine at what point in production to take the sample. For example, if the random number for one of the samples is 0.43218, then that sample would be taken when the day’s accumula- tive tonnage reaches 2,160 tons or 1,959 tons (5,000 X 0.43218 = 2,160) (4,536 = 0.43218 X 1,960).

An accurate estimate of the day’s production is needed to determine random sample locations. In some cases, lots with different sizes may have a different number of tests. For example, if the project estimate for produc- tion is 6,520 feet (1,978 meters), and the quality-control plan establishes the sample frequency for coring at every 1,000 feet (305 meters), then that lot will contain seven core locations.

Table 10.3 shows how to calculate the stations for random locations for cores. Another set of random numbers would need to be used to determine how far right of the centerline the location would be. So, as an example, the final location of a given core location could be “Station 15 + 83 (0 + 482 meters), and 7 feet (2.1 meters) right of centerline.”

Windrow Sampling

Windrow sampling (Figure 10.6) techniques are generally used for aggregates, but they have also been used to obtain samples of HMA. Wind- row samples can be used if “belly dump” haul units are used. These units will create a windrow of HMA that can be sampled before placement into the paving machine. In-Place Pavement Sampling

Representative portions of HMA can be obtained by sampling behind the paving machine. These samples are taken immediately after placement by the paving machine but before any rolling. The use of plates (Figure 10.7) placed on the grade in front of the paver is recommended except when thin lifts are being placed.

Core Sampling Core sampling is the most common form of sampling from completed HMA pavements (Figure 10.8). Portable electric drills with wet-cut diamond-studded core barrels provide rapid removal with little or no damage to the specimens. These samples are normally used to evaluate compaction and thickness. Once removed from the pavement, the test specimens must be handled with care because they are very sensitive to damage from heat and point loads.

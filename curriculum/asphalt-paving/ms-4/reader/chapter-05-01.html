Classification Systems

5.1.4 Introduction

The performance of an HMA pavement is directly related to understanding the soils upon which it is built. The strength of both the untreated base course and the subgrade soil are directly related to the moisture content; therefore, the drainage of the pavement is extremely important.

Likewise, drainage is also an important aspect in asphalt pavement design and construction. The latter part of this chapter reviews some key issues of pavement-related drainage.

If not thoroughly knowledgeable about soil mechanics and the effect of different soils and their drainage, the reader is encouraged to consult a qualified geotechnical engineer for further guidance.

5.1.2 Objectives for Classifying Subgrade

The objective behind using any soil classification system for road and airport construction is the ability to predict performance of a given soil subgrade on the basis of a few simple tests. These test results, along with the soil classification procedure and criteria, allow an engineer to identify the soil type and characterize it according to other soils with similar char- acteristics and properties.

Grouping soils into a classification system is not the final objective, but it should be regarded as a tool to understand soil action and behavior as they relate to subgrade performance.

5.1.3 Classification Systems

The two classification systems commonly used to describe soils used for pavement construction are the American Association of State High- way and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) Classification System for Soils and the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS). Both systems are described in detail in many soil mechanics textbooks. The reader is referred to An Introduction to Geotechnical Engineering, by R. D. Holtz and W. D. Kovacs.

The principal soil classification system used in the United States by highway engineers is the AASHTO system. This system was originally developed by the U.S. Bureau of Public Roads in about 1928. The present AASHTO system, which dates from 1945, was the result of an extensive revision by the Highway Research Board.

Under the AASHTO system, soils having approximately the same general load-carrying capacity and service characteristics are grouped together to form one of seven basic groups (A-1, A-2, A-3, A-4, A-5, A-6, and A-7). In general, the best highway subgrade soils are classified as A-1. Poorer soils are rated in numerical order, with the A-7 group being the poorest. The exception is the A-3 group. Soils classified A-3 are better subgrades than soils in the A-2 group. From this, it can generally be assumed for a given set of conditions that the thickness of the pavement section must progres- sively increase as the soil classification group increases from A-1 to A-7, except as noted.

The USCS is based on the Airfield Classification System developed by Professor Arthur Casagrande of Harvard University during World War I. In 1952, Casagrande, together with the Bureau of Reclamation and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, modified the system for a broad range of applications, including dams and foundations.

The USCS groups soils based on grain size and plasticity. The system is rooted in the concept that coarse-grained soils can be classified accord- ing to grain-size distribution, while it is more appropriate to classify fine- grained soils based on plasticity characteristics. These characteristics are evaluated by plotting the plasticity index (PD versus the liquid limit (LL) on a standard plasticity chart. The position of the plotted points yields information for predicting soil behavior as an engineering construction material. The following properties form the basis of soil identification:

5.2 Methods for Evaluating

Subgrade Strength and Stiffness

5.2.1 Introduction

Thickness design of asphalt pavements depends to a large extent on the strength and/or stiffness of the finished subgrade. Common empirical methods of thickness design depend on a subgrade strength index such as the CBR or R-value. Mechanistic-empirical methods use the subgrade resilient modulus, a measure of material stiffness, to establish pavement thickness. In either method, characterization of the subgrade strength or stiffness is a fundamental part of thickness design (for further information, see Chapter 6, “Thickness Design”).

The concept of testing soils at their anticipated moisture and density should be the governing criterion for thickness design.

Subgrade strength or stiffness of a soil or aggregate base or subbase depends on soil type and is closely related to density and moisture con- tent. Variable strength or stiffness can be expected, depending on the degree of compaction and moisture content after the pavement has been completed. If experience has indicated that a certain soil cannot be com- pacted economically to high density, it would be unwise to perform design tests with the soil at a high-density value.

Design strength or stiffness should be based on values obtained from soil samples in their worst anticipated condition. To obtain such values generally involves testing a sample that has been soaked in water.

Design methods used by different agencies vary widely, and their approach to evaluating the strength or modulus of the unbound materials for design purposes also varies. Evaluation methods discussed here are probahly the most widely used methods for asphalt pavement design for airfields and highways.

Both field and laboratory test methods have been established by vari- ous agencies over the years. Each has advantages and disadvantages that must be considered. Field-test methods have the advantage of testing the unbound layers (base and subgrade) in situ at the confining pressure and moisture content that exist at the site. This may also be a disadvantage in that the existing conditions may not represent the intended conditions after construction. For example, if a pavement is to be built over a satu- rated soil, a field test would provide a strength index for the soil in a saturated condition; however, drainage improvements to the site will likely change the subgrade moisture content after construction.

Laboratory tests may be advantageous because the unbound layers are tested under a wide range of confining pressure and moisture conditions,

5.2.2 Laboratory Test Methods

The CBR, with its numerous variations, is probably the most widely used strength index for designing asphalt pavement structures. This method was first developed by the California Division of Highways in the 1920s. It was modified by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for use on airfields during World War Il and is still widely used in airfield pavement design and for highway and road- way design outside the United States. While primarily a laboratory test, field CBR test methods have been developed for use in evaluating soils in situ.

The Resistance Value (R-Value) test method for evaluating treated and untreated materials for bases, subbases, and subgrades was developed by F. N. Hveem and R. M. Carmany of the California Division of Highways.

The Resilient Modulus (M) method was developed by the Asphalt Insti- tute based on a test originated at the University of California by H. B. Seed, C. K. Chan, and C. E. Lee. This laboratory test is used to determine the stiff- ness of a soil specimen for conditions that represent stress in pavements subjected to moving wheel loads.

The CBR is a comparative measure of the shearing resistance of soil. The test consists of measuring the load required to cause a piston of standard size to penetrate a soil specimen at a specified rate. This load is divided by the load required to force the piston to the same depth in a standard sample of crushed stone. The result, multiplied by 100, is the value of the California CBR. Usually depths of 0.1 or 0.2 inch (2.5 or 5 millimeters) are used, but depths of 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 inch (7.5, 10, and 12.5 millimeters) may be used if desired. Penetration loads for the crushed stone have been standardized. This test method is intended to provide the relative bearing value, or CBR, of base, subbase, and subgrade materials. Procedures are given for laboratory-compacted swelling, nonswelling, and granular mate- rials. These tests are usually performed to obtain information that will be used for design purposes.

The CBR value for a soil will depend upon its density, molding moisture content, and moisture content after soaking. Because the product of labo- ratory compaction should closely represent the results of field compac- tion, the first two of these variables must be carefully controlled during the preparation of laboratory samples for testing. Unless it can be ascertained that the soil being tested will not accumulate moisture and be affected by it in the field after construction, the CBR tests should be performed on soaked samples.

Certain routine soil tests should be performed before conducting the CBR test. These tests are shown in Table 5.2.

Test Procedure

The R-value test (ASTM D 2844) is made on a relatively small sample, and it is necessary to remove all large material. If 75 percent or more of the material passes the 34-inch (19.0 millimeters) sieve, the material retained on that sieve is removed. If less than 75 percent of the material passes the 34-inch (19.0 millimeters) sieve, then all material retained on the 1-inch (25.0 millimeters) sieve is removed. The soil is then separated over the No. 4 (4.75 millimeters) sieve but recombined in the proper proportions with the coarse sizes to obtain 1200-gram (2.65 pounds) samples plus an extra 200 grams (0.44 pounds) for moisture content determinations.

In each R-value test, four test briquettes at different moisture contents are prepared. Each briquette has one-half to two-thirds the amount of water nec- essary to saturate the sample or exude moisture between 100 and 800 pounds per square inch (689 and 5516 kiloPascals). After mixing, loose material is allowed to cure overnight in a covered container. Then more water is added and mixed until the estimated saturation moisture is attained. The first bri- quette is used as a pilot specimen to obtain specimen heights of 2.5£0.1 inches (63.52.5 millimeters) and to have exudation pressures both above and below 300 pounds per square inch (2068 kiloPascals).

Specimens are compacted by a kneading compactor, which is the same as that used for the Hveem method of asphalt mix design. The compaction procedure provides a kneading action intended to give soil particles the same orientation and contact pressure obtained during field compaction.

The prepared soil sample is placed in the compactor feeding trough with the material distributed evenly along the full length. A 4-inch (101.6 milli- meters) diameter by 5-inch (127 millimeters) tall mold is fitted into a special holder for placing in the compactor. Material is deposited in the mold by a special procedure while the kneading compactor is operating and the foot pressure is set at 250 pounds per square inch (1724 kiloPascals).

When all material has been placed in the mold and 10 extra blows have been added to level and seat the material, the sample is compacted by using 100 blows with a foot pressure of 350 pounds per square inch (2413 kiloPascals).

Resilient Modulus (M_)

General Theory

The resilient modulus (M) is used to characterize subgrade soils and unbound base/subbase materials. The resilient modulus test provides a material stiffness that more closely simulates the behavior of the material under a moving wheel. It can be used in the mechanistic analysis of mul- tilayered systems for predicting roughness, cracking, rutting, etc. This test covers the determination of resilient modulus of untreated fine-grained soils for conditions that represent stress in pavements subjected to mov- ing wheel loads. The results of the test describe the resilient character of a soil specimen when a repeated axial deviator stress of fixed magnitude, frequency, and load duration is applied while the specimen, in a triaxial chamber, is surrounded by pressure that acts as a static, all-around stress.

Test Procedure

Resilient modulus testing of unbound materials is performed in accor- dance with one of two current procedures: (AASHTO T 307), Determining the Resilient Modulus of Soils and Aggregate Materials or (AASHTO T 292), Resilient Modulus of Subgrade Soils and Untreated Base/Subbase Materials. A triaxial cell suitable for use in resilience testing of soils is shown in Figure 5.5. The equipment is similar to most standard cells, except that it is somewhat larger to facilitate the internally mounted load and defor- mation measuring equipment, and has additional outlets for the electrical leads from the measuring devices. (Triaxial cells with suitable externally mounted load and deformation measuring equipment also may be used.) For the type of equipment shown, air is used as the cell fluid.

The deformation measuring equipment consists of Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDTs) attached to the soil specimen by a pair of clamps. Two LVDTs are used for the measurement of axial deformation. The clamps and LVDTs are shown in position on a soil specimen in Figure 5.5. Preferably, load is measured by placing a load cell between the sample cap and the loading piston. In addition to measuring devices, suitable signal excitation, conditioning, and recording equipment must also be maintained.

The external loading source may be any device capable of providing varying repeated loads in fixed cycles of load and release. These devices range from simple cam and switch control of static weights or air pistons to closed loop electro-hydraulic systems. A load duration of 0.1 second and load-release duration of 0.4 to 3 seconds has been found to be satisfactory for most applications.

The confining pressure and deviator stress used should approximate the expected in-place state of stresses. A confining pressure of 2 pounds per square inch (14 kiloNewtons) and deviator stress of 6 pounds per square inch (41 kiloNewtons) are reasonable values for most testing.

The test is started by applying 200 repetitions of a deviator stress of approximately 3 pounds per square inch (21 kiloPascals), followed succes- sively by 200 repetitions each at 6 pounds per square inch and 9 pounds per square inch (41 kiloPascals and 62 kiloPascals). The foregoing stress sequence constitutes sample conditioning, that is, elimination of the effects of the interval between compaction and loading, and also the effects of initial load versus reloading. The deviator load is decreased to 6 pounds per square inch (41 kiloPascals), after which 200 repetitions of load are applied and deviator load and vertical recoverable deformation at or near the 200th repetition are recorded. At the completion of the load, the cham- ber pressure is reduced to zero.

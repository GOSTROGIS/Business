10 Mix Selection

An HMA pavement surface must serve the demands of multiple traffic loadings and environments. The HMA mixture chosen for the project must meet the project requirements. The pavement designer has more choices now than in the past for the type of mix selected and for the materials (aggregates and asphalt binders) to be used on the project. This has led to the development of specialized mixes to address very specific problems. New design procedures such as the Superpave design procedure and others discussed earlier in this chap- ter have been implemented. In many cases, higher-quality materials are being specified than are needed for the particular project. Because of these concerns, the National Asphalt Pavement Association—in a coordinated effort with the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and an Expert Task Group consisting of representatives from the pav- ing industry and Departments of Transportation—developed National Asphalt Pavement Association Information Series 128, HMA Pavement Mix Type Selection Guide. This document provides detailed information on mix selection. As described earlier in this chapter, there are three primary types of mixes used for the construction of HMA pavements: fine and coarse graded dense mixes, stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes, and Open-Graded Friction Courses (OGFC) mixes.

4.10.1 Pavement Layers and Traffic Levels (definitions)

The following provides a short description of each of these layers in a pavement structure. Generally, materials of lesser quality are needed for each layer as they progress down in the pavement structure.

* Surface layer—contains the highest-quality materials. It pro- vides friction, smoothness, noise control, rutting resistance and durability.

° Intermediate layer or binder layer—consists of one or more lifts of HMA below the surface layer. It provides structural strength to the pavement.

° Base layer—consists of one or more lifts of HMA placed at the bottom of the pavement structure.

The mix selected for use in an HMA pavement will vary depending on the traffic level of the pavement. Table 4.12 provides some defini- tions and the typical roadway application represented by that traffic level.

4.10.2 Dense Graded Mixes

A dense graded mix is a mixture where the aggregate particles have an even distribution from coarse to fine. The goal is that the mix chosen will be stable and durable. To achieve these, it is generally necessary to intro- duce enough asphalt into the mix to insure durability but not so much as to reduce the stability of the mix below that needed for the traffic levels on the pavement. To insure that the chosen mix performs properly, it also must be built as it is designed and proper quality control procedures must be implemented. A special concern is the need for obtaining proper com- paction on the roadway.

Dense graded mixes are defined by their nominal maximum aggre- gate size (defined earlier in this chapter and in Chapter 3, “Mineral Aggregates and Mixture Additives”) and can be further defined as either fine-graded or coarse-graded. A fine-graded mix will have a higher pro- portion of fine sand-sized particles than a coarse-graded mix. Table 4.13 presents the criteria for fine/coarse graded HMA mixes. Coarse mixes are defined as having a combined aggregate gradation that passes below the primary control sieve control point, and fine mixes are defined as having a gradation that passes above or through the pri- mary control sieve control point.

In general, the fine gradation mixes with the smaller nominal maximum aggregate sizes will be used for the surface mixes (top 1% to 2 inches).

These mixes will have higher asphalt contents and lower permeability (due to greater aggregate surface area and smaller size voids), which will result in improved durability (ess block and shrinkage cracking). For applica- tions where the surface will receive little or no vehicle traffic, such as bike trails and walking paths, consideration should be given to designing the mix with 22 percent air-voids rather than 4 percent air-voids (standard requirement) to increase the durability of the mix further and reduce non- load associated cracking.

The lower levels (binder and base courses) should use the larger aggre- gate sizes. These mixes provide increased resistance to pavement deforma- tion. This allows the placement of thicker lifts and requires less asphalt cement due to less surface area.

The lift thickness for all mixes (surface, binder and base) is dictated by the nominal maximum aggregate size. If the nominal maximum aggregate size is too large with relation to the lift thickness it will be very difficult to obtain proper compaction of the mat. It is recommended that the lift thickness-be three times the nominal maximum aggregate size for fine graded mixes and four times the nominal maximum aggregate size for the coarse graded mixes. These same criteria will apply for SMA mixes. For OGFC mixes the thickness should be two times the nominal maximum aggregate size.

11.1 Pavement Management Concepts

11.1.1 Introduction

Transportation agencies have made significant investments in their pavement assets when both the costs of original construction and ongoing maintenance are considered. These assets are a vital part of the nation’s economy, providing important links to markets, schools, medical facilities, and places of employment. Demand on this road network continues to grow with time, placing more importance on maintaining suitable pavement con- ditions at a time when increasing loads are causing pavements to deteriorate more rapidly.

Faced with these types of challenges, managers of transportation agen- cies are making use of tools that provide the type of information needed to support their decision-making processes. A Pavement Management Sys- tem (PMS) is an example of this type of tool. The American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) defines PMS as: “A pavement management system is a set of tools or methods that assist decision-makers in finding optimum strategies for providing, evaluating, and maintaining pavements in a serviceable condition over a period of time.” A PMS uses reliable pavement inventory and condition information to help identify and prioritize pavement maintenance and rehabilitation needs within budget or other constraints that may exist. A PMS may or may not be computerized, but a computerized PMS holds a significant advantage in the ability to manage and analyze large databases of pavement inventory infor- mation. Since most computerized PMSs are able to forecast future pavement conditions, an agency can quickly evaluate the impact of today’s investment decisions on the long-term conditions of the road network. Consequently, agencies can use the information from a PMS to make more cost-effective maintenance and rehabilitation decisions, which results in a more efficient use of agency resources.

11.1.2 The Pavement Management Concept

Pavement management concepts can be used to manage the maintenance and rehabilitation of a road network by cost-effectively using a mixture of low-cost maintenance treatments on roads in good condition and more expensive rehabilitation treatments on roads with advanced levels of dete- rioration. This “mix of fixes” results in continuing efforts to reduce the num- ber of roads in poor condition (which are very expensive to fix) while maintaining good roads in good condition for as long as possible.

Life-Cycle Cost Analysis

Determining the most cost-effective maintenance or rehabilitation strategy for an existing pavement can be a daunting task. However, a life- cycle cost analysis (LCCA) provides a means for comparing competing treat- ment strategies over an analysis period of 20 to 40 years. In an LCCA, all costs expected to be incurred over the life of a pavement are identified and converted to a single point in time using economic equations that represent the time value of money. Because all costs are converted to a single point in time, different treatment strategies with different performance lives can easily be compared. Additionally, an LCCA allows an agency to consider both agency costs and user costs in the same analysis. Agency costs are represented by direct costs to the agency for construction, maintenance, and rehabilitation. User costs, which are not always considered in an LCCA, rep- resent the costs borne by the users under each scenario. For example, user costs might include differences in vehicle operating expenses, crash costs, or delay costs associated with the strategies being considered.

Condition Survey

A pavement management system uses the results of pavement condition surveys to help identify current and future maintenance and rehabilitation needs. All recommendations from the pavement management system are based on the results of the pavement condition survey so the importance of this component cannot be underestimated. The results of the survey are used to report current pavement conditions (usually in terms of a condition index), to forecast future conditions, and to determine appropriate mainte- nance and rehabilitation strategies.

The next section of this chapter discusses the different types of pave- ment condition surveys that are used to support a pavement management analysis. These techniques range from windshield surveys in which a sub- jective rating is assigned by a rater to represent the current pavement con- dition to more objective survey approaches in which distress type, severity, and quantity are recorded. Many agencies have begun using automated vehicles to collect pavement condition information. These vehicles oper- ate at near-traffic speeds, collecting digital images of the pavement surface and using lasers to record pavement surface irregularities like roughness, faulting, and rutting.

Regardless of which approach is used, it is important that the survey pro- cedures are followed consistently from one survey to another survey (and from one rater to another rater) and that they represent actual conditions. In general, objective condition surveys are more repeatable than subjective rating approaches, but they are often more labor intensive. Since it is impor- tant that pavement condition surveys are updated at least every three years, each agency must carefully evaluate the costs associated with each pavement condition survey procedure to ensure that the approach selected matches the resources available to support this activity.

Database

The inventory and condition information is stored in a pavement manage- ment database so it is available for use in generating reports and evaluating different maintenance and rehabilitation strategies. Databases can take a vari- ety of forms, but relational databases are most commonly used in pavement management today.

Reporting Tools

The results of a pavement management analysis, and the information contained in the database, can be reported in a number of different formats, including text, tables, and graphics. Many agencies link their pavement man- agement databases to geographic information systems (GIS) or other map packages to visually display the information. GIS displays of pavement management information are very effective tools when reporting pavement conditions to decision makers, elected officials, or the public.

Differences Based on Agency Size

Pavement management systems vary considerably in terms of the capa- bilities they provide, the user-friendliness of the program, and the degree to which the program can be customized to fit the particular needs of the agency. Both public domain and proprietary software packages are avail- able. In general, public domain programs cost less to install but have limited capabilities and/or flexibility in meeting the needs of individual agencies. On the other hand, proprietary software packages are typically more expensive but also more sophisticated, with the flexibility needed to be tailored to meet the needs of an individual agency. When selecting pavement management software, an agency should first define its needs and then select a software package that meets those needs. Otherwise, the agency may incur expenses for features that are not needed and will not be used. For a pavement manage- ment system to be useful to an agency, it should addresses the agency’s needs and be maintained over time according to the available resources.

11.1.4 Pavement Management Analysis Levels

Pavement management systems are used in transportation agencies to support decisions at three different levels: the project level, the network level, and the strategic level. Each of these levels is described further in this section of the chapter.

Project-Leveil Analysis

As the name implies, a project-level analysis involves decisions regard- ing the maintenance and rehabilitation of specific pavement sections. At this level, detailed pavement evaluations are conducted to determine the extent of any deterioration present, to identify the probable cause of the deterioration, and to design the appropriate treatment to correct the deficien- cies. Pavement management systems can support decisions at this level by providing information on the deterioration rate of a pavement section, main- tenance and construction histories, or other factors that may influence the selection of the appropriate repair strategy.

Network-Level Analysis

Pavement management systems are most commonly used at the network level, where the maintenance and rehabilitation needs of the entire road net- work are considered with respect to the amount of funding available. At the network level, the agency’s maintenance and rehabilitation needs are priori- tized so the most cost-effective use of available funding can be determined. Pavement needs are typically considered over a multiyear period and differ- ent maintenance and rehabilitation strategies are considered in the pavement management system. The initial recommendations from the pavement man- agement system provide general guidelines for the type of work needed and possible locations where the work should be performed. The development of final work plans is usually an iterative process that involves moving neighbor- ing projects from one year to another, or combining similar projects, to gain economy of scale. The work list is further developed at the project level in order to develop final project work plans and treatment designs.

Strategic-Level Analysis

In recent years, pavement management professionals have recognized the contributions of pavement management information to support an agency’s strategic decisions. At this level, individuals such as governmental officials, upper agency management, and the engineers in charge of pavement main- tenance, and bridge management all make decisions that play a part in deter- mining pavement performance targets, establishing needed funding levels to achieve the performance targets, distributing funds among regions or districts, and establishing a pavement preservation policy. When strategic-level deci- sions must be made, there is a direct need to be able to utilize the information from the pavement management system in order to demonstrate the conse- quences on the long-term pavement condition of various treatment strategies, funding levels, or performance targets.

11.1.5 Role of Pavement Management in Pavement Preservation

One cost-effective strategy that an agency may consider for preserving its pavement network is a pavement preservation program that includes the use of low-cost preventive maintenance treatments to preserve the functional characteristics of a pavement in relatively good condition.

Definitions The Federal Highway Administration’s (FHWA) Pavement Preservation Expert Task Group defines a pavement preservation program as follows:

A program employing a network level, long-term strategy that enhances pavement performance by using an integrated, cost-effective set of practices that extend pavement life, improve safety and meet motorist expectations.

These programs incorporate the use of preventive maintenance treat- ments, which have been defined by the AASHTO’s Standing Committee on Highways as follows:

A planned strategy of cost-effective treatments to an existing roadway system and its appurtenances that preserves the system, retards future deterioration, and maintains or improves the functional condition of the system (without significantly increasing the structural capacity).

Preventive maintenance treatments are typically applied to roads in relatively good condition before extensive amounts of deterioration are present. Examples of preventive maintenance treatments include crack sealing, chip seals, microsurfacing, and thin hot mix overlays.

Demonstrating Benefits of Pavement Preservation Agencies that have initiated pavement preservation programs have used their pavement management systems to illustrate the expected benefits in terms of overall network conditions. For instance, the predicted overall net- work conditions associated with a pavement preservation program can be compared to another program in which a worst-first strategy is employed (where the worst roads in the network are fixed first), as illustrated in Figure 11.3. The benefits of pavement preservation can also be demonstrated using the following techniques: * A summary of treatment costs needed to achieve a targeted condition level using different strategies ° The expected distribution of network conditions in different condition categories (such as good, fair, and poor) under different preservation strategies ° A life-cycle cost comparison of the anticipated agency costs associated with a pavement preservation strategy as compared to a more traditional rehabilitation strategy

11.2 Pavement Evaluation

11.2.1 Introduction

A pavement management system relies on objective, repeatable pavement condition information to determine current and future maintenance and rehabilitation needs. Therefore, conducting a meaningful pavement evalu- ation is one of the first steps in the identification of feasible maintenance and rehabilitation strategies. Two types of pavement evaluations are nor- mally conducted as part of an agency’s pavement management practices: a functional evaluation and a structural evaluation. A functional evaluation considers the surface characteristics of a road, including certain types of cracking, surface smoothness, noise, and surface friction characteristics. A structural evaluation is used to determine the ability of the pavement structure to carry traffic loadings. A structural evaluation typically requires detailed information about pavement layer thicknesses, paving layer mate- rial properties, subgrade support conditions, traffic, and the response of the pavement to loading. Nondestructive testing (NDT) using falling weight deflectometer (FWD) devices is one of the most common approaches to determining the response of the pavement under traffic loadings. More detailed information on methods used to evaluate pavement functional and structural characteristics follows.

11.2.2 Functional Characteristics and Evaluation

Pavement surface characteristics are typically incorporated into pave- ment condition surveys to support the types of analyses conducted with a pavement management system. Three of the most common methods of evaluating the functional condition of a pavement are discussed here.

Pavement Distress

By collecting pavement distress information, an agency is able to determine the relative amount of pavement deterioration present and the appropriate level of repair that is needed to correct the deficiencies. Many different types of pavement distress are included in a pavement condition survey, as discussed further in Section 11.3. Some types of pave- ment distress (such as transverse and block cracking) reflect deteriora- tion mechanisms that are more functional in nature, such as deterioration caused by climate or material properties. Other types of pavement distress (including alligator cracking or rutting) indicate load-related deterioration.

Manual Surveys

Pavement condition surveys may be conducted using either manual or automated approaches. In a manual survey, individuals collect the pavement distress information by walking the pavement surface or by viewing the pave- ment surface from a slow-moving vehicle traveling along the shoulder. Man- ual surveys are more commonly used in areas without heavy traffic volumes, which might hinder the data collection process or impede the safety of the survey crews. Automated Surveys

Where traffic conditions must be considered, some agencies conduct automated surveys using data collection vehicles that capture digital or video images of the pavement surface at prevailing speed limits; these images are later processed in the office. In addition to addressing some of the safety con- siderations associated with the conduct of pavement condition surveys, many of the vehicles used to conduct automated surveys are also equipped with sensors to record rutting, roughness, and faulting measures. These vehicles may also include a camera for taking panoramic shots along the roadway, which can be useful for monitoring the condition of signs or other road- way hardware. Some devices are equipped with special lighting to eliminate problems caused by shadows or to facilitate surveys conducted at night. An example of an automated data collection van is provided in Figure 11.4.

The images captured during the survey process are typically post-processed in an office. Sensor data are usually processed in real time and reported in accordance with guidelines established by the agency. Distress data may be processed. automatically using crack recognition software capable of recog- nizing and quantifying variations in grayscale that relate to the cracks on the pavement surface. Alternatively, the images may be viewed at a workstation where distress details are manually reviewed at a computer and distress quan- tities are recorded. This latter approach is referred to as a semi-automated method of distress processing.

The use of this technology has some disadvantages. The primary disad- vantage is that it is difficult to discern characteristics associated with verti- cal displacement when using this technology. For example, it is difficult to determine whether there is a vertical displacement along a crack using this technology. Some pavement condition survey procedures use verti- cal displacement along a crack as a means of determining crack severity. It is‘also difficult to collect accurate sensor data in urban environments that prevent the equipment from operating at constant speeds. Because of the resources required to conduct a pavement condition survey of an entire pavement network, some agencies utilize sampling techniques to determine the condition of each pavement section. Agencies that use this approach typically divide each pavement section into inspection units of a predetermined size, such as 2,500 square feet for asphalt-surfaced roads. Ride Quality

The longitudinal profile of a road provides an indication of the road’s tide quality. Early measures of ride quality were based on the subjective ratings of passenger comfort within a vehicle traveling at posted speeds. Since that time, equipment has been developed to measure ride quality, which is typically reported in terms of an International Roughness Index (IRD). Ride quality, which is also referred to as roughness or smoothness, is a popular measure of a road’s functional condition because it represents a condition measure that the public recognizes. The measurement of ride quality is common on highways and other high-speed facilities.

Most modern pavement-profiling devices measure vertical acceleration in response to the road and provide a pavement profile. Among the instru- ments on these vehicles are accelerometers that provide a horizontal plane of reference and sensors (laser, acoustic, or infrared) to measure the devia- tions of the pavement surface from the horizontal plane. These vehicles operate at near traffic speeds and are often outfitted with rut bars and cameras so that distress information can be collected at the same time. An example of this type of vehicle is provided in Figure 11.4.

Testing Approach

For acceptance testing on new construction, profilographs have been used in the past. Today, more agencies are using IRI for acceptance pur- poses. Profilographs produce a trace of the pavement surface, which is then compared with acceptable limits established by the agency. More information on the standard test method and equipment used to measure the pavement profile is available in ASTM E 1274, “Standard Test Method for Measuring Pavement Roughness Using a Profilograph.”

Surface Friction

Adequate skid resistance, or surface friction, is an important functional characteristic that influences directional control and stopping ability on a wet or dry pavement. Most agencies become aware of surface friction problems during inclement weather, when the potential for hydroplaning (in which the vehicle tire has lost contact with the pavement surface and is actually riding on a thin film of water) is high. However, safety can also be compromised on dry pavement surfaces where surface friction is low.

Surface friction is a measure of the resistance provided by the pavement surface to the sliding of tires when they are prevented from rotating. In addition to pavement surface characteristics, surface friction can also be influenced by factors such as tire wear, vehicle speed, tire pressure, and the presence of water.

Surface friction information is typically collected as part of a safety program to reduce crash potential. However, agencies are beginning to incorporate surface friction measures into their pavement management systems to help identify areas that might benefit from treatments that restore pavement surface friction characteristics.

Types of Equipment

Three different approaches are used to measure surface friction: the locked-wheel mode, the slip mode, and the yaw mode. The most com- monly used device to measure surface friction in North America is the locked-wheel skid trailer, such as the one shown in Figure 11.5. A test wheel is mounted on a trailer, which is pulled at the desired speed. Water is applied in front of the trailer, and the test wheel(s) is locked. The friction force at the tire and the speed of the vehicle are measured and reported in terms of a skid number (SN). 11.2.0 Structural Characteristics and Evaluation

A pavement structural evaluation is used to estimate the structural adequacy of an existing pavement to determine whether the pave- ment can sufficiently meet projected traffic loadings. In situations when the pavement is not adequate to meet projected loadings, the results of the structural evaluation can provide the information needed to design the appropriate treatment.

Because of the level of detail normally associated with this type of eval- uation, structural evaluations are typically conducted as part of a project- level investigation. This may include more extensive pavement condition surveys conducted in conjunction with destructive testing (such as coring and boring) and with nondestructive testing using pavement deflection devices. Pavement deflection testing does not disturb the underlying pave- ment structure and does not require pavement materials to be removed for laboratory testing. However, it does require accurate pavement thickness information at each test location so coring is often conducted to verify pavement layer thicknesses.

Pavement Deflection Testing

Pavement deflection testing measures the response of the pavement to a load in terms of the resultant pavement deflection. The deflection data are used to backcalculate in situ material properties, to calculate pavement responses to load, and as inputs to other pavement analyses. By plot- ting maximum deflection measurements with road stations, areas of high deflections can be easily delineated to define project or subproject limits. The deflection information can also be used in deflection-based overlay design procedures.

Types of Equipment

Several types of pavement deflection devices are available, including impulse deflection devices (such as the falling weight deflectometer), steady-state dynamic deflection devices (such as the Dynaflect), and static deflection devices (such as the Benkelman Beam). The falling weight deflectometer (FWD) is the most commonly used deflection device for assessing pavement responses.

The FWD is capable of simulating in magnitude and duration the dynamic loads applied to a pavement by wheel loads. A dynamic load is applied by dropping a set of weights onto a circular load plate. A range of load magnitudes can be achieved by varying the number of weights used and the heights from which they are dropped.

13.5 Cold-Mix Recycling

13.5.7 Introduction

Cold-mix recycling is the process of using RAP as a component material when producing a cold mix asphalt material. The RAP is obtained either by excavating the asphalt pavement from an existing pavement and then crushing and screening it to the desired gradation or by milling the RAP from the roadway using milling equipment, as previously discussed. After the RAP has been removed from the roadway and processed as necessary, it is mixed with an asphalt emulsion, a recycling agent, or with a foamed asphalt and additional aggregate (if necessary). The cold-mix recycling process can be accomplished in either a central plant—where the RAP is hauled to a central location similar to how RAP is used as a component in HMA—or in an in-place process on the roadway.

13.5.2 Central Plant (CP) Cold Recycling

General

Millings and recovered asphalt pavement that may need crushing are an excellent source of material for constructing an asphalt-treated base or as a patching material for maintenance of HMA pavements. A discussion of cold mixing is included in Chapter 15, “Special Uses of Asphalt and HMA.”

Materials

RAP is produced by milling, ripping, breaking, crushing, or pulverizing the roadway. RAP is hauled to a central location. RAP material handling and storage is similar to that of conventional aggregate. It should be stored in conical stockpiles of 10 feet (3 meters) or less. Conical piles are rec- ommended because RAP has a tendency to re-agglomerate in large piles. A crust will form on the first 8-12 inches (200-250 millimeters) of the pile. The crust and the use of conical piles will help shed water from precipita- tion. The crust is easily broken with a front-end loader. The RAP stockpile will hold water and not drain like a conventional aggregate stockpile. The use of a horizontal stockpile will result in a greater accumulation of mois- ture (up to 7-8 percent).

The sources of RAP for central plant cold recycling vary (they can come from different projects, contain chip seals and slurry seals, and/or mainte- nance patches). Thus, many agencies require that the RAP not be blended or commingled with RAP from other projects. When this policy is followed, the stockpiles should be kept separated and identified by source (preferably on a map and with signs identifying the material in each stockpile). When they are

13.6 Cold In-Place Recycling

13.6.1 General

The Cold In-Place Recycling (CIPR) process consists of milling the exist- ing pavement to a depth of 3-5 inches (75-125 millimeters), screening, crushing, and mixing with an asphalt emulsion, foamed asphalt, additives (lime or cement), and adding aggregate if needed. The recycled mixture is then placed and compacted using conventional paving equipment or by motor grader.

Project selection is critical to the success or failure of a CIPR project. It must include an assessment of the existing pavement condition, types of distress, and modes of failure. To design the required overlay thickness, the pavement materials (base, subbase, and subgrade) must be sampled and tested. In addi- tion, an evaluation of the history of the pavement maintenance operations and the expected traffic volume must be included in the analysis.

Most pavement distresses, such as fatigue cracking, transverse cracking, reflection cracking, and raveling, can be successfully corrected using CIPR. Using CIPR eliminates damaged layers, resulting in a crack-free layer that pro- vides a base for the HMA surface layer. For CIPR to be effective in providing a long-life pavement structure, as much of the existing HMA layer as possible should be treated. When considering the depth of recycling, the designer must consider the ability of the pavement structure to support the recycling equipment operating on a weakened pavement section as the existing materi- als are being processed. It is suggested that at least 70 percent of the existing pavement thickness be treated and that at least 1-2 inches (25-50 millimeters) of pavement remain in place to support the weight of the recycling train. If there are paved shoulders, they should be treated to prevent propagation of cracks into the driving lane. See Figure 13.20 for a typical pavement for cold in-place recycling. The right side of the figure shows the cold in-place pave- ment and the left side is the pavement before recycling.

Badly rutted pavements with excessively high asphalt content can be cor- rected with CIPR, but additional aggregate may be necessary to offset the high-asphalt content in the existing roadway. Hydrated lime, Portland cement or Class C fly ash are often used as additives to stiffen the mixtures and reduce curing time for CIPR. If additional aggregates are used, they should be crushed angular materials in an amount limited to 25 percent by weight of the RAP. A pavement that failed because of a wet unstable base, subbase, or subgrade is generally not a suitable candidate for CIPR. A pavement exhibiting this type of failure should be considered for full-depth reclamation (discussed in the next section) or for complete reconstruction, along with pavements where the underlying soils are expansive in nature and/or where the asphalt layer is badly stripped.

The simplest methods consist of using empirical equations or the experience of the mix-design engineer to establish suggested asphalt content for the mix based on the amount and consistency of the asphalt binder in the RAP. Sampling Existing Pavement

The first step in obtaining samples of the existing roadway is to conduct a visual survey of the project so that the sampling plan will result in suffi- cient samples to fully describe the materials in the roadway. It is suggested that samples be taken at a minimum of one per each half mile (kilometer) of roadway. The sample should be full-depth—either a core sample, a bulk sample obtained by sawing or milling, or a combination. A core sample should be at least 6 inches (150 millimeters) in diameter. Samples obtained from coring or sawing can be processed in a laboratory crusher to simu- late the effect of a milling machine during construction. The samples used in the mix design should be from the actual layer(s) of the pavement that will be cold recycled. Samples should be obtained during construction to compare with the mix design materials and thus provide a basis for revi- sion of the design quantities for the additives.

Materials Selection and Mix Design Process

The properties of the aggregate and recovered asphalt binder content should be determined. The asphalt binder should be extracted from the RAP using ASTM D 6307 or AASHTO T 308, “Test Method for Asphalt Con- tent of Asphalt Cement of Hot Mix Asphalt by the Ignition Method.” After extraction, the asphalt binder content and the gradation of the extracted aggregate should be determined. If additional aggregate needs to be added to improve the mix stability of the final CIPR layer, this is the gradation that should be used. The addition of an aggregate with a high percentage of crushed coarse aggregate can make significant improvements in the stability of the mix. The percentage of new aggregate to be added to the mix should be determined using standard blending procedures to provide a mix with the desired gradation.

Additional asphalt binder is necessary for facilitating compaction, improv- ing the cohesion of the new mix, and reducing the stiffness of the aged asphalt binder in the RAP. Typically, 0.5-1.5 percent additional residual binder is needed. The more uncoated new aggregate used in the mix, the higher the additional binder content will be. For most mix designs, this amount is based on previous experience in ensuring the coating of the RAP material. The amount of binder selected during the mix design for most projects is a starting point, with changes made in the field. The binder can be an emulsi- fied asphalt cationic or anionic slow-set emulsion, or a high-float emulsion. Polymer-modified versions of these materials are also used to improve early strength and to reduce rutting and thermal cracking. The binder can also be a foamed asphalt.

When using emulsions, some moisture generally must be added to provide for coating of the RAP particles. The water can be added at the cutting head of the milling machine or directly to the CIPR mixture. A coating test is recommended. This test consists of mixing a quantity of RAP (typically 2 pounds; 1000 grams) of RAP with various amounts of water at 0.5 percent increments. The water is mixed into the RAP and then the asphalt binder is added. Any admixtures (lime, fly ash, or cement) to be included should be added to the mix before the asphalt binder is added. Adding dust-sized materials will generally increase the amount of moisture needed. They are mixed by hand in a bowl, and the dispersion of the materials is visu- ally monitored. The lowest moisture content that results in no additional increase in curing is selected. The result of the asphalt binder and the added moisture is referred to as the fluids content in the mix.

Mix design samples are manufactured using the RAP, the proposed asphalt binder, and water content (which make up the total fluids con- tent). Generally, the mixing is done at ambient temperatures of 68°-77°F (20°-25°C). The samples are mixed at the total fluid content discussed previously, and the proposed asphalt binder is varied above and below the initial additive content by 0.7 percent. The mix water is adjusted to keep the total fluids content the same for all samples. The samples are either manufactured using a 50-blow Marshall compaction at ambient field con- ditions or can be compacted with a Superpave gyratory compactor. Studies have shown a compaction level of about 35 gyrations is appropriate.

Before further testing, the samples must be cured to eliminate their excess water and for the emulsions to set up to develop their maximum strength. The curing process varies with the agency doing the testing. The samples are cured in the mold for 4 hours at 140°F (60°C) and then extruded from the mold and cured until they reach a constant mass.

After the samples are manufactured, the volumetric properties are deter- mined using the procedures discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.” CIPR samples may break up during the standard bulk specific gravity test, and thus the CoreLok procedure discussed in Chapter 4 should be used. Strength testing—such as Marshall stability and flow, unconfined compression, and resilient modulus tests—has been used to evaluate the CIPR mixes. But no guidelines have been established. The job-mix formula used as a starting point in the field is based on the coating of the RAP mate- rials and the optimum density of the mix.

13.6.4 Placement of CIPR Materials

The CIPR mix can be placed in a windrow (Figure 13.23) or discharged directly into the paving machine. The procedures discussed in Chapter 8, “Hot Mix Asphalt Paving” should be followed. Conventional paving equipment is used to place the RAP. A paver with a surge bin or hopper insert can be used to reduce the fluctuations in the production of the mix. Unlike HMA paving, the screed must be operated cold because a heated screed causes the asphalt emulsion to break and the mix to stick to the screed. The mix should be handled in such a manner to avoid segregation.

13.6.5 Compaction of CIPR Layers

The compaction of a CIPR mix should be done at a slow and uniform speed. Any change in the direction of the rollers should be done carefully to avoid displacement of the CIPR. Solvents should not be used on the roller wheels or drums to prevent pickup of the CIPR. To prevent adhesion of the CIPR to the rollers, water can be sprayed on the wheels/drums. A small amount of liquid detergent or water-soluble release agent can be added to the water to reduce pickup.

Initial compaction can be done with heavy vibratory, pneumatic rollers, and steel-wheel rollers. It has been found that a CIPR mix cannot be com- pacted to the same level as HMA. Typically, a CIPR mix will be compacted to an in-place air-void range of 9-14 percent due to the moisture content of the

13.6.6 Curing and Aeration

When using emulsions, the compacted CIPR mix must be cured before a wearing course can be placed. Curing is needed to reduce the moisture content in the mixture before it is sealed by the wearing/surface courses. The time required for this process depends on daytime and nighttime temperatures, the humidity levels and rainfall after completion of the recy- cling process, and the amount of water added to the mix during the CIPR process. If necessary, laydown and compaction can be delayed and the mix aerated with a motorgrader to accelerate the curing process. If rain is forecasted the CIPR mix can be bladed flat and sealed with a steel wheeled roller until the weather clears up and aeration and curing can continue.

Different agencies define this moisture level on the basis of total moisture in the mix or on the increase in the moisture content in the pavement before recycling. Some agencies feel that if a dry core is obtained from CIPR mix relatively intact, the mix has cured enough to place it on the wearing surface. This curing/aeration process can take 10 to 14 days. In advance, if this timeframe is a problem, hydrated lime or Portland cement can be added during the mixing process to accelerate curing.

During the curing process, traffic is generally allowed to operate on the CIPR surface. In fact, the kneading action of traffic can aid the aera- tion process. If minor rutting occurs during the curing aeration period, the surface of the CIPR mix can be lightly scarified, bladed and re-rolled using a heavy pneumatic roller following by a static steel roller to remove any roller marks. This rolling should be done on a hot day during the heat of the day. Another problem that can occur is raveling of the surface aggregate on the CIPR. If this occurs, a fog seal of diluted emulsion (50 percent with water) can be applied at a rate of 0.05-0.10 gallon/yard? (0.23-0.45 litre/ meter’). The application of a fog seal should not be done unless absolutely necessary and traffic can be kept off the surface until the fog seal has had a chance to completely dry (at least 2 hours) because the fog seal can seal the CIPR surface and impede the curing process. When foamed asphalt is used for CIPR, no curing or aeration is required. The small amount of water used in the foaming process flashes to steam when it comes in contact with the hot liquid asphalt leaving no moisture behind.

13.6.7 Wearing Course

CIPR mixes are not intended to be a finished surface or wearing course and will require a wearing course because of the high air-voids and to protect the surface from raveling. For pavements with low traffic volumes, single- and double-surface treatments have been used. For pavements with a higher traffic volume, the thickness-design procedures detailed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design” should be used.

Roadway Inspection During the construction process, the following should be checked:

664

The weather and seasonal requirements in the specifications must be met.

If aggregate and/or additive is being added to the mix by spreading in front of the milling machine, periodic yield checks should be made. The yield of the emulsion, aggregate and additive should be computed and compared to the mix design.

The CIPR mix should be visually checked to ensure that it is homogeneous, with no segregation; it should also be checked to ensure that no large (sometimes agglomerated particles or uncrushed pieces) are in the mix. If this occurs, the crushing and screening pro- cess should be checked.

The coating of the mix should be checked. If the mix is not adequately coated, the amount of mix water should be increased. Excessive water can cause the asphalt to flush to the surface and increase the curing time. Too little water may cause segregation. Reducing the asphalt emulsion one grade (for example, using an HFE 300 instead of an HFE 150) may also improve coating.

The CIPR mix should be checked for cohesion. If the mix lacks co- hesion, the emulsion content should be increased. Too much asphalt emulsion can result in an unstable mix; too little can cause the mix to ravel. A simple field test can be used to evaluate cohesion. A ball of the material is made by squeezing the material in the fist. If the ball falls apart after the pressure is released, the mix lacks cohesion. The palm of the hand should contain specks of asphalt, indicating proper asphalt content. If the hand is stained, the asphalt content is too high. Over-mixing can lead to scrubbing the emulsion off of the aggregate particles and early emulsion break that causes the mix to be too stiff. Under-mixing results in undercoating of the aggregate. 100 percent coating, as in HMA, is not the goal with CIPR mixes.

The binder content can also be evaluated during placement of the CIPR mix after initial compaction. The mat should be brown and cohesive. Excessive raveling indicates too little asphalt in the mix.

13.7. Full-Depth Reclamation

Full-depth Reclamation (FDR) consists of pulverizing the full thickness of the asphalt pavement and a portion of the underlying base, subbase, and/or subgrade. The layers are blended to form a homogeneous, sta- bilized base layer. This pulverized layer can be stabilized mechanically, chemically, asphalt stabilized or a combination of the approaches. The Basic Asphalt Recycling Manual prepared by the Asphalt Recycling and Reclaiming Association is an excellent resource on the FDR process.

Mechanical stabilization, which may be accomplished by adding crushed aggregate, RAP, or crushed PCC, increases the strength solely through improving the aggregate interlock. Chemical stabilization uses lime, cement, or lime/fly ash to form a binder, while asphalt stabilization uses asphalt emulsions or foamed asphalt.

The type, severity, and frequency of pavement distress in the existing pavement should be evaluated during the project’s design process. The FDR process destroys the existing pavement to produce a homogenous pulverized layer. If the existing pavement has large or frequent patching, the variability of the final product will be affected. In areas where the patches are the result of subgrade instability or in high water-table areas, the cause of the problem should be fixed before beginning the FDR pro- cess. This may require excavating the problem areas and replacing some subgrade with improved materials, or improving the drainage system by installing a subdrainage system or reworking the ditches in the area.

The pavement structure should be designed according to the proce- dures detailed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design,” using the appropriate design values for the FDR layers.

43./.1 Stabilization with Chemical Additives

When chemical stabilization is being evaluated for use in FDR, the fol- lowing criteria should be used to evaluate which chemical should be selected: ° Lime: If the PI > 20 and the minimum clay content (2 microns) is > 10 percent

° Cement: If the PI = 10 percent and the percent passing the No. 200 (.075 millimeter) sieve is < 20 percent

* Lime, cement, or fly ash combination: If layer has material with less than 10 percent passing the No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) sieve and the Plasticity Index range is 10<PI<20

Calibration of Compactors

A number of manufacturers produce Superpave Gyratory Compactors. See Figure 4.15 for a composite photo of different compaction equipment.

In the fabrication of the SGC specimens, a constant consolidation pressure is applied to the sample while the mold gyrates at a nomi- nally constant angle (the angle of gyration). To provide specimens of consistent density, it is very important that the SCC maintain a constant pressure and a known constant angle of gyration during the compac- tion process. Each model of SGC uses a different method of setting, inducing, and maintaining the angle of gyration. Each model also uses a unique calibration system to measure the angle of gyration externally in relation to the mold. These calibration systems cannot be used uni- versally on all models of SGCs. Inconsistencies in the density of asphalt specimens produced by different SGCs can be attributed to variations in the angle of gyration. This can be very troublesome when comparing mix designs prepared by different compactors or during quality con- trol, when the quality control specimens are manufactured using one brand of compactor and the acceptance specimens are manufactured using another brand of compactor. The American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) have developed a pro- cedure for evaluating the internal angle of a SGC. It is AASHTO PP 48 “Evaluation of the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC) Internal Angle of Gyration.”

Preparation of Aggregate

Each aggregate blend being evaluated should have a batch sheet show- ing the batch weights of each aggregate component and the asphalt binder. Three mix sample sizes are needed to complete a mix design. For the com- pacted mix specimens that will be used in the Superpave mix design, the specimen size is 150 millimeters (diameter) by 115 millimeters (height) and requires approximately 4700 grams of aggregate. Samples of mix to be used for the determination of the maximum specific gravity using AASHTO T 209 or ASTM D 2401 will remain uncompacted, and their size will vary by aggregate size, ranging from 1000 grams to 2500 grams. Moisture testing using AASHTO T 283 or ASTM D 4867 requires a mix specimen height of 95 millimeters and will require approximately 3700 grams of aggregate.

Compaction of Volumetric Specimens

Detailed procedures for the preparation of the volumetric specimens are contained in the SP-2 manual. The specimens are compacted to either N,,., or N_,, 48 required by the specifying agency. A minimum of two specimens are compacted at the trial blend’s estimated asphalt content, at 0.5 percent of the estimated asphalt content, and at 1.0 percent above the estimated asphalt content. Four asphalt contents are required for the accomplish- ment of a Superpave mix design. Many designers will estimate the trial binder content based on experience.

The specimens are mixed and compacted at temperatures using a plot of viscosity versus temperature (Figure 4.16). This data is available from the asphalt binder supplier. The mixing and compaction temperatures are those corresponding to a binder viscosity value of 0.17 + 0.02 Pascal-second and 0.28 + 0.03 Pascal-second, respectively. These viscosity ranges are not valid for modified asphalt binders. After compaction of the samples, the volumetric properties of each spec- imen are determined. The results of the laboratory testing are plotted to provide curves of asphalt content versus air-voids, voids mineral aggregate, and voids filled with asphalt. Typical curves are shown in Figure 4.17.

4.3.4 Design Asphalt Binder Content

After the data has been plotted, the designer will pick an asphalt binder content that will provide 4 percent air-voids at N,,,. Then the designer determines the values for voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA) and voids filled with asphalt (VFA) from the other curves. These values will then be checked against the mixture specifications. The recommended Superpave volumetric mixture design requirements are shown in Table 4.5.

4.3.5 Steps That Can Be Taken to Adjust _ Properties

If the testing indicates that the mix does not meet the mix design require- ments, the following suggestions are provided (AASHTO R 35):

° Adjusting the VMA—A change in the aggregate gradation or aggre- gate source is usually required to adjust the VMA. There are three likely options:

o Change the gradation. Moving the gradation curve toward the maxi- mum density line will generally reduce the VMA; moving it away from the maximum density line will generally increase the VMA.

o Change the amount of minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeters) mate- rial. Increasing the No. 200 material usually lowers the VMA, and decreasing it will typically raise the VMA.

o Change the surface texture and/or the shape of the particles of the fine aggregate fraction. The increased use of an aggregate with a rough texture (higher FAA) will generally increase the VMA, and the increased use of a rounded aggregate will reduce the VMA.

4.3.6 Moisture Sensitivity

The loss of bond, or stripping, caused by the presence of moisture between the asphalt and the aggregate is a problem in some areas and can be severe in some cases. Many factors, such as aggregate character- istics, asphalt characteristics, environment, traffic, construction practices, and drainage, can contribute to stripping.

The moisture susceptibility test used to evaluate HMA for stripping is AASHTO T 283 or ASTM D 4867. This test is not a performance-based test, but it serves two purposes. First, it identifies whether a combination of asphalt binder and aggregate is moisture susceptible. Second, it measures the effectiveness of antistripping additives.

In the test, two subsets of test specimens are produced. Specimens are compacted to a height of 95 millimeters and to an air-void content of 6.5 to 7.5 percent with a target value of 7 percent. Test specimens should be sorted so that each subset has the same average air-void content. One subset is moisture conditioned by vacuum saturation to a constant degree of saturation in the range of 70 to 80 percent. This is followed by an optional freeze cycle. The final conditioning step is a hot-water soak. After conditioning, both sub- sets are tested for indirect tensile strength. After conditioning, the specimen is removed from the water bath and placed on its side between the bearing plates of the testing machine. Steel loading strips are placed between the specimen and the bearing plates. A load is applied to the specimen at the rate of 2 inches (50 millimeters) per minute. The maximum load is recorded. This is the indirect tensile strength of the specimen. This test is conducted on the conditioned and the unconditioned specimens. The average strength for the conditioned and the unconditioned specimens is determined. The ratio of these two values is the tensile-strength ratio or TSR. Superpave requires a minimum TSR of 80 percent. Lower values are indicative of mixtures that may exhibit stripping problems after construction.

Steps to Improve Moisture Resistance

The best way to improve the moisture resistance of an HMA mixture is a proper mix design with sufficient asphalt binder to coat the aggregates. This must be coupled with proper compaction in the field. The goal is to produce as impermeable an HMA mixture as possible to prevent the intru- sion of water. However, some asphalt/aggregate combinations will require an additive to improve the moisture resistance of the mixture. The two most commonly used admixtures are hydrated lime and proprietary liquid antistrip agents. It is imperative that the mix design be accomplished using the admixture and the asphalt binder that will be used on the project. The reaction is chemical, and the results will vary depending on the type and amount of additive, source of aggregate and source of asphalt binder. For more information on moisture sensitivity, see the Asphalt Institute’s MS-24, Moisture Sensitivity manual.

4,4 Marshall Method of Mix Design

4.4.1 Introduction and Historical Context

The concepts of the Marshall method of designing paving mixtures were formulated by Bruce Marshall, formerly a bituminous engineer with the Mississippi State Highway Department. The U.S. Corps of Engineers, through extensive research and correlation studies, improved and added certain features to Marshall’s test procedure, and ultimately developed mix design criteria. The Marshall test procedures have been standardized by the American Society for Testing and Materials. Procedures are given by ASTM D 1559.

The Marshall method has generally been applicable to dense-graded HMA paving mixtures using Performance Graded (PG) asphalt binders and containing aggregates with a maximum size of 1 inch (25 milli- meters) or less. It has also been adapted to the design of SMA mixtures. The Marshall method is used for both laboratory design and the qual- ity control of hot mix asphalt pavements. For a detailed description of the steps and equipment needed to conduct a Marshall Mix Design, the reader is referred to the Asphalt Institute’s MS-2 Mix Design Methods manual. The Asphalt Institute Mix Design Program SW-2 performs all of the calculations needed to conduct a Marshall Mix Design. (It is recom- mended that mix designers refer to either AASHTO or ASTM require- ments, or to the design agency’s requirements so that those specific procedures can be followed.)

4.4.2 Description of Method

The Marshall method uses standard cylindrical test specimens of 24 inches (64 millimeters) in height by 4 inches (102 millimeters) in diam- eter. These are prepared using a specified procedure for heating, mixing, and compacting the asphalt-aggregate mixtures. These specimens are compacted using a Marshall compaction hammer (Figure 4.18) and tested in a Marshall stability and flow device (see Figure 4.19). The two principal features of the Marshall method of mix design are a density-voids analysis and a stability-flow test of the compacted test specimens.

The stability of the test specimens is the maximum load resistance in pounds (newtons) that the standard test specimen will develop at 140°F (60°C) when tested as outlined here. The flow value is the total movement or strain, in units of 1/100 inch (0.25 millimeter), occurring in the speci- men between no load and maximum load during the stability test.

4.5 Hveem Method of

Mix Design

4.5.1 introduction and Historical Context

The Hveem method of mix design is a laboratory procedure based on two properties of the compacted specimen: cohesion and friction. Den- sity and voids of the compacted specimen are established before mea- suring the stability with the stabilometer. The stabilometer is a triaxial testing device in which vertical loads are applied and resulting lateral pressures are measured. The concepts of the Hveem method of design- ing paving mixtures were advanced and developed under the direction of Francis N. Hveem, who was the materials and research engineer for the California Division of Highways. The test procedures and their application were developed through extensive research and correlation studies on asphalt highway pavements.

The method shown is applicable to the design of hot, dense-graded asphalt paving mixtures.

4.5.2 Description of Method

Hveem method test procedures have been standardized by the ASTM and AASHTO. Test procedures are found in AASHTO T 246 or ASTM D 1560, and AASHTO T 247 or ASTM D 1561. Testing procedures presented here are basically the same as those in the ASTM procedure. For a detailed description of the Hveem procedure, the reader is referred to the Asphalt Institute’s MS-2, Mix Design Methods manual. It is recommended that agen- cies refer to either AASHTO or ASTM, or to the design agency’s require- ments, so that those specific procedures can be followed.

The Hveem method uses standard test specimens of 2.5 inches (64 mil- limeters) in height by 4 inches (102 millimeters) in diameter; these are pre- pared using a specified procedure for heating, mixing, and compacting the asphalt-aggregate mixtures. The principal features of the Hveem method of mix design are the oil soak and Centrifuge Kerosene Equivalent (CKE) tests on the aggregates to estimate the asphalt requirements of the mix, followed by a stabilometer test, a cohesiometer test, a swell test, and a density-voids analysis on test specimens of the compacted paving mixtures. The stabilom- eter test uses a special triaxial-type testing cell for measuring the resistance of the compacted mix to lateral displacement under vertical loading, and the swell test measures the resistance of the mix to the action of water. The speci- mens are maintained at 140°F (60°C) for the stability tests; the swell tests are performed at room temperature.

The Hveem mix design process consists of three basic steps:

1. Aggregate selection. This step consists of insuring that the aggregates used in the mixture meet the design requirements. For a detailed discussion of the aggregate requirements for an HMA mixture, the reader is referred to Chapter 3, “Mineral Aggregates and Mixture Additives.” Once the designer is assured that the aggregates meet the specified physical requirements, the next step is to blend the aggregates to meet the requirements.

2. Asphalt binder selection. The designer identifies the asphalt binder to be used in the mixture based on the procedures described in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalt.”

3. Test specimen preparation. A series of test specimens is pre- pared using a particular blend or gradation of aggregate over a range of different asphalt contents so that the test data curves show a well-defined optimum value. Tests should be scheduled on the basis of 0.5 percent increments of asphalt content, with at least two asphalt contents above optimum and at least two below optimum. The following procedure outlines the steps associated with the determination of the optimum asphalt content using the Hveem method:

a. In the Hveem method, the initial asphalt content is determined by the oil soak and Centrifuge Kerosene Equivalent tests and the calculated surface area. For example, for a mix with an esti- mated asphalt content of 5.5 percent, one sample each would be made at 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, and 6.5 percent asphalt by weight for a total of five samples. A minimum of 2 additional samples would be used for the determination of the maximum specific gravity as discussed in Section 4.2. Compact the samples using the California Kneading Compactor (see Figure 4.21).

b. Test each sample for stability and cohesion using the Hveem Stabilometer and Cohesiometer (see Figure 4.22). The passing values of stability and cohesion are specific to the agency using the design procedure.

c. Determine the volumetric properties of the mix. Data is plot- ted as shown in Figure 4.23.

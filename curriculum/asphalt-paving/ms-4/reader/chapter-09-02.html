Contact Pressure

The contact pressure of a pneumatic-tire roller is directly related to the wheel load divided by the contact area (footprint) that the tire has in contact with the pavement surface. The ply rating of the tire determines the maximum and minimum inflation pressures, thereby affecting the con- tact area and resulting contact pressure. Running the tires at the mid to lower end of the inflation pressure range for the particular tire will help improve the surface texture by sealing the surface. Running the tire infla- tion pressure higher increases the compactive effort of the roller. All the tires should have the same ply rating and the same inflation pressure.

Adjusting Compactive Force

There are several ways to adjust the compactive force of a pneumatic- tire roller. The first is to add ballast, which will increase the mass of the roller. The second means of adjusting the compactive force is to increase the air pressure in each of the tires per the tire inflation pressure recom- mendations. Increasing air pressure reduces the contact area (footprint) and increases the contact pressure of the roller. The third means of adjust- ing the compactive effort is to adjust the speed of the roller Gust like the steel-wheel roller). Adjusting the speed of the roller affects the dwell time the tires have on the surface of the HMA.

Tire Pickup

When using pneumatic rollers, keeping the tires hot is the most effective means of preventing pickup. Running the roller back and forth on a pre- viously placed mat for at least 10 minutes will help warm the tires; once paving has started, the roller can slowly work into the hot mix. If needed, applying a small amount of nonfoaming detergent or water soluble release agent on the wetting mat of a pneumatic-tire roller at the beginning of rolling operations helps prevent the HMA from sticking to the tires before they warm up. Do not use diesel or other petroleum fuels/solvents as a release agent. Keeping pads and scrapers on each tire will prevent pickup from getting worse if a tire does pick up in an area. If tires start to pick up, move the roller off the mat, clean the tires carefully, then start again. Skirting around tires helps minimize heat loss due to wind.

9.3.3 Vibratory Rollers

Vibratory rollers are the most versatile and effective rollers for compact- ing HMA. They may include one or two smooth-surface steel drums, as shown in Figure 9.7, that have eccentric weights spinning around a shaft within the drum. Vibratory rollers can be used for compacting any type of HMA mixture but generally are not used in the vibratory mode when the mat thickness is 1.5 inches (37.5 millimeters) or less to avoid fracturing the aggregate and damaging the mixture. The compactive effort of vibratory rollers can be adjusted for the mix type and base conditions on the project by varying the frequency and amplitude. Spilt-drum vibratory rollers can also

Weight

Vibratory rollers vary in static weight from 2.5 to 18 tons (2.3 to 16.4 tonnes). Widths vary from 39.4 to 84 inches (1000 to 2134 millimeters). The static linear load is generally less for vibratory rollers than for static steel-wheel rollers.

Frequency

Vibratory rollers may be used in static mode while compacting thinner lifts of HMA, but the use of dynamic force is what makes them so effective when compared with static rollers. Frequency is expressed in vibrations or impacts per minute, or hertz (Hz), and is a measure of the rotational speed of the eccentric weight inside the drum of the roller. Typical frequencies of rollers used on an HMA pavement range from 2,500 to over 4,000 vibrations per minute (vpm) (42-67 hertz). The high-frequency drum movement puts the aggregate particles in motion, allowing them to slide past one another more easily under the weight of the drum. Mixes with high percentages of aggregate with crushed faces benefit the most with vibratory rollers. The general rule of thumb is to use highest-frequency setting available. Amplitude

Simply explained, amplitude is the up-and-down motion of the drum that is caused as the eccentric weight spins inside the drum. The higher the amplitude, the greater the vertical movement generated, and the greater the force exerted on the HMA mix. The general approach is to use the lowest- amplitude setting that will achieve density. However, amplitude is tied closely to the thickness of the layer being placed. Higher-amplitude settings are appropriate when lift thicknesses are 3 inches (75 millimeters) or greater. Thicker layers can handle a higher amplitude. Care must be taken not to roll too much with a vibratory roller because it is possible to overcompact. Too much force can damage the HMA surface, crushing aggregate particles.

Impact Spacing

The vibratory roller should always be moving when the vibration system is enabled. As the drum vibrates and the roller is moving, the drum is lifting and dropping on the mat, creating small impacts in a uniform pattern. This pattern is difficult to see except at night when the light angles are low. The general rule of thumb for impact spacing is 10-14 impacts per foot (33-46 impacts per meter). This allows the entire surface to be impacted as the roller travels in vibratory mode. A number of manufacturers have indicators at the operator’s station to indicate whether the roller is being operated properly. If the frequency of the roller is known, it can be divided by the desired impact spacing to establish the speed of the roller. For example, if a roller can develop 3500 vibrations per minute and a 10 impact-per-foot impact spacing is desired, the optimum speed would be 3500/10 = 350 feet per minute (107 meters per minute). In summary, impact spacing, vibration, and amplitude can be used to adjust the compactive effort of a vibratory roller. Slowing the forward speed of the roller decreases the impact spacing, causing the number of impacts per foot to increase as shown in Figure 9.8.

9.3.4 Guidelines for Choosing a Roller

Any type or combination of rollers can be used to compact HMA mixes. The key to roller selection is efficiency and effectiveness. A general rule of thumb is that lighter rollers are better suited to more tender mixes placed in thin layers, and heavier rollers are better suited to thicker, stiffer layers. A light roller on an interstate highway would be neither efficient nor effec- tive. Wider rollers can cover the mat quickly and are more efficient and effective on mainline highways, but their use in a parking lot with many turns and slope changes would be ineffective.

Leveling Courses—Uneven Bases

Pneumatic-tire rollers are very useful when compacting leveling courses over uneven surfaces or when placing variable-depth layers. The action of the tires allows for compaction into the uneven areas that a wide steel- drum might bridge over.

Most rolling patterns are described to be set up for constant-width pav- ing or mainline paving. Often paving is done in irregular areas, curved sections, cul-de-sac, or parking areas. Static rollers are common in these areas, aS are pneumatic and vibratory rollers. Again, a combination of roll- ers generally works best. Split-drum steel rollers can make tighter turns without tearing or cutting the mat.

Statistics-Based Specifications

These types of specifications are based on how uniform and how close to the target all the density tests are. Monitoring density is vital, and adjusting the roller pattern as conditions change will ensure the best possible specifica- tion with a statistics-based specification such as Percent Within Limits (PWL).

9.3.5 New Technology

Perhaps the most significant development in recent years has been the increase in frequency available for “highway class” vibratory rollers. Since 2000, most roller manufacturers have developed large tandem vibra- tory rollers capable of operating at frequencies of over 3000 vibrations per minute (50 hertz); some can operate at 4000 vibrations per minute (67 hertz) or higher. This permits a single roller to cover a much larger area while maintaining the minimum recommended impact spacing.

There have been substantial advances in equipment technology in the compaction area, and this trend appears to be growing. Advances in sen- sory and vibratory systems have resulted in the development of rollers capable of automatically adjusting the amplitude based on the stiffness of the HMA pavement. As the mix becomes denser (also stiffer) through the compaction process, several manufacturers have equipment that adjusts the intensity and direction of the amplitude to maximize efficiency. These concepts and equipment were first developed for soil and embankment compaction, and are often called Intelligent Compaction (C).

Rollers may have built-in temperature sensors to help the operator better understand the mix as it cools. Technology exists to monitor and record the stiffness of the pavement in relation to the location of the roller and to provide printouts or color displays of areas needing additional coverage. Advances in pneumiatic-tire technology have yielded tires that HMA doesn’t stick to and stiffer tires that help focus the energy into the compaction process.

Vibratory pneumatic rollers have been developed to harness the advan- tages of both types of vibration and pneumatic-tire compaction technology. Continuing advances in compaction equipment seem certain.

4 Rolling Procedures

The goal of the compaction process is to achieve the required density in as few coverages as necessary while preserving the smoothness and tex- ture created by the paver. Rolling should start as soon as possible after the HMA has been spread and can support the weight of the rollers without lateral distortion.

9.4.1 Test Strip

A test strip should be built at the beginning of every project for each mix or thickness to determine the proper roller pattern. The test strip should be built at the same paving speed at which normal operation is anticipated. The in-place density is checked after each coverage, using a nondestructive gauge (nuclear or non-nuclear). A relationship between number of coverages and density is determined while monitoring the temperature of the mixture. This process helps determine the optimum number of coverages for each roller in the rolling train and estimate the dimensions of rolling zones for each roller. As shown in Figure 9.9, density increases with each coverage, then peaks. The peak is relatively flat with static steel-wheel rollers but very sharp with vibratory rollers. Care must be taken to not go beyond the peak with vibratory rollers because the density will decrease due to the mix being damaged.

Subsequently, during the compaction process, those involved must be particularly observant and knowledgeable. A qualified quality-control technician using a calibrated density gauge is invaluable in determining the appropriate rolling pattern and adjusting the pattern as needed to ensure that the target density level is achieved.

9.4.2 Determining Roller Requirements

The width of paving should be considered when selecting rollers. If a wide pavement layer is constructed using narrow rollers, the rollers will need to make many passes to completely cover the surface. This takes time during which the mix will cool and differential density can result. Generally, the width of the pavement is no more than three times the width of the roller. Often the contractor selects two wide rollers operating slightly offset from each other so that they effectively cover the width of the mat each time they make a pass over the mat.

9.4.3 Lift Thickness

The thickness of the lift being placed will have an effect on the time available for compaction and on the types of equipment used to compact the HMA pavement. If the lift is less than 1.5 inches (30 millimeters) after rolldown (compaction), it can be compacted with combinations of static steel-wheel rollers, vibratory rollers operating in static mode, and/or pneu- matic rollers. Thin layers tend to cool quickly, requiring attention to the compaction process so that the desired level of density is achieved. Thicker lifts of HMA tend to hold heat longer, allowing more time for compaction, and may require more effort to compact due to the thickness. More rollers may be needed, including vibratory rollers operating at higher amplitude settings, and combinations of vibratory, pneumatic, and static rollers.

9.4.4 General Rolling Hints

Two or more rollers are needed on most projects, other than small jobs such as driveways. During rolling, roller wheels are kept moist with only enough water to avoid picking up material. Do not use petroleum-based fuels or oils on roller drums or pneumatic tires. 9.4.5 Sequence of Roller Operations

The number of rollers required on a project is related to the type of mix, the production rates, and the desired density levels. It is impossible to establish one roller pattern that will achieve density on all mixes. How- ever, the generally accepted sequence has three phases: breakdown or initial rolling, intermediate rolling, and finish rolling. Breakdown rolling provides most of the increase in density during the compaction operation. Intermediate rolling further densifies and seals the surface. Finish rolling removes roller marks and other blemishes left from previous rolling and minimally increases density.

Breakdown Rolling

Breakdown rolling is usually accomplished with steel-wheel rollers. Vibratory or static-weight tandem rollers are recommended. Pneumatic-tire rollers, however, have also been used successfully for breakdown rolling, but normally should be limited to base courses.

The weight of the roller used for breakdown rolling depends to a large degree on the temperature, thickness, and stability of the mix being placed. Generally, a roller weighing 8-12 tonnes (7300-10,900 kilograms) is used for this operation.

In general, it’s best to start the rolling operation on the low side of the mat, which is usually the outside of the lane being paved, and progress toward the high side. The reason is that these HMA mixtures, when hot, are not as stable as current mix types and tend to migrate toward the low side of the mat under the action of the roller. If rolling is started on the high side, this migration is much more pronounced than if rolling progresses from the low side. When adjoining lanes are placed, the same

Vibratory rollers allow for more options than static rollers. Dual-drum vibratory rollers have power to both drums, so direction is not an issue. The best rolling pattern is the one that results in a consistent density that meets the target values. A simple pattern will be more consistently followed than a complicated one.

Intermediate Rolling

Secondary or intermediate rolling should closely follow breakdown roll- ing while the HMA mix is still hot and compactable.

Pneumatic-tire or vibratory tandem rollers may be used for intermediate rolling. Tire contact pressures should be as high as possible without caus- ing displacement of mix that cannot be remedied in the final rolling.

Pneumatic-tire rolling should be continuous after breakdown rolling until all the mix placed has been thoroughly compacted. Turning of pneumatic- tire rollers on the fresh mix should only be permitted where it does not cause scuffing or undue displacement.

Some mixes exhibit what has been termed a tender zone at an interme- diate temperature range of 230°-190°F (110°-88°C). This is most common with coarse-grade mixtures that may retain a slight amount of moisture after production. Rolling on the mix in this temperature zone with a steel drum will cause the mix to laterally displace or be shoved to the point of tearing the mat. If this phenomenon occurs, compaction with steel-wheel rollers should cease until the mixture is sufficiently stable to support the roller without shoving. Pneumatic-tire rollers, which apply less contact pressure than steel-wheel rollers, are less likely to seriously deform the sur- face under these conditions. Additional densification can also be achieved with the finish roller if it is sufficiently heavy and operated slowly.

Vibratory rollers—of proper static weight, vibration frequency, and amplitude—are used to meet required densities with fewer coverages than with static-weight tandem or pneumatic-tire rollers (or combinations of the two). Regardless of the type of rollers used, the rolling pattern should be developed in the same manner as for the initial, or breakdown, rolling. This pattern should be continued until the desired density is obtained.

Finish Rolling

Finish rolling is done primarily for the aesthetic improvement of the surface. It should be accomplished with steel-wheel tandems operating in static mode (without vibration) while the material is still warm enough for removal of roller marks.

Compaction is the last step that will determine the performance of the HMA. There is a seemingly endless variety of roller patterns to accomplish this task. It is a critical function, and finding the right pattern for produc- ing consistent density results while maintaining smoothness and texture is a rewarding and long-lasting effort.

9.4.6 Rolling Joints and Edges

Transverse Joints

Just as a variety of different roller patterns are available, a number of dif- ferent techniques are also available for compacting a transverse joint. Unless there are restrictions on either side of the lane being placed (live traffic, bar- riers, curbs, etc.), most operators like to cross roll the joint with the roller on the previously laid material, except for a 6-inch (150 millimeters) projection of the wheels when using a tandem (see Figure 9.12) and of one rear wheel when using a three-wheel roller. This operation should be repeated with successive passes covering 6-8 inches (150-200 millimeters) of fresh mate- rial until the entire width of a drive wheel is on the new mix.

If lane restrictions prohibit cross rolling, it is possible to roll straight onto the new mat very slowly so that the mix will be pinched down into the joint area instead of being pushed away from it. Other options include pinching approximately 6 inches (150 millimeters) by transverse rolling, and then rolling in straight. The goal is to make a tight, level joint flush with the existing edge. This joint should be checked with a straightedge, typically before, during, and after rolling to ensure that it meets specifications.

Longitudinal Joints

Just as there are options for rolling transverse joints, options for rolling longitudinal joints also exist. The method chosen should be maintained to ensure uniformity. Longitudinal joints should be rolled directly behind the paving operation as illustrated in Figure 9.13.

Hot-side rolling. For lifts under 3 inches, it is best to roll from the hot side and place most of the roller on the hot mat with only about 6 inches (150 millimeters) of the drum overlapping on the adjacent cold mat. This approach places the roller on the uncompacted mat, and the roller is both compacting the mix and the joint at the same time. This is also the only realistic way to roll a longitudinal joint when paving with traffic present in the adjoining lane. This is generally more efficient than cold-side rolling.

Another variant of the hot-side method is holding the roller drum approximately 6 inches (150 millimeters) away from the longitudinal joint on the first pass toward the paver. This leaves a narrow strip of uncom- pacted mix. As the roller reverses direction, the roller drum is moved over to overlap 6 inches (150 millimeters) on the cold mat, trapping the narrow strip under the drum and forcing it into place.

For lifts greater than 3 inches (76 millimeters), the rolling process should start 12-15 inches (300-375 millimeters) from the lower unsupported edge, with subsequent passes covering the interior portion of the mat. Succeeding passes of the roller should then gradually progress toward the edges of the spread. The uncompacted edge provides initial confinement during the first pass, thus minimizing lateral movement of the mix. After the central portion of the spread has been compacted, the mix will have cooled slightly and can sup- port the roller. This will allow the edge to be compacted with minimal lateral movement.

Cold-side rolling. A method that is sometimes used is to place the roller on the previously placed lane so that not more than 6 inches (150 millime- ters) rides on the edge of the newly spread material. Here it is operated to pinch and press the uncompacted material into place. The roller should continue to roll along this line, its position being shifted gradually across the joint until a thoroughly compacted, neat joint is obtained. Vibration should not be used when the roller is operating on a cold mat. This is normally the least efficient rolling method because the hot mat is cooling while the roller is mostly operating on the cold mat.

Rolling Edges

When rolling a longitudinal joint or HMA edge against an existing con- crete gutter or curb lip, the roller should not span the joint in a manner that puts part of the roller weight on the gutter. By bridging the roller on the PCC curb, insufficient compaction along the HMA edge may result. One common method to obtain good HMA compaction along an existing curb is to require the edge of the finished pavement to have an elevation slightly higher than the adjacent gutter. This ensures that the full compac- tive force of the roller will be on the HMA.

9.5 Troubleshooting

This section is not meant to be an exhaustive summary of all issues relative to compaction. Rather, it will cover the following problem areas: pickup, low density, erratic density, and roller marks. A key to successful compaction is the use of an experienced quality-control technician with a density gauge and infrared thermometer. With these two tools and the authority to work with the roller operators to adjust the rolling pattern as needed, a quality-control technician is a major asset to a paving operation.

9.5.1 Pickup

Mix pickup leaves scars on the mat and is not aesthetically pleasing. Pickup is defined as mix sticking to the drums or tires of the roller. * Pick up on steel drums is typically due to a lack of water-spray sys- tems and scrapers/pads on the equipment. e Pick up on pneumatic-tire rollers is typically caused by tires that are too cold, are not properly inflated, or lack pads/scrapers.

9.5.2 Low Density

In brief, if the target density is not being achieved, the operation needs to be adjusted. Merely speeding up the rollers is counterproductive and will actu- ally lower density. In fact, slowing the paving and rolling operation should be among the first things to consider, along with the following:

° Establish a new test strip to evaluate the paving-and-rolling process.

e Check the temperature of the mix upon delivery and ensure that it is adequate to achieve density and uniformity between loads.

* Check the calibration of the density gauge against a core to ensure that the gauge is properly calibrated.

° Make sure the paver has not increased speed because the rollers will speed up to catch up.

° Ensure that the rollers have been and continue to be compacting using the established rolling pattern at the proper speed, frequency, and amplitude.

° Check the environmental conditions to determine whether something significant has changed (air temperature, cloud cover, wind speed, shade) because this can affect the time available for compaction.

° Consider shortening the rolling zone.

Increase the number of coverages and recheck density.

9.5.3 Erratic Density

Erratic density is more difficult to troubleshoot because, by its nature, it is not uniform. In addition to ensuring all the items listed in the previous section have been checked, one should look for changing conditions and erratic roller operation. Changes in density, temperature, mix characteris- tics, paver speed, etc. should be monitored.

9.5.4 Roller Marks

Roller marks are any marks or deficiencies caused by the rollers.

* Pneumatic-tire rollers can “pick up” if the tires are not hot, properly inflated, and clean.

Rollers sitting behind a stopped paver will leave marks in the mat that will be almost impossible to remove. If a roller has to stop, it must stop in an area where the mix has cooled.

¢ If a roller is turned too sharply while it is moving, the edge of the drum can cut or tear the mat.

° If marks are still present after the finish roller has completed, the finish roller is either too light or the time between intermediate and finish rolling was too great (mix cooled too much).

° Poorly maintained equipment, such as worn or pitted drums, can imprint patterns in the mat.

The key to uniform density throughout the compaction operation is keeping track of the little details and monitoring conditions. Compacting hot mix asphalt is not difficult, what is challenging is doing it consistently all the time.

Windrow Paving

Windrow paving places the HMA in a longitudinal windrow directly on the grade in front of the paver as it arrives on the project, and as with the MTD, this surge aids in achieving a nonstop paving operation. An elevat- ing windrow device or pickup machine (see Figure 8.9) in front of the paver picks up the material and places it in the hopper. The quantity of HMA on the grade is controlled by a dump operator as the hauling unit is unloaded and the size of the windrow is adjusted according to the level of material in the hopper. A well trained crew can successfully pave for hours, producing a high quality HMA pavement without stopping.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Windrow Paving

The advantage to windrow paving is the continuous aspect of the operation, allowing the paver to lay a consistent high quality HMA pave- ment. The problems can be heat loss into the grade, the inability to size the windrow, the inability to cleanly pick up the material off the grade, and finally vibrations caused by the powered pickup machine that affect the paver’s performance. All these issues can be overcome with proper attention to detail. A shorter windrow will reduce heat loss; proper lay- down. crew training about windrow sizing will solve quantity problems; and a properly adjusted pickup machine, isolated from the paver, will pick up the material cleanly without adversely affecting the paver. Windrow paving operations are usually confined to warm dry climates, where the chance of a surprise rainstorm cooling the material in the exposed windrow is minimal.

8.3.5 Visual Inspection of the HMA

Inspection of the HMA as it flows from the hauling units into the MTD, into the windrow in front of the paver, or directly into the paver hopper will help determine the consistency (temperature, gradation, aggregate coating, asphalt content) of the HMA. Observing how the HMA is laying in the hauling unit reveals load inconsistencies. Watching the material as it breaks out of the hauling unit is also important—one load may break uniformly as the bed is raised while the next load might break in larger chunks or move all at once into the hopper, windrow, or MTD. Uncoated aggregate is easily identified and shows that the material has not been properly mixed during production. A visible fume as the HMA is moving out of the hauling unit is a normal condition. But if the level of fumes is inconsistent from one load to the next, the HMA temperature might be fluctuating and should be checked for compliance. The temperature of the HMA should be kept to +25°F (£15°C). Finally, segregation should be noted, especially at the beginning and at the end of each load. If end-of- load segregation is apparent, the loadout techniques at the HMA plant should be adjusted to reduce or eliminate end-of-load segregation.

8.4.1 Asphalt Paving Machine—Basics

An HMA paver is a self contained, self-propelled pavement finishing machine made up of three basic units: the tractor, a material handling sys- tem, and the screed. The tractor portion of the asphalt paver contains the power unit that provides the power needs of all three components, plus the drive train that propels the machine. The material handling system receives the HMA material and moves it from the hopper to the spreading augers in front of the screed. The screed levels and profiles the material being laid, provides some initial density, and seals the surface ready to be compacted to the final specified density as described in Chapter 9, “HMA Compaction.”

identification of Components of the Paver

° Push rollers. These rollers mounted on the front of the paver push on the hauling unit tires as the HMA is being transferred from the hauling unit to the paver hopper.

8.4.2 Types of Tractors

Only two types of drive mechanisms power today’s asphalt pavers—either a track or a rubber-tire propulsion system (see Figures 8.12 and 8.13).

The selection of either a rubber-tire or a track-mounted paver is up to the individual user. Either unit will provide the uniform forward speed required to lay smooth asphalt pavements. A track system provides a lower ground pressure with greater traction, while a rubber-tire paver has greater mobility and a lower operating cost. The mobility of track machines has been improved by the introduction of a continuous rubber-track system. On rubber-tire pavers, both drive tires should be inspected each morning for cuts, punctures, and other damage that might result in a flat tire dur- ing the work shift. To achieve maximum traction from drive tires, most are filled with calcium chloride or some other weight-producing material. The air pressure must be closely monitored and checked with special pressure gauges and should never vary by more than 2 pounds per square inch (13.8 kilopascals) between the two drive tires. Caution must be exercised when checking the air pressure in liquid-filled tires to avoid the liquid’s spraying out and coming in contact with the skin and eyes. Track systems on track pavers should also be checked daily to ensure that they properly align, have correct tension, and have no gouges in the track pads.

Material Handling System

The material handling system consists of the mechanisms used to move the material from the hopper to the augers in front of the screed. Slat con- veyers are used on most pavers to move the material through the paver. One paver manufacturer uses four counterrotating augers in place of slat conveyers to remix the HMA to minimize segregation as the material moves from the hopper to the augers. The volume of HMA required to match the width, depth, and speed of placement is precisely controlled either by flow gates mounted at the rear of the hopper or by varying the speed of the slat conveyer system. When flow gates are not used, the drives of both the auger and the conveyer are separated and controlled independently to transfer the correct volume of HMA. Split systems use an additional mate- rial sensor to control the volume of material carried by the conveyer into the auger area. As the HMA flows into the area in front of the screed, the augers move it transversely to the outer ends of the screed. The material sensors at the outer ends of the screed control the amount of HMA in this area by speeding up, slowing down, or stopping the rotation of the augers. The auger systems on most pavers can be raised or lowered to provide clearance for loading and unloading the paver from equipment hauling trailers. This vertical adjustment can also be used when paving deeper lifts of HMA. The augers should always be 1% or 2 inches @8 to 50 mil- limeters) above the HMA pavement thickness being placed. When paving is done with a screed wider than the basic screed, auger extensions and material confining plates known as tunnel extensions should be added to uniformly carry the HMA the full width of the screed. Auger tunnel exten- sions (slide-out or bolt-on) are located in front of the augers when running at extended widths (14 feet [4 meters] or more). Ideally, the auger tunnel extensions should be 12-18 inches (0.3-0.5 meters) from the edger plate of the screed to minimize the chance that the material will segregate.

The volume of material in front of the screed is automatically controlled by material-control sensors that signal the material-feed system to deliver the proper amount of HMA to the front of the screed. These sensors should be properly positioned to make sure that the volume is not allowed to fluctuate beyond operational limits in achieving a quality mat.

Utilization of the Material-Control Sensors

The material-control sensors precisely control the volume of material (head of material) in front of the screed. The augers on the right and on the left are independent of each other and are controlled by separate material- control sensors. The sensors control the movement of material by starting and stopping and running at variable speeds in order to furnish the correct volume of material to match the paver’s width, depth, and speed.

To operate properly, the sensors must index from the live head of material that is moving constantly to fill in an empty area in front of the screed. Paver manufacturers use different types of sensors and mount them in different positions to monitor and control the flow of material. Vibratory and Tamper Bar Systems

Vibrators on the asphalt screed provide some initial density. The com- pactive force, which is generated by offset weights as they are spun at high rates around a central shaft, is applied to the HMA as it passes under the screed. Most vibrators on today’s pavers are adjustable. The vibrators should not be used when paving a thin lift because fracturing of the aggre- gates might result. A nuclear density gauge can be used to check behind the screed at various vibrator settings to evaluate the density. If density does not increase, the vibrators should be turned off. This trial-and-error process helps establish the correct setting for each combination of HMA mixture and lift thickness and should be done on each new mixture that is required to meet a density specification.

Some screeds have tamper bars at the leading edge of the screed that serve a dual purpose. They strike off the HMA as it passes under the screed and give a greater initial density than a vibratory screed. The only shortcoming to the tamper-bar system is that higher rates of paving speed do not give the tamper bars enough time to tuck the material under the screed. This can result in a torn HMA pavement surface or one with a dragging texture. The tamper bar should protrude below the leading edge of the screed just enough to catch a fingernail on it, or %—42 inch (0.4 to 0.8 millimeter). Screed Heating Systems

At the start of a paving shift, the screed is much colder than the HMA being laid. To keep the asphalt material from sticking to the colder metal of the screed, it must be preheated. Two types of heating systems are used to pre- heat the screed before each day’s paving: diesel and electrical. Diesel burners are simple and easy to use, but they require proper operational procedures so that excess fumes are not produced when air and fuel in the burner chamber are out of balance. Electrical heating systems have heating elements immedi- ately above the screed plate that are surrounded by special heat transfer oil that helps heat the screed uniformly without hotspots. This system minimizes fumes and provides a more even heat across the entire screed plate. Electrical heating systems operate in a couple of different modes. In one, the screed is heated to operational temperature and the heating system shuts off. The other heats the screed to the desired temperature and cycles on and off to maintain this temperature throughout the paving operation.

Free-Floating Screed

Free-floating screeds (see Figure 8.16) have been a standard feature of HMA pavers since they were first introduced in the 1930s. The basic prin- ciple of the free-floating screed is that, when it is pulled into the material deposited in front of it by the augers, it will automatically ride up or down seeking the level where the path of its flat screed plate is parallel to the direction of pull. At this point, the forces (listed below), which act upon the screed, will be in balance and the screed will continue to lay a uniform mat thickness until one or more of the forces change.

Angle of Attack

The angle of attack is the angle of the screed in relation to the paving grade. The screed pivots about the screed pivot point. The angle can be adjusted by the depth-screw assembly or by raising or lowering the screed tow point. The depth-screw is adjusted manually by the screed operator. Tow-point changes are generated by automatic grade and slope control. The angle of attack creates an extrusion angle under the screed; once the extrusion angle can support its weight, the screed is free to float at that average depth. Pavers have a natural leveling effect, placing asphalt thin- ner over the highs and filling in the lows of the base. Increasing the angle of attack allows more material to flow under the screed, causing the screed to rise until it reaches the elevation that finds equilibrium of the forces that act upon the screed. The paver will continue to place HMA at the newly established depth until one or more of the forces acting on the screed is changed. Reducing the angle of attack results in less material passing under the screed, and the screed will drop until the new depth is reached and a new equilibrium of forces is established.

Head of Material

The head of material is the depth of material being evenly spread in front of the screed by the auger. Controlling the head of material is the most important factor in laying a smooth HMA pavement. The head of material is affected by the volume of the HMA in the auger chamber. Once the head of material has been established, it must be maintained within a tolerance of +1 inch at all times. Starving the screed of material will result in the screed settling and will cause a dip in the pavement. Immediately placing a large amount of HMA in front of the screed will guarantee a bump as the screed attempts to crawl over the excess material deposited in its path. Ideally, the head of material should be maintained at a height that exposes one half of the auger and not vary by more than +1 inch (25 millimeters).

Paving Speed

Nonstop paving at a uniform forward speed results in the highest- quality and smoothest pavements and should be the goal of every paving crew. Changing the paving speed will change the angle of attack on the screed, causing a corresponding change in the mat thickness. Manual adjust- ments to offset this depth change must be made with the depth screws. When equipped with automatic controls, these devices immediately sense the change in depth caused by speed changes and make the appropriate adjustments to maintain the required depth. Screed Weight

The screed weight is usually translated into pounds per square inch (kilograms per square meter), or the vertical force applied by the screed into the HMA mixture being laid. HMA Stiffness (Loose)

A change in the temperature of the HMA or the gradation of the aggre- gates can result in a change in the stability or stiffness of the loose HMA during paving. This change in stiffness will cause the screed to either rise or fall as it reacts to a change in vertical force and seeks to rebalance all of the forces acting upon it.

Screed Extensions

Screed extensions are rigidly mounted, fixed-width, or hydraulically powered variable-width attachments that extend beyond the width of the main screed to facilitate paving widths that are wider than the basic machine width. Both types of extensions provide full screed plates with heat and compaction similar to the cross section of the main screed. This allows the HMA placed by the extension to have the same characteristics in terms of texture, smoothness, and precompaction as the material placed by the main screed. Both types of extensions can be angled differently than the main screed, allowing adjacent lanes and shoulders to be paved at a different cross-slope than the main screed.

Variable-Width Screeds

With a variable-width screed, the paving width can be changed with the push of a button while still maintaining a uniform surface across the entire HMA mat being placed (see Figure 8.17). Extensions on the variable-width screeds can be either front- or rear-mounted and will require different adjustments to achieve a quality HMA pavement. The extensions can usu- ally be sloped positively or negatively at the outer edges of the main screed so the adjacent lane(s) can be paved at the same time as the mainline.

Fixed-width screeds with rigidly fixed extensions are used for extended- width paving when a fixed width is to be paved without any variations in the required width (see Figure 8.18). Fixed screeds begin with a basic 8- or 10-foot (2.4- or 3.0-meter) screed, and rigidly fixed extensions of various widths are added to extend one or both sides of the screed to the desired paving width. The structural stabil- ity of the fixed-width screed offers the ability to pave much wider widths and maintain better control of the desired profile. Screeds with rigidly fixed extensions cannot change paving width unless the paving operation is halted long enough to add or detach the extension(s) needed to change the required paving width. Rigidly fixed extended screeds are best suited to long stretches of fixed paving widths.

8.4.4 Automatic Screed Controls (Types and Operational Differences)

Automatic Grade Controls

Automatic grade controls take a continuous reading from a known lon- gitudinal reference and transfer it to the paver’s control system, which continually changes the depth of the HMA pavement being placed. The controls are either mechanical or electronic.

The mechanical device uses a spring-loaded rotational potentiometer attached to a wand as a sensor to measure changes in the vertical distance between the reference line and the sensor mounted on the side arm of the paver. In response to these changes, the sensors send a signal propor- tional to the amount and direction of change to the paver’s grade controls. The grade control then actuates the hydraulic cylinder, which makes a corresponding change in the height of the tow point. On a free-floating screed, as the tow point rises or falls, the angle of attack changes and the depth increases or decreases accordingly. The screed changes depth until the distance between the reference and the sensor is reestablished.

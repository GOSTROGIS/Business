Apparent Specific Gravity of Aggregate

Apparent specific gravity (G,,) (Figure 4.4) is the ratio of the mass in air of a unit volume of an impermeable material at a stated temperature to the mass in air of equal density of an equal volume of gas-free distilled water at a stated temperature. In other words, the aggregate apparent specific gravity does not include the volume of the water permeable voids in the aggregate.

Bulk Specific Gravity of Aggregate

Bulk specific gravity (G,) (Figure 4.5) is the ratio of the mass in air of a unit volume of a permeable material (including both permeable and impermeable voids normal to the material) at a stated temperature to the mass in air of equal density of an equal volume of gas-free distilled water at a stated temperature. In other words, the aggregate bulk specific gravity includes the volume of the water permeable voids in the aggregate (often termed the saturated surface dry or SSD volume of the aggregate). The test procedure for determining the bulk specific gravity of the fine and coarse aggregate fractions is discussed in Chapter 3, “Mineral Aggregates and Mixture Additives.”

Maximum Specific Gravity of the HMA Mixture, AASHTO T 209 or ASTM D 2041

Maximum theoretical specific gravity (G,_) is the ratio of the mass in air of a unit volume of the asphalt and aggregate in the mixture at a stated temperature to the mass in air of equal density of an equal volume of gas- free distilled water at a stated temperature. In other words, the maximum theoretical specific gravity (G,,) is the mass of the asphalt and aggregate mixture divided by the volume of the asphalt coated particles.

Test Procedure

There are three basic steps in determining the maximum specific gravity (see Figure 4.6). The loose mix is warmed and separated into loose, indi- vidually coated aggregates. A predetermined mass of the dry loose mix is placed in a metal bowl or calibrated pycnometer and covered with water. A vacuum lid is fitted and secured to the bowl or pycnometer and placed on a vibratory shaker table. A vacuum pump is started and the manometer reading used to determine the proper vacuum adjustment. In designing a paving mixture with a given aggregate, the maximum specific gravities (G,,_) at different asphalt contents are needed to calculate the percent- age of air-voids for each asphalt content. While individual maximum specific gravities can be determined for each asphalt content, the precision of the test is best when the specimen is at, or over the optimum asphalt content. The Asphalt Institute recommends measuring the maximum specific gravity in duplicate or triplicate on individually batched specimens at one trial asphalt content. The end result of the maximum specific gravity test is to determine an accurate G,, (Effective Specific Gravity of Aggregate) as described in the following sec- tion. Many agencies have different procedures to determine G,. The designer is encouraged to be familiar with methods in their respective localities.

Bulk Specific Gravity of the HMA Mixture

The bulk specific gravity of a compacted sample is the mass of aggre- gate and binder divided by the bulk volume (i.e., volume of aggregate, binder, and air). Two methods are used to determine the bulk specific gravity of a mix. The first is the AASHTO T 166 (ASTM D 2726) procedure and the second is the CoreLok® procedure.

Bulk Specific Gravity, AASHTO T 166 or ASTM D 2726 Procedure

The steps for determining the mixture bulk specific gravity are given in this test procedure. After mixing, aging, and compacting the mixture, the mass of the sample is determined in air (dry), while submerged in water, and then in air again after drying the surface (saturated surface dry). The mass of the oven-dry specimen is being determined in Figure 4.8. The next step is to place the specimen in the water bath directly below the scale (not shown) and determine its mass under water. The last step is to deter- mine the mass of the saturated surface dry specimen in air. The saturated surface-dry (SSD) mass is obtained by quickly blotting the sample so that the surface is still wet but not shiny (see Figure 4.9). The bulk specific gravity is the mass of the sample divided by the mass (volume) of water it displaces.

Bulk Specific Gravity, CoreLok® Procedure

The CoreLok® test is conducted by the water-displacement method using saturated surface-dry (SSD) specimens. The SSD method has been proven to work for dense-graded HMA mixtures. A problem may occur with coarse-graded mixtures. These mixes tend to have larger internal air- voids than the conventional mixes, even though the volume of air-voids is the same. Mixes with coarser (or gap-graded) aggregates will have a higher percentage of large aggregate particles. At some overall air volume, which is mix-specific, the large internal air-voids of the coarse aggregate can become interconnected. During the bulk specific gravity testing with the SSD method, water can quickly infiltrate the sample. After removing

3. Superpave HMA Mix Design System

4.3.1 Introduction

In 1987, a five-year FHWA study to improve the procedures for the performance of HMA pavements was conducted. The result was the Superpave (SUPerior PERforming Asphalt PAVEments) system. This sys- tem was developed to give pavement engineers and contractors the tools they needed to improve the performance of HMA pavements subjected to extremes of traffic loading and temperature. The Superpave system as implemented consisted of two interrelated elements: an asphalt binder specification (discussed in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalts”) and a mix design and analysis system. Since the implementation of Superpave in the mid-1990s, research has continued on the development of performance models. The status of that research is summarized in Section 4.8 in this chapter. The following summarizes the Superpave mix design system:

The Superpave mix design procedure consists of:

1. Selecting asphalt and aggregate materials that meet the design criteria 2. Developing several aggregate trial blends that will meet the Superpave gradation requirements

3. Blending the asphalt binder with the aggregate trial blends and short-term oven aging of the mix

4. Compacting the specimens

5. Analyzing the volumetric properties of the mixture (using the procedures described in Section 4.2)

6. Selecting the best aggregate blend as the design aggregate struc-

ture based on the desired performance criteria for the mixture

A detailed discussion is provided in the Asphalt Institute’s SP-2, Superpave Mix Design. The detailed procedures for conducting the calculations associ- ated with Superpave mix design are included in SP-2 and the Mix Design Program, SW-2. This program is written in strict conformance with the SP-2 manual. It is an Excel-based program that allows users to modify the pro- gram components to their needs.

4.3.2 Materials Selection

Asphalt Binder

The asphalt binder to be used in the mix is chosen based on environ- mental and traffic loadings using the procedures described in Chapter 2, “Petroleum Asphalts.”

Mineral Aggregate

During the development of Superpave, pavement experts agreed that certain aggregate characteristics were critical to a well-performing HMA. These characteristics were broken into two categories: consensus proper- ties and source properties. A detailed discussion of these properties and test procedures is included in Chapter 3, “Mineral Aggregates and Mixture Additives.

Consensus Aggregate Properties

The consensus aggregate properties used in the Superpave mix design procedure are the following: coarse aggregate angularity, fine aggregate angularity, flat and elongated particles, and clay content. The criteria for these properties are based on the traffic level and position within the pavement structure. The materials near the pavement surface are subjected to higher stress levels and require materials of higher quality. The criteria should generally be applied to a proposed aggregate blend rather than individual components. The desired consensus properties are detailed in Table 4.2.

Source Aggregate Properties

The source properties used in the Superpave mix design procedure are the following: toughness or Los Angeles Abrasion, sodium or mag- nesium sulfate soundness and deleterious materials. These properties are source specific, and the requirements vary throughout the United States because of the variety of aggregate types available in the various geo- graphical areas. The requirement for a maximum loss value from the Los Angeles Abrasion test will typically range from 35 percent to 45 percent.

Gradation

Superpave utilizes the 0.45 power gradation chart to analyze the agere- gate gradation and to define the maximum density line. The maximum den- sity line represents a gradation where the aggregate particles fit together in their densest possible arrangement. The development and use of this chart is discussed in Chapter 3. Superpave uses the maximum and nominal maximum size of the aggregates to categorize mixtures and define grada- tion requirements. These are defined as follows:

* Maximum size: One sieve size larger than the nominal maximum size

* Nominal maximum size: One sieve size larger than the first sieve to

Select Initial Asphalt Binder Content

When the aggregate blends have been chosen, the next step is to evaluate the trial blends by compacting the specimens and determining the volumetric properties for each trial blend. For each blend, a minimum of two specimens is compacted using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC). The trial asphalt binder content can be estimated based on the designer’s experience with simi- lar materials. If the designer does not have experience with the particular aggre- gate source, the trial binder content can be determined for each trial blend by estimating the specific gravity of the blend and using a calculation procedure detailed in AASHTO R 35. SP-2 lists typical trial binder contents for aggregate blends having a combined bulk specific gravity of approximately 2.65.

Superpave Gyratory Compactor

The Superpave mix design procedure uses the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (see Figure 4.12) to compact the specimens. The basis for the SGC was the Texas gyratory compactor, which was modified to use the compaction principles of a French gyratory compactor. The Texas device accomplished the goal of achieving realistic specimen densification, and it was reasonably portable. Its 6-inch sample diameter (ultimately 150 mil- limeters on an SGC) could accommodate mixtures containing aggregate up to 50 millimeters maximum (37.5 nominal) size. The developers of the Superpave system modified the Texas device by lowering its angle and speed of gyration and adding real-time specimen-height recordation.

A loading mechanism presses against the reaction frame and applies a load to the loading ram to produce a 600-kiloPascals compaction pressure on the specimen. A pressure gauge measures the ram loading to maintain constant pressure during compaction. The SGC mold (see Figure 4.13) has an inside diameter of 150 millimeters, and a base plate in the bottom of the mold provides confinement during compaction. The SGC gyrates at a constant rate of 30 gyrations per minute with the mold positioned at an external compaction angle of 1.25 degrees. The internal angle is calibrated using procedures discussed below.

Specimen height measurement is an important function of the SGC. Using the mass of material placed in the mold, the diameter of the mold, and the specimen height, an estimate of specimen density can be made at any time throughout the compaction process. Specimen density is com- puted by dividing the mass by the volume of the specimen. The height is measured by recording the position of the ram throughout the test. Using these measurements, a specimen’s compaction characteristic is developed. See Figure 4.14.

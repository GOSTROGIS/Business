Significant equipment and material differences exist between the small-scale operation of the laboratory mixing bowl and an asphalt mixing facility, which could lead to the mixture property changes noted. Many causes for volumetric changes in field-produced mixture have been proposed:

¢ Moisture removed more effectively in the laboratory than in the field

° An excessive amount of baghouse fines returned to the mixing process

° Highly absorptive aggregates used. (Field absorption may differ from

laboratory absorption.)

* Poor sampling used to obtain aggregate samples for the laboratory

design

* Nonstandard mix design procedures used to develop the JMF

° Material sources (location and/or strata) changed between the mix

design and field production

° Aggregate degradation and shape changes during plant mixing

* Non-uniform stockpiling of aggregates

The changes that occur in the field-produced mixture are likely due to a combination of some or all of the listed causes in any particular case. Attention to detail in material production and mix design development can reduce the potential effects of many of the factors shown.

Some form of verification that the proposed job-mix formula will produce materials meeting the project specifications will occur on every project. Some agencies require that verifications be performed by the agency labora- tory or by a laboratory different from the one that did the mix design. Other agencies will utilize test strips to validate the job-mix formula. Additional discussion of test strips can be found in Section 10.4.4. Some agencies allow the contractor to make minor changes in the job-mix formula without a new mix design if the changes are made to improve the volumetric properties of the mix. Improvements to volumetric properties in such cases generally mean getting the laboratory air voids closer to the target value.

If there are no specified means to verify the job-mix formula, then it will be done during the initial paving. This creates a higher risk that non- complying or marginal HMA will be produced for a longer period of time before adjustments are made.

10.5.2 Mixture Control

Quality-control test data must be quickly reported and analyzed so that it can be used to improve the consistency and overall quality of the HMA product. The goal is to understand the data and to evaluate whether any adjustments to the plant process or materials processes are warranted. It is necessary to keep thorough records so the quality-control manager can understand the overall production and observe trends that may need fur- ther action. Good mixture control is accomplished by first organizing the data in a meaningful way. Control charts are often used for this. Then the data must be analyzed and understood. Finally, appropriate adjustments to the production operations need to be made.

Control Charts

One way that quality-control data is presented is on control charts (Figure 10.10). Control charts are graphed plots of quality-control data over time. The specification limits may also be plotted. Control charts are useful because they provide a quick visual reference of how close to the specification limits the test data are. They can also be invaluable in spot- ting trends in the data.

Data Analysis

Some data collected for quality-control is straightforward, and corrective action is relatively automatic after receiving the information. An example of this would be measurements of temperature at the batch plant. If the target temperature for mixture leaving the plant is 320°F (160°C), and the quality-control check determines that the temperature is 300°F (149°C), the plant operator can begin adjustments immediately to increase the mix- ing temperature to the target.

Other data collected for quality-control requires careful analysis and con- sideration before corrective action is taken. An example of this would be measurements of laboratory air voids in the mix. 10.5.3 Plant Adjustments

The purpose of quality-control testing for HMA production is to provide plant managers and operators with valuable information they need to control the plant. If the data show that the produced material is deviating from the job-mix formula, the plant personnel should decide what adjustments are needed to bring the material closer to the job-mix formula.

It is important to realize that all HMA plants (in this discussion, aggregate crushing and production equipment are considered part of the plant) will produce material that has a given amount of variation. Some plants will produce materials that are more consistent than others. Under- standing this is important because adjusting the production will not cor- rect normal plant variations.

As an example of this, consider a plant that is operating under an allowable tolerance for the percent passing the No. 200 screen of 3 percent to 7 percent. Assume the average result from quality-control tests is 6 percent passing the No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) and the QC test results range from 4.5 percent to 7.5 percent. No adjustments have been made to the plant up to this point. In this example, the job-mix formula for minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) was 5 percent and the average QC results were 6 percent. Based on this infor- mation, the plant operator should be considering an adjustment that could slightly reduce the minus No. 200, preferably by 1 percent. All the other quality-control data, of course, would have to be reviewed before any adjust- ments are made to be sure that it would not be detrimental to other quality- control test parameters.

Assume in the above example, that the first quality-control test result received was the 4.5 percent minus No. 200 (0.075 millimeter). An immediate

10.6 Placement Quality-Control Data Analysis

The primary quality factors for placement of HMA are compaction, thickness, and smoothness. Placement quality-control, therefore, focuses on these same factors. These three factors are discussed below. Tempera- ture is also discussed because it plays such a vital role in controlling and achieving compaction.

Similar to quality-contro] data for hot mix production at the plant, it can be very useful to summarize the placement quality-control data to get a better overall picture of what is happening during placement and compac- tion. The two key factors measured—smoothness and compaction—are critical to the performance of an asphalt pavement, so understanding and controlling these aspects of the construction operation are very important. Tables 10.7A and 10.7B (metric) show examples of a quality-control data summary for HMA placement. For complete quality-control documenta- tion, summaries should be compiled and given to the quality-control man- ager on a regular basis. For interpretation and analysis of the data, it is useful to get an overall understanding of what is happening on the project and then to evaluate the details of particular issues more specifically.

10.6.1 Temperature

Temperature is perhaps the most critical variable in HMA compaction. The temperature of the mix is, therefore, very important to those respon- sible for compaction. One difficulty that the compaction crews have is that the HMA goes through so many other operations before it is ready to be compacted. The temperature is affected by each operation, and the com- paction equipment operators and foremen have little influence on them. Good quality-control, which includes temperature readings at all phases of production and delivery, will help provide HMA at the appropriate compaction temperatures to the rollers. Some quality-control specifica- tions require temperature readings to be recorded at many points along the way and reported each day. Specifications for a project using a batch plant, for example, may require that temperature readings be recorded for the asphalt cement prior to mixing, the aggregate after heating, the mixture immediately after mixing, and at delivery to the paving machine. With this kind of data stored for each day’s production, it would be easy to determine the root cause of temperature problems if they occurred on a particular day. More importantly, it would be easy to establish a correc- tive action plan to get the temperatures where they need to be for proper compaction.

10.6.2 Compaction Control Methods

The goal of compacting a hot mix asphalt pavement is to achieve an optimum air void content and provide a smooth, uniform surface. The resulting in-place air void content of the HMA is in many ways the single most important factor that affects performance of the mixture throughout the life of the pavement. The activities involved in the proper design, pro- duction, placement, and compaction of the asphalt mixture are combined to achieve the in-place density of the HMA pavement and to ultimately determine whether the pavement will perform as expected.

The key points here are that controlling compaction is very important and that compaction is affected by many factors other than those spe- cifically measured during placement of the mixture. This means that low compaction problems on a given project may not be resolved simply by adjusting the rolling pattern or the temperatures. The quality-control] man- ager and project superintendent may need to analyze all the quality-control data, including that from the mixture production, to address compaction problems.

Of course, in addressing a compaction problem, the paving crew should first review their own operations and procedures for the placement and compaction operation, paying particular attention to temperatures, disci- plined adherence to good rolling technique, and consistent and tested roll- ing patterns. The compactability of the asphalt mixture provided should also be evaluated.

A variety of methods are available to control the quality of the com- paction for HMA, and those methods will be discussed in more detail below. These methods are usually driven by specification, however, so the quality-control will be based on the specified methods for acceptance of compaction. The quality-control efforts should be centered on establishing and documenting compliance with the actual specifications.

in-Place Density/Compaction Control Measurements

A common control method for compaction is to simply measure the in- place density. These measured densities will be compared with a laboratory density (the specified reference density), and a percentage of the labora- tory density will be calculated. Nuclear density measurements are the most common form of quality-control density testing. Non-nuclear devices using electromagnetic waves to measure the dielectric constant in the pavement materials have been developed and are gaining popularity for quality-control density testing. Information on comparisons of nuclear and these non-nuclear devices can be found in the reference list. In some instances, quality-control testing for density is done using cores; however, the use of cores imparts a delay in the results. Cores should always be used, however, to correlate and/or calibrate the quality-control test methods selected, especially if cores are to be used for acceptance.

Rolling Patterns

Most paving contractors will create defined “rolling patterns” for a par- ticular placement on the first day of paving. Nuclear density test data can play a key role in developing these rolling patterns. A rolling pattern is defined as the number and sequence of successive passes of compac- tion equipment over an asphalt pavement. The rolling pattern is generally adjusted until the desired compaction is achieved. It is a trial and error process. Details of compaction principles and rolling procedures can be found in Chapter 9, “Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction.”

In order to provide meaningful information to the quality-control man- ager, the quality-control density testing must be done at many points in the rolling operation. Rolling patterns are established on the first day of paving and ideally done during construction of a test strip. The test strip will provide calibration of the nuclear gauge with cores cut from the pave- ment, and the rolling pattern may need to be adjusted. The following example describes how the quality-control testing is used in the develop- ment of a compaction control-rolling pattern.

Rolling Pattern Example

The equipment that will be used for the rolling pattern must be the same as will be used during construction. For this example, the compac- tion equipment consists of a double-drum vibratory compactor for the breakdown rolling, a heavy pneumatic compactor for the intermediate rolling, and a static steel-wheel compactor for the finish rolling.

After that coverage, the quality- control technician takes a nuclear density test and marks the location of the test on the pavement. This is done by drawing a rectangle outlining the bottom of the gauge on the pavement so the gauge can be returned to the same location and orientation for subsequent tests. 10.6.3 Mixture Compactability

Some mixtures are easier to compact than others. It can be useful when controlling the paving operation to understand the effect of the mixture and the variables that affect compactability.

Experience says that coarse-graded mixtures with low fines tend to require significant effort to compact. Success with such mixtures can be achieved with excellent compaction methods and good quality-control measures.

Two other mixture parameters that should be of particular interest to the paving crew or paving subcontractor are air voids and lift thickness. The following sections describe these two mixture properties and how they can affect compaction. These two items should be carefully planned and moni- tored with respect to their impact on the compactability of HMA.

Laboratory Air Voids

If a mixture is produced with laboratory compacted air voids that are higher than the target, additional compactive effort in the field will be required to achieve density. Suppose, for example, that a project has an established roll- ing pattern that achieves about 94 percent compaction when the mixture is produced at 4 percent laboratory voids. If the laboratory air voids of the mixture suddenly increased by 1.5 percent (to 5.5 percent), then the same rolling pattern in the field would yield something on the order of 1.5 percent less (about 92 to 93 percent).

When changes in laboratory air voids create a need for additional com- pactive effort, the establishment of a new or revised rolling pattern may be needed. Even if no air void limits are specified for production, the laboratory compacted voids must be controlled to produce a compactable mix.

Compaction problems can occur as well if the air voids are low, espe- cially in projects with statistical acceptance requirements. In those cases, fluctuations in air voids will cause fluctuations in compaction and can affect pay factors that include standard deviations. Good consistency in laboratory air voids of the produced mix is essential for maintaining good control of compaction.

Lift Thickness

Lift thicknesses must be well planned, and compactability should be the driving factor in that decision. If the desired lift thickness is less than the rec- ommended values, then a mixture with a smaller nominal maximum aggre- gate size should be used so that proper compaction can be achieved. See Chapter 9 for recommended lift thicknesses based on nominal maximum ageregate size.

These coarse-graded Superpave mixtures require thicker lifts than fine-graded mixtures, and they can tend to require more compaction energy than finer mixtures. In order to control and achieve required compaction, proper lift thicknesses must be maintained.

It is also important to realize that adjustments to compaction opera- tions are required any time the lift thickness changes. New lift thicknesses necessitate revisions to nuclear density calibrations and rolling patterns, as well as reconsideration of timing and temperature.

10.6.4 Thickness and Spread Rates

Quality-control testing for pavement thickness is accomplished using depth gauges (Figure 10.13) that are pushed into the HMA after the paver has placed the hot mix but before the compaction equipment has rolled it down. For most dense-graded mixes, a rule of thumb is that the thickness of the uncompacted hot mix should be 1.25 times the final thickness. The actual decrease in thickness could be more accurately determined for a given mix design using core thicknesses measured and compared with the initial depth gauge thicknesses.

Thickness is also measured by cutting cores from the completed pave- ment surface. Core thicknesses are considered the most accurate method, but because they are taken after the work is complete, they do not lend themselves well to quality-control purposes. These values could be used to adjust thicknesses for subsequent paving days, but the paving equip- ment is usually controlled with thickness readings behind the paver with a depth gauge.

In measuring and adjusting lift thicknesses, it is important to consider that paver screeds are self-leveling. They are designed to fill in the low spots and cut down the high spots, making it impossible to lay a uniform lift thickness on an uneven surface. If the depth checks were exactly equal all of the time, the new surface would have the same rough profile as the underlying pavement or base. In reality, many quality-control “depth checks” will be made behind the paver, but these checks should lead to very few adjustments to the paving machine. Consideration of this natural variation in lift thickness, especially for overlays on rough pavements, is necessary for evaluating core thicknesses for accep- tance purposes as well.

Some agencies specify spread rates (yield) in lieu of thickness. Spread rates can be checked as a quality-control measure by calculating the weight of mate- rial placed over a measured area. If measurement of spread rates is required as part of the quality-control plan, a technician can keep a log of beginning and ending stations for each truck delivered, along with the weight of each load. On this log, running values of spread rates can be calculated and reported to the paving superintendent on a periodic basis throughout the day.

10.6.5 Pavement Smoothness

Pavement smoothness for quality-control purposes is generally measured with a straightedge. Typical acceptance specifications require hand mea- surements with a 10-foot (3 meter) straightedge, a rolling straightedge (also called the California profilograph) or, inertial profilometers which measure the International Roughness Index (IRD.

Typical requirements with a 10-foot straightedge are that there be no bumps or dips greater than ¥% inch when compared with the two outer edges of the straightedge. Measurements are generally taken in both the longitu- dinal and the transverse direction. The straightedge is laid on the pavement surface, and the maximum deviation of the surface from this straightedge is measured and recorded. These measurements can be taken while compac- tion is ongoing, and feedback can be provided to the paver and the roller operators for making adjustments.

The rolling straightedge, or profilograph, is used by a large number of states to measure the initial roughness of new construction and overlay proj- ects. These profilographs use wheels at each end of the straightedge as the contact point on pavement surface, and deviations in the pavement profile are measured at the center point between the wheels. The devices can gener- ally be used in lengths of 10-30 feet 3-9 meters). The measured deviations are accumulated and reported as roughness in inches per mile. Profilograph measurements and readings can be taken while compaction is ongoing .or immediately afterward. Because profilograph testing can provide quick feed- back to the paving crews, such testing can be useful as a quality-control test. Some contractors will also use profilograph testing to evaluate the smooth- ness of a pavement before placement of the surface course. The cost of pro- filograph testing and subsequent grinding to improve smoothness could be inexpensive when compared with potential incentive payments.

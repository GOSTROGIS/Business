10.7 Acceptance Point and Test Results

Acceptance testing is usually provided for the following properties in HMA project specifications:

° Asphalt content

° Aggregate gradation

* Compaction

® Volumetric properties (air voids, VMA, VFA)

° Aggregate characteristics (CAA, FAA, F&E, SE, etc.)

° Ride quality

° Pavement thickness or spread rate

The criterion for acceptance is generally the same as the parameters tested for quality-control. This makes sense because the purpose of quality- control is to control production so the final product meets the acceptance criteria. Because of differences in the use of quality-control and acceptance tests, however, there are different sampling points and differences in test procedures in some cases. The following sections discuss the sampling and testing methods commonly used for acceptance, giving emphasis to those that may be different from the quality-control methods discussed in Section 10.3.

10.7.1 Asphalt Content and Gradation

The point of acceptance for asphalt content and aggregate grada- tion is normally derived from the finished HMA product. HMA samples are typically obtained from the roadway or the delivery truck. Refer to Sections 10.2.3 and 10.2.4 for more detailed descriptions of these sam- pling methods.

Asphalt binder content may be determined by solvent extraction, nuclear, or ignition oven testing. The use of the ignition binder content method is increasingly becoming the method of choice for acceptance testing. This test provides reasonably accurate test results if it is properly calibrated for the specific mix design materials being used, and it allows the use of the recovered aggregate materials to be tested for gradation.

10.7.2 Aggregate Angularity (Fractured Faces)

The point of acceptance for aggregate angularity is normally in the road- way or after blending of the aggregate stockpiles. HMA samples from the

10.7.3 Volumetric Properties (Voids, VMA, VFA)

The sampling point for acceptance of volumetric properties is normally at the point of delivery on-site or in delivery trucks at the plant. HMA samples are generally taken from the roadway behind the paving machine, from plant sampling devices, or from the delivery trucks prior to leaving the plant. Refer to Sections 10.2.3 and 10.2.4 for more detailed descrip- tions of theses sampling methods.

Volumetric properties generally refer to one or more of the following three test parameters: 1) Laboratory air voids; 2) voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA); or 3) voids filled with asphalt (VFA). These properties are calculated using values derived from several different tests. Labora- tory air voids require bulk specific gravity results from the laboratory compacted specimen and the maximum theoretical specific gravity. Calcu- lations of VMA require the following data: laboratory compacted bulk spe- cific gravity of the mix; the bulk specific gravity of the blended aggregate; and the asphalt content as a percentage of total mix. Calculations for VFA use the air voids and the VMA values.

10.7.5 Thickness

The acceptance point for thickness is the finished pavement. It is almost exclusively measured by cutting cores from the completed pavement sur- face. The cored specimens taken for the compaction testing described above are measured for thickness before testing for unit weight.

Some agencies specify spread rates in lieu of thickness. Spread rates can be checked as either an acceptance criteria or as a quality-control mea- sure by calculating the weight of material placed over a measured area. For example, if the paver is placing a 14-foot (4.3-meter) wide mat and it spreads a 22.0-ton load over a length of 100 feet (30.5 meters), the spread rate would be 31.4 pounds per square foot. Assume the target-spread rate was 30.2 lb/ft? (147.5 kg/m?) to achieve an average design thickness of 2% inches (63.5 millimeters). The actual spread rate in this example is 4 percent over the target-spread rate.

Agencies do not like to have thinner pavement sections than are speci- fied in the design, so a typical specification would require that spread rates fall between the target and 10 percent over the target.

Spread-rate specifications are especially useful for overlay projects that are seeking smoothness improvements on the roadway. Paving equipment, by its nature, provides significant leveling characteristics as it places the asphalt thicker in low areas and thinner in high areas. This improves ride quality but creates variations in pavement thickness. The more rough the existing pavement, the more variation in pavement thickness.

10.7.6 Ride Quality

Pavement smoothness is the leading factor in the traveling public’s per- ception of road quality. In addition, rough or uneven pavements can have an adverse affect on fuel usage, roadway safety, and vehicle maintenance costs. Smoother pavements can also improve pavement durability, thereby extending the life of the pavement.

Many agencies use pavement smoothness as an acceptance criteria, and many offer incentives to contractors for building smoother pavements. Ride quality is generally specified and tested with one of the following four methods: Straightedge measurements, Profilograph, Response Type Road Roughness Measuring Systems (RTRRMS), or Inertial profilometers. All four methods have been used by agencies for acceptance testing of roadway smoothness. Straightedge and Profilograph measurements have also been used for quality-control testing. Each of the four pavement smoothness measuring methods are discussed briefly below.

Straightedge

Straightedge measurements are perhaps the oldest form of measuring pavement smoothness, but are generally included in even the most mod- ern specifications, and they may be required for both quality-control and acceptance testing. Quality-control checks of the pavement surface can be made with a straightedge during the compaction operation, provid- ing immediate feedback to the paving-machine and compactor operators. Acceptance criteria using straightedge measurements generally include requirements for joints, pavement edges, and miscellaneous paved areas that are not accurately measured by other smoothness equipment.

Handheld straightedges range in length from 8 to 15 feet (2.4 to 4.6 meters), and rolling straightedges can be 30 feet (9.2 meters) in length or more.

Profilograph

Profilographs measure the longitudinal profile of pavements. They are used extensively for both quality-control and acceptance testing of HMA pavements. Profilographs measure smoothness with a profile index that is reported in inches per mile. In a rough sense, the inches per mile are the number of inches of bumps or depressions that are accumulated in a mile. The units are generally hand operated, and can be used in short lengths. They can be used for quality-control or acceptance, but not for network level pavement management.

Two types of profilometers are commonly used. They are referred to as the “California type profilograph” and the “Rainhart type profilograph.” The two types of equipment do not give the same results, so it is important to use the type that is specified for the project.

Response Type Road Roughness Measuring System

Response Type Road Roughness Measuring Systems (RTRRMS) gauge smoothness by the up and down movement of a standard car in response to changes in road profile. This approach is intended to be what a traveler would feel if driving the roadway. As such it is susceptible to variations based on items such as speed, acceleration, and vehicle suspension. Read- ings are taken at standard speeds to simulate actual driving conditions.

The Mays Meter is the most common form of RTRRMS. The units of roughness are inches per mile. This equipment would not give the same values of inches per mile as the profilographs. Mays Meter values are used by agencies for acceptance and in pavement management systems to mea- sure changes in pavement condition over time.

inertial Profilometers

Inertial profilometers measure the actual profile in a longitudinal direction with readings typically taken along every inch of the roadway. They measure profile changes with laser or acoustic devices that are typically mounted on vehicle bumpers. They utilize inertial systems (accel- erometers) that remove the up and down movement of the vehicle suspen- sion from the data. Testing can be done at highway speeds (between 10 and 70 mph (6.2 kph and 43.4 kph) and the roadway roughness is presented in

10.8 Acceptance Criteria

The criteria for acceptance of HMA construction projects are established in the project specifications. Various types of specifications are used—and oth- ers are being proposed—that emphasize different levels of accountability and risk to construction contractors. The four specifications listed below can be viewed as a spectrum representing the progression of development for HMA specifications in the United States. They are the following:

¢ Method, or recipe, specifications

® End-result specifications

° Quality-assurance specifications

° Performance-type specifications

Method, or recipe, specifications detail the products and procedures used in the construction process. Equipment types and quantity, material cri- teria such as aggregate gradations, methods of construction such as number of roller passes, and other items closely control the construction process.

End-result specifications move away from controlling the process of con- struction and concentrate on the testing of the mixture and pavement, after completion, for specification conformance. The intent of this type of specifica- tion is that the quality criteria used to judge final acceptance be selected based on their impacts on maintenance costs and long-term pavement performance.

Quality-assurance specifications, while similar to end-result specifica- tions, are based on statistical sampling and testing performed by the con- tractor and the purchasing agency. Quality-control procedures conducted by the contractor are used to assure that the specifications are being met throughout the process. Acceptance sampling and testing, performed by the agency, check the contractor’s QC process and determine whether the materials should be accepted. The acceptance tests are based on appropriate quality criteria, as are end-result specifications. In addition, Independent- assurance (IA) sampling and testing are performed by a third party to validate the acceptance and/or quality-control results.

Performance-type specifications emphasize performance of the fin- ished product over time and do not concentrate on construction activities or materials properties. Specific performance criteria are established in the specifications, which are based on what the finished roadway must meet after a specified length of time. Warranty specifications would fall in this category.

In addition, specifications that use performance-related tests to evaluate the ability of a mix to perform as intended over a period of time are considered to be performance-based specifications. Mathematical models are used to cal- culate the future performance of the pavement based on the test results from construction. Further discussion of performance specifications can be found in Section 10.9.

In evaluating acceptance test data, it is important to realize that the results of testing contain recognized statistical variation. In order to understand what the data means, the engineer cannot ignore the statistical nature of the results. The variations occur because of recognized limitations in the precision and the consistency of test equipment and procedures. Statistical acceptance methods are described in Section 10.8.4, “Statistical Acceptance,” but even for traditional acceptance criteria, an understanding of the statisti- cal nature of test results cannot be overlooked. It is necessary to have an understanding of the normal variation in specific test parameters to make reasonable decisions about acceptance and/or rejection of materials.

10.8.1 Historical Acceptance

Acceptance criteria are usually based on a number of specific properties of the mixture and the finished pavement. Historically, the following set of items was used to evaluate HMA construction projects:

° Materials—aggregate gradation, asphalt binder viscosity properties,

and asphalt content

° Thickness tolerances—cores, yield checks, and string lines

° Pavement density—cores and nuclear gauge readings

° Pavement smoothness—straightedge

For many years, the responsibility for testing and controlling materials was held by the specifying agency. Contractors often submitted proposed aggregate and asphalt materials to the agency. The agency then performed a mix design and dictated the mixture proportions. At the start of produc- tion, the agency conducted asphalt content tests and aggregate gradations to make sure the job-mix formula was being duplicated within allowable tolerances. As long as the job-mix formula was adhered to, the assumption was made that the mixture volumetric criteria were also being met. This was often a mistaken assumption.

This type of combined process control and acceptance also required the agency to be responsible for adjusting the aggregate proportions and/or the asphalt content of the mixture. By changing the mixture proportions at the plant, the agency actions potentially had a direct effect on the con- tractor’s ability to achieve density in the finished pavement. In some cases, penalties were assessed to the contractor for low densities even though the contractor was not in a position to make changes to the mixture to remedy the situation.

10.8.3 General Compaction Requirements

In most cases, pavement-density specifications are used to judge the acceptability of the compaction process during construction. The goal of compacting an HMA pavement is to achieve an optimum air-void content and provide a smooth, uniform surface. The resultant in-place air-void content of the HMA is a very important factor that affects performance of the mixture throughout the life of the pavement.

There are a variety of factors that are not part of the compaction operation that can have a significant impact on compaction. Many of these factors are listed below:

° Properly designed plant-produced mixtures

° Proper sampling and handling procedures of samples from the mixing

facility or roadway

° Proper field laboratory testing procedures, especially correct compac-

tion techniques and maximum theoretical density as appropriate

° Proper sampling, handling, and testing of the pavement core samples

e Adequate underlying support for the mixture during the compaction

process

Most pavement compaction specifications fall into two categories. The first and most common specifies that compaction shall achieve a certain percentage of a reference density. This is called a reference density speci- fication, and the reference density can come from the laboratory or from test strips in the field. The second category is called a method specification. This type requires that specified means and methods be accomplished. Each of these types of specifications will be discussed in more detail on the following pages.

Control Strip Density Specifications

Some agencies specify compaction requirements to meet a percentage of a control strip density. Under these specifications, a test strip of HMA is placed and compacted at the beginning of normal production paving. The control strip is placed using specified or otherwise selected rolling proce- dures and equipment. The intent is that the test strip be compacted to the maximum achievable density, which then becomes the specified reference density. Once the density of this control strip is established, the rest of the paving is required to achieve a percentage of this density. These types of specifications usually require the control strip to achieve a minimum percentage of the laboratory density as well, to avoid mixtures that are not compactable. This type of specification allows the relative difficulty (or ease) of compacting a given mix to become a factor in establishing the compaction requirements. New control strips are required for a different mix design or pavement application.

Quality-control testing for this type of project includes in-place density testing as described in 10.6.2, except that the compaction is calculated differently.

10.8.4 Statistical Acceptance—Percent Within Limits (PWL) Process

Acceptance test results on many projects are evaluated using statistical acceptance procedures. Pay factors may be calculated on specified criteria to award incentives or disincentives on the criteria that are evaluated. Some- times pay factors may be calculated with a statistical process called Percent Within Limits (PWL), which is calculated for a given lot of material produced. The materials produced and placed for each lot would have pay factors that would be applied to the quantities produced in that lot.

Pavement owners wish to get their most important quality parameters as close as possible to the target values. The asphalt content of a mixture, for example, will be detrimental to the longevity of a pavement if it is either too high or too low. So the owners want plants producing HMA for their projects to be focused on achieving the actual target value of the job- mix formula, as opposed to achieving some minimum value. Statistically speaking, this means the owner wants the sample mean to match the tar- get values. The sample mean is the average value of all test results. If four asphalt content tests were run for a given lot, for example, the average of those four tests would be the mean asphalt content for that lot.

The owners also want consistency of the materials produced. They want all the material produced to be close enough to the target values so that the long-term performance of the entire pavement will be good. Roadways often have to be replaced when the worst portion of the material begins to fail. Statistically speaking, the owner wants to have low variability or low variance in the test results. Variance is the difference between a tested value and the average tested value. So, specifying low variance is the same thing as saying all of the test results should be close to the average. Statisticians calculate a “standard deviation” to represent the variability in test results.

The third thing that owners want is to have a higher level of confi- dence that they know the level of quality actually achieved on the project. The test results from one compaction test for 5,000 feet of roadway, for example, would not give an engineer a high degree of confidence that he knows what compaction level was achieved throughout the pavement. A higher degree of confidence would be achieved if the appropriate num- ber of samples were taken and the best (most accurate) sampling and test- ing methods available were used.

that an asphalt plant produces hot mix asphalt during a given day and the binder content starts out at 5.1 percent, increasing to 5.5 percent at the end of the day, but the overall average of the binder content is exactly 5.3 percent. Assume the laboratory gets two samples from the plant, one in the morning, and one at midday, and their test results are 5.1 percent and 5.3 percent. In this example, the average of the test results is (5.1 + 5.3)/2 = 5.2. The tested average was 5.2 percent and the actual average was 5.3 percent. This example also shows that if the laboratory had taken more samples, and had the samples been spaced more evenly to better represent the day’s production, it could have come closer to achiev- ing the actual average value.

This concept, that the test results do not exactly represent all of the material produced, is important for understanding the statistics. It is the reason that a statistical parameter called percent within limits (PWL) is cal- culated. If the asphalt content is measured on four samples evenly spaced throughout the day and all four of the tested asphalt contents fall within the upper and lower limits of the specification, intuitively that means we had 100 percent within the limits. Statistically speaking, however, that means there were four test values that can describe something about the mean asphalt content and the standard deviation of the asphalt content. Based on those numbers (mean and standard deviation), the percentage that fell within the upper and lower limits can be calculated. That percent- age is the PWL, and it is calculated for each set of test results to determine what percentage, of all of the mix produced, likely fell within the specifi- cations limits. The more test results there are, the more accurately the PWL represents the materials produced. If an insufficient number of samples are tested, the PWL will be inaccurate. If an agency desires to reduce the number of samples taken, the number of samples per lot should not-be reduced. Increasing the lot size would be more appropriate.

Statistical acceptance procedures assume reasonably uniform produc- tion. The statistical models will not adequately represent materials’ proper- ties if a significant change occurs in the production process. This can be remedied by varying a lot size or truncating a lot when a known change is made to the production process.

Quality Characteristics for Pay Factors

Projects that are poorly controlled will have high variability in test results. The more consistent the materials and methods are, the lower the variability will be. Specifications establish acceptance parameters that will be used in pay-factor calculations. Attributes often used for acceptance include asphalt content, aggregate gradation, mixture volumetric proper- ties, compaction, and ride quality. Agencies do not use statistical methods for all their acceptance criteria. Only parameters that can be tested and produced within reasonable statistical variation can be used. Segregation, for example, is not a parameter that can be effectively incorporated into a statistics-based acceptance program. Test procedures that generate repeatable numbers are needed for application of statistical methods.

Specification Limits on Quality Characteristics

Specifications establish upper and lower limits for each of the quality characteristics that will be measured. These are defined as the upper spec- ification limit (USL) and the lower specification limit (LSL). For example, if the asphalt content specifications set limits at 5.1 percent to 5.9 percent, then 5.1 percent is the lower specification limit and 5.9 percent is the upper specification limit.

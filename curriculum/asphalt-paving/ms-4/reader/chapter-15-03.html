The HMA overlayment design involves placing the HMA layers in a similar manner except that no intervening ballast is used between the HMA layer and the ties (Figure 15.9). The ties are placed directly on the smooth, plain HMA surface. Cribbing aggregate is placed between and at the end of the ties to restrain track movement. 15.3.2 Desirable Benefits and Applications of HMA Underlayment

An HMA underlayment has the following benefits:

e A strengthened track-support layer below the ballast to uniformly distribute reduced pressures to the roadbed (subgrade)

e A waterproofing layer and confinement to the underlying roadbed to provide consistent load-carrying capability for track structures even on roadbeds of marginal quality

° An impermeable layer to divert water to side ditches, essentially eliminating roadbed (subgrade) moisture fluctuations, to consistently maintain adequate underlying support

° A consistently high level of confinement for the ballast to provide high shear strength and uniform pressure distribution

° A resilient layer between the ballast and roadbed to reduce the like- lihood of subgrade pumping without substantially increasing track stiffness

e An all-weather, uniformly stable surface to place the ballast and track superstructure

underlayment are those having heavy freight traffic and/or high-speed passenger traffic, where one or more of the following conditions exist or are expected:

15.3.3 Prevailing Design Practices

Recommended ideal HMA mix specifications, trackbed section designs, and application practices have steadily evolved over the years, reflecting refinements from initial recommendations. Slight variations from the rec- ommended mix designs and construction techniques are typical and do not appear to adversely affect HMA trackbed performance.

The HMA mix, which is considered to impart the ideal properties to the track structure, is a low modulus (plastic) mix, having design air voids of 1-3 percent. This mix will easily compact to less than 5 percent air voids. This has been accomplished by specifying the local dense-graded highway base mix having a maximum aggregate size of 1.0-1.5 inches (25-37 mil- limeters). Ideally, the asphalt binder content can be increased by about 0.5 percent above optimum for highway applications because rutting and bleeding are not concerns in the insulated trackbed environment.

Long-term monitoring and testing of in-service trackbeds indicate that this low-voids, impermeable mix undergoes minimal oxidation from the effects of air and water. It is also isolated from extreme temperature fluctuation within the insulated trackbed environment. This isolation provides a layer with reasonably consistent stiffness in hot weather but slightly resilient in cold weather, thus eliminating any chance for the mix to rut and bleed in hot weather or crack in cold weather. A long life for the mix is thus ensured.

Tests on subgrade/roadbed samples, obtained directly from under the HMA layer, indicate that the in situ moisture contents are very close to opti- mum values for maximum density of the respective materials. For struc- tural design analyses, it is reasonable to assume base support strength or bearing capacity values at optimum conditions for the material under the HMA layer.

The predominant HMA layer width is 12-14 feet (3.7—4.3 meters) for open track, but the width is wider under special trackwork, such as turn- outs, to provide support under the longer ties.

HMA layer thickness varies depending on the quality of the roadbed (subgrade) support and traffic loadings. A layer 5 to 6 inches (125-150 millimeters) thick is normally used for average conditions. For unusu- ally poor roadbed support conditions and high-impact areas, a minimum thickness of 8 inches (200 millimeters) is used. Ballast thickness usually is 6-12 inches (150-300 millimeters).

The HMA layer should extend several feet (meters) beyond the ends of special trackwork so that subsequent track surfacing operations and any impact from track stiffness changes will not infringe on the area. The roadbed should be reasonably well-compacted, well-drained, and capable of accommodating the hauling and spreading equipment without exces- sive rutting or deformation. A slight crown or side slope is desirable. The need for sub-surface drainage or roadbed support improvements, before placing the HMA, will depend on site-specific conditions.

The structural design of railway trackbeds containing HMA underlay- ment can be performed using KENTRACK, a finite element computer program available through the University of Kentucky Civil Engineering website. Asphalt Institute’s Research Report RR 84-1 covers the use of this program and the design of railway systems utilizing HMA underlayments as an alternative to the traditional ballast systems. The primary governing factor is limiting the vertical compressive stresses, or permanent deforma- tion, on the subgrade. For the HMA layer, the tensile strains at the bottom of the HMA layer control its service life. Damage analyses are conducted to predict the service life of the trackbed components for various combina- tions of traffic, tonnages, subgrade support, and component layer compo- sitions and thicknesses.

15.3.4 Typical Installation Practices

The construction of new rail lines and extensions, classification yards, and intermodal terminals represent ideal conditions for HMA underlayment. These are usually large projects with exposed subgrades that are readily accessible to conventional asphalt paving equipment for placing the HMA layer before placing the ballast and track. Typical highway paving technol- ogy can be used. A typical paving view on the Burlington Northern-Santa Fe Railway's double-tracking of its transcontinental mainline in Southern Kansas is shown in Figure 15.10. This project involves approximately 200 miles (320 kilometers) of asphalt underlayment. The HMA is being placed in two lifts, with a total compacted thickness of 6 inches (150 millimeters).

New intermodal yards use HMA for all or portions of new track con- struction. A particular advantage is the waterproofing characteristics of the HMA layer and the positive drainage systems that can be utilized in the design of the loading area. Also, better construction accessibility reduces the construction time required.

15.4 Athletic and Recreation Facilities

15.4.1 Types of Facilities

Almost every conceivable type of athletic or recreational facility can use asphalt in some way—from running tracks (see Figure 15.12) to walk- ing paths to tennis courts (see Figure 15.13) to playgrounds to football fields. A wide selection of all-weather asphalt construction is economi- cally available for these purposes. When asphalt is properly designed and constructed, maintenance costs are negligible. These surfaces are designed and constructed differently than a typical roadway or parking lot; when developing these types of facilities, a qualified consultant with experi- ence in the particular type of facility (walking or running track, tennis court, etc.) should be engaged. Also, it is suggested that the contractor have experience with the construction of the type of facility being built.

15.4.2 Basic Considerations

A properly designed and constructed asphalt pavement can provide a long-lasting surface. The surface must be designed to meet the needs of the users and to accommodate local site conditions and materials. The existing terrain, drainage, and pavement uses of the facility must be addressed in the design phase. The basic construction must provide the following:

15.4.3 Pavement Thickness

A thorough soils investigation should be conducted to determine the strength of the existing soils and drainage characteristics. The investigation and design recommendations should make sure that the project is not being built on unstable soils (such as expansive clays or collapsible soils) that will cause significant problems. These must be dealt with during design and con- struction. The drainage of the site must be thoroughly evaluated because saturated soils can have a dramatic effect on the load-carrying capacity of the pavement. When areas of poor soils are encountered, their removal and replacement with a higher-quality material should be considered.

For bicycle/walking paths, the type of traffic that will operate on the sur- face should be considered. Periodic vehicular traffic, such as emergency, patrol, snow removal, maintenance, or other vehicles, must be considered in the design. For most paths, full-depth asphalt provides an excellent surface because of its speed of construction. The thickness of the HMA surface will vary from 3 to 5 inches (75 to 125 millimeters) depending on the traffic and the soil conditions of the site. It may be desirable to place a base course of crushed stone or reclaimed asphalt pavement to help distribute the load or as a construction aid to ease the grading and layout of the path.

Tennis courts should generally have 3 to 4 inches (75 to 100 milli- meters) of HMA placed on a 4- to 8-inch (100 to 200 millimeters) layer of compacted base course (consisting of crushed aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement). It is also recommended that the subgrade be leveled and compacted to provide a finish elevation of 4 to 6 inches (100 to 150 millimeters) above the surrounding ground surface. This will provide for water runoff and vegetation control around the perimeter.

Asphalt Mix Design

The type of mix to be used for bicycle/walking paths and other recre- ational facilities should be different than that used for parking lots or high- way facilities. The mix-design criteria used for asphalt pavements designed for vehicular traffic will not, in most cases, provide a mix of the needed durability and workability required for recreational facilities. A mix devel- oped for recreational use will generally contain more asphalt binder, have less air voids, and be less prone to oxidation, raveling, and cracking than a typical roadway mix. The objective of the asphalt mix is to provide a cost- effective mix that will have the following characteristics:

° Sufficient asphalt to provide for a durable surface

° A smooth uniform surface texture

° Sufficient workability to allow ease of handling during construction

and proper compaction

For some applications such as tennis courts, it is recommended that a nominal maximum size of % inch (9.5 millimeters) or less be considered. Construction Considerations

Before construction, all vegetation and all unsuitable soils should be removed and the subgrade should be scarified and compacted to at least 95 percent of modified proctor density (ASTM D 1557/AASHTO T 180). After compaction, the subgrade should be proof-rolled with a heavy pneumatic roller or dump truck to ensure that there are no soft spots. Any soft spots identified should be removed and replaced with a stable material. After compaction, the subgrade should be treated with an herbicide. This must be done carefully to insure that surrounding vegetation is not adversely affected. If an aggregate base course is used, it should also be compacted to at least 95 percent of modified proctor density.

For bicycle/walking paths, the asphalt layer can be placed in one lift. To enhance surface smoothness on tennis courts and running tracks, an asphalt layer placed in two equal lifts is recommended. Most tennis court specifications require that tolerance not vary more than % inch in 10 feet (3 millimeters in 3 meters) when measured in any direction.

15.5. Asphalt Roofs

15.5.1 History

Roofing was probably the world’s first craft because shelter has always been a primary need of people. Asphalt, with its extraordinary combina- tion of waterproofing, preservative, and cementing qualities, was an early discovery of ancient people and has been used extensively in the con- struction of their buildings and roads.

Today, asphalt is recognized more than ever as an outstanding roofing material and is used extensively to cover all kinds of buildings, from homes to factories. Asphalt roofing materials and construction methods are fully covered by specifications of the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) and many government and private agencies. In addition, all types of asphalt roofing are sanctioned by federal and other public agencies.

Asphalt roofs may be built in single- or multiple-layer systems with impregnated fabrics or with manufactured shingles.

15.5.2 Types of Asphalt Roofing

Prepared Roofing

Asphalt shingles. This type is composed of three basic materials— asphalt, felt, and mineral granules. The felt is impregnated with asphalt. Both sides of the saturated felt are then covered with a harder, tougher coat of asphalt, in which are embedded mineral granules. This composi- tion is then cut into individual shingles or strips. Asphalt shingles are available in almost any color from pastels to deep rich tones. Factory techniques for the application of granules have improved with the devel- opment of new permanent colors, and granules may be applied in almost . any blend or pattern of colors desired.

Asphalt roll roofing—smooth-surfaced. This type consists of a single layer of roofing felt saturated with asphalt and coated on both sides with a harder asphalt.

Asphalt roll roofing—mineral-surfaced. This type consists of smooth- surfaced roll roofing in which mineral granules have been embedded on either one or both sides.

Prepared roofings are those products manufactured and packaged ready to apply to the roof deck, usually by nailing only. Asphalt shingles and cer- tain roll roofings are in this category. They consist of a base of saturated felt to which has been added an asphalt coating and, usually, a surfacing of mineral granules.

Of the types listed, the shingle roof has been the most common. Asphalt shingles are applied to the steep roofs of homes, barns, and similar structures. They fill the need for attractive, efficient, but low-cost roofing for residential construction.

Asphalt Built-Up Roofing

An asphait built-up roof is composed of several layers of asphalt-saturated felt applied principally to a flat-roof deck. Asphalt is applied between the felt layers for adhesion. Over the top layer, a flood coat of asphalt is added and then covered with gravel or slag.

The built-up roof is a contrasting type, constructed on the flat or nearly flat tops of very large buildings—industrial, public, and many others.

For built-up roofing, only the components are manufactured and deliv- ered to the job. The roofing itself is built up directly on the roof deck in a series of layers or plies, sandwiched between layers of asphalt. A cover of aggregate is then applied over the final layer. The mopping on of the asphalt and the spreading of slag or gravel may be accomplished with the most readily available and simplest equipment. All operations can be performed quickly by hand application, although mechanical felt-laying machines, hot-asphalt applicators, and gravel spreaders will do the job more easily and rapidly on very large roofs.

The heart of the built-up roofing operation is the kettle for heating the asphalt to the proper application temperature. For efficient operation, the kettle must be able to bring the asphalt up to this temperature in a short time without overheating at the start—or at any time during the kettle’s operation. Overheating may seriously damage the asphalt and consider- ably reduce the life expectancy of the roof. Overheating may also cause the asphalt to be applied too thinly, resulting in poor adhesion between the roofing felts and possible later separation of the plies.

Properly constructed, an asphalt built-up roof will remain in service condition for many years with little or no maintenance required.

Either prepared or built-up roofing can be used on many roofs, but the choice is limited somewhat by the slope of the deck to which it is to be applied. Built-up roofs are usually applied only on the flatter slopes, while the application of asphalt shingles and other prepared roofing is typically used for the steeper slopes.

@ Pavement Noise Mitigation

15.6.1 Introduction

Research in Europe and in the United States has shown that it is pos- sible to build asphalt pavement surfaces that will provide reduced noise levels. Throughout the world, sound caused by transportation systems is the number one noise complaint. Noise generated by traffic is one of the prime offenders. Engine (power train), exhaust, aerodynamic, and tire/ pavement noise all contribute to traffic noise. Modification of pavement surface type and/or texture can result in significant tire/pavement noise reductions. A quiet road surface can be built while at the same time con- sidering safety, durability, and cost using one or more of the following approaches:

¢ A smooth surface texture using small top-size aggregate

* An open-graded friction course (OGFC) with a high air-voids content

15.6.2 Nature of Noise

Noise, like all other sounds, is a form of acoustic energy. It differs from pleasant sounds only in that it often disturbs us and has the characteristics of an uninvited guest. Understanding noise requires a basic knowledge of sound physics and how humans respond to it.

Sound is fluctuation of air in a wavelike motion. Associated with this motion is acoustic or sound pressure that is measured in decibels. The decibel combines the magnitude of sound with how humans hear. Because human hearing covers such a wide range of sounds, it does not lend itself to measurement with a linear scale. If a linear scale were used, most sounds occurring in daily life on a scale of 0 to 1 would be recorded between 0.00 and 0.01. Thus, it would be difficult to discriminate between sound levels in our daily lives.

Instead of a linear scale, a logarithmic scale is used to represent sound levels and the unit is called a decibel or dB. The term dB(A) is used when referring to the sound levels that have been A-weighted. The ear has its own filtering mechanisms, and the inclusion of the A after dB indicates that the scale has been normalized or “fine tuned” to hear like a human. The decibel scale ranges from 0 dB(A), the threshold of human hearing, to 140 dB(A) where serious hearing damage can occur. Table 15.9 represents this scale and some of the levels associated with various daily activities.

Alongside a freeway, the sound level (i.e., noise) might be in the range of 70-80 dB(A). As a rule, an individual can differentiate between two levels of similar sound that are at least 3 dB(A) different in level.

Noise from traffic is due to a large number of moving noise sources spaced along an entire roadway. This type of noise is classified as a line source because noise is transmitted along the entire roadway. As the dis- tance from the line of source increases, the noise level decreases at a lower rate than it would from a single-point noise source. The proper selection of the pavement surface can result in a significant reduction in the noise level alongside the roadway. The selection of pave- ment type and the maximum gradation of the aggregate will affect the noise level. Selection criteria are discussed in the following paragraphs. As a pavement ages, its noise level increases because of changes in the surface texture. The HMA surface may start to lose particles, crack at the surface, and in the case of an OGFC, result in clogged pores from accumu- lated dust or deicing materials.

Miscellaneous Asphalt Uses

Some types of asphalt construction require special consideration of either the construction methods or the composition of the asphalt mix- ture. Asphalt as an engineering material is versatile and, when designed properly, is ideal for many forms of paving and other applications.

15.7.1 Bridge-Deck Paving

General

Asphalt surfaces are desirable for bridge decks because they are not affected by salts used for de-icing, and if properly constructed they may waterproof the bridge floor, thus preventing water and salts from damag- ing it. Asphalt surfaces can also be highly skid-resistant, providing a safe bridge deck.

Asphalt Mixes

Dense-graded asphalt mixtures are recommended for leveling and inter- mediate courses. In areas where chemicals are expected to be used to de-ice the pavement, aggregates that do not react with de-icing chemicals should be selected. A lightweight aggregate may be desirable to reduce the weight of the asphalt pavement on the bridge.

Both dense-graded and stone matrix asphalt HMA mixtures have been used for bridge decks. In many cases on special projects, very stiff polymer- modified asphalt binders (up to PG 82-22) have been used to prevent rutting or other forms of permanent deformation in that HMA layer. Also specially designed asphalt waterproofing membranes have been placed on the deck before the HMA layer is placed.

15.7.2 Construction Methods

The surface to be paved, whether it is a PCC or a corrugated steel bridge deck, must be cleaned of all loose and foreign matter. Power brooms and compressed air tools may be necessary to accomplish this task. The sur- face of the PCC deck is then given a tack coat of SS-Ih or CSS-1h asphalt emulsion diluted with an equal amount of water and applied at a rate of 0.05-0.20 gallons per square yard (0.2-0.9 liter per square meter).

After mill scale is removed, steel decks usually require painting with a heavy asphalt paint. Vertical surfaces are also painted to a depth greater than the thickness of the subsequent paving. The tack coat is scrubbed in, thus providing a continuous asphalt film with no holes in the coating.

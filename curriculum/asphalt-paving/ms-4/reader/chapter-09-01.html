9.1 Introduction

Compaction is the process used to densify, or reduce the volume of, a mass Of material. For hot mix asphalt (HMA), compaction locks aggregate particles together to provide stability and resistance to deformation while simultaneously reducing the permeability of the mixture and enhancing its durability.

This chapter will discuss the mechanics of compaction, factors affecting compaction, compaction equipment, rolling procedures, and troubleshoot- ing compaction problems.

9.2.2 Need for Compaction

During the compaction of HMA, three very important factors that affect pavement performance take place. The asphalt-coated aggregate particles are pressed together, air voids are reduced, and mix density (weight-to- volume ratio) increases. This squeezing together of the aggregates increases their surface-to-surface contact and inter-particle friction, resulting in higher mix stability and pavement strength.

The reduction of air voids to the optimum level in the mix produces a pavement that is nearly impermeable. In an undercompacted mix, the voids tend to be interconnected, thereby permitting the intrusion of air and water into the pavement structure. Premature pavement distress can then occur. The air oxidizes the asphalt binder, making it brittle and caus- ing the mix to crack under the repeated deflections from traffic loads. Premature aging can also result in premature thermal cracking. Water per- meating into and through the pavement can lead to the stripping of the asphalt binder from the aggregate, the weakening of the base and sub- grade soils, or freeze-thaw damage in colder climates. Finally, if an optimal level of compaction is not achieved during construction, subsequent traffic will further densify the mix. This will occur principally in the wheelpaths, and the resulting ruts may be a safety hazard for traffic, especially during wet weather.

9.2.3 Mechanics of Compaction

The goal of compacting HMA is to achieve the optimum air-voids content. Behind the paver, the HMA typically has 15 to 20 percent in-place air voids. It is the task of the rollers to reduce that void content to 8 percent or less for dense-graded HMA mixes. Typically, stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes should be compacted to 6 percent or less air voids. At these levels, the voids are usu- ally not interconnected and the detrimental effects of air and water (primarily raveling and stripping) are avoided, as illustrated in Figure 9.2. If a mix is compacted to a very low air-voids level (less than 2 percent), it is an indicator that a problem exists in the mix design or mix production itself—for example, too much asphalt binder or dust.

Even with a properly designed mix, compaction of the mat is possible only if two conditions exist: correct mix temperature and mix confinement. At high temperatures, the asphalt binder is in a fluid state, allowing the aggregate particles to readily move past one another and rearrange into a tight mass. As it cools, the asphalt binder stiffens and resists further mix densification. Therefore, compaction must be completed while the mix is hot and workable. Further, no one specific set of temperature ranges applies to all asphalt binders or mixes. For example, mixtures using polymer-modified asphalt binders stiffen at higher temperatures, allowing less time for compac- tion than mixtures using an unmodified-asphalt binder.

Even when the mat is the correct temperature, the target density can only be obtained when the mix being compacted is adequately confined. This confinement is controlled by three forces: the compressive force of the roller, the support provided by the surface beneath the mat, and the cohesion of the mix. The compressive force of the rollers comes either from their weight or from a combination of weight and dynamic energy due to vibration. The supporting force of the subgrade results from its stability and firmness. The cohesion of the mix is a result of the combined effect of interparticle

9.2.4 Factors Affecting Compaction

Factors affecting compaction can be categorized into several classes: mix properties, environmental effects, layer (lift) thickness, subgrade and bases, and time available for compaction.

Mix Properties

Properties of various aggregates and asphalt binders have a pronounced effect on the workability of mixes. In addition, the specific mix types cho- sen for a project may have different target density requirements, which will affect the types of equipment and procedures used. For example, a gap-graded mixture that uses a polymer-modified asphalt binder such as SMA requires a different approach for compaction than does a fine-graded mixture designed for light-duty traffic. These properties and the tempera- ture of the mix at the time of compaction must be considered when decid- ing on a compaction procedure.

Aggregate

Gradation, surface texture, angularity, and characteristics of the filler/dust affect the workability of the mix. As the maximum aggregate size or the per- centage of coarse aggregate in the mix increases, the workability decreases and greater compactive effort is required to achieve target density.

Similarly, mixtures using aggregates with a rough surface texture require greater compactive effort, as opposed to a smooth-aggregate surface. Many

mixtures used today on high-volume and/or heavy-load facilities, such as interstate highways and major airports, have specifications that require high percentages of crushed aggregate. Mixtures designed to resist rutting under heavy traffic often require more compactive effort applied at higher temperatures than those designed for light-duty traffic.

Mixtures produced from gravels typically have more rounded particles than quarried stone and are thus more workable and easier to compact. Nat- ural sands, which commonly have rounded particle shapes, are often added to lower-traffic volume mixes in the interests of economy and to improve workability. Too much sand, particularly in the middle particle sizes—around the No. 30 (0.60 millimeter) mesh sieve—will result in tender mixes (mixes with high workability but low stability). Tender mixes are easily displaced by heavy rollers and too much rolling. They are often susceptible to scuffing and shear deformation by traffic after several weeks in place.

The fines or filler content in the mix will also affect the compaction process. The combination of filler and asphalt binder is what glues the aggregates together in HMA pavements; therefore, the mix should contain sufficient fines to combine with the asphalt binder to produce the neces- sary cohesion when the mix cools. The addition of mineral filler will help offset the tenderness or slow-setting properties of mixes containing too much sand. Conversely, if a mix contains too many fines it will become “summy” and very difficult to compact.

Asphalt Binder

At room temperatures, asphalt binder is virtually a solid, whereas at 250°-300°F (121°-149°C), it is a fluid. As previously mentioned, for a mix to be properly compacted, the asphalt binder must be fluid enough to permit the aggregate particles to move past one another. In effect then, the asphalt binder acts as a lubricant during compaction. As the mix cools, the asphalt binder solidifies and resists compaction. In general, once mix- tures made with an unmodified asphalt binder fall below 175°F (80°C), the asphalt binder, in combination with the fines in the mix, begins to bind the aggregate particles firmly in place. Consequently, productive compac- tion of the mix without damaging it is extremely difficult once the mix has cooled below that point.

The grade of asphalt binder used and the temperature at which the mix is produced determine its viscosity. Other factors being equal, higher- viscosity asphalt binders in the mix may require a slightly higher compac- tion temperature and/or greater compactive effort. Generally, Performance Graded (PG) binders with a high temperature grade difference of 92°C or more require polymer modification. The use of polymer-modified asphalt binders in HMA has increased steadily since the mid-1990s. These bind- ers must be heated to higher temperatures to achieve the same fluidity as unmodified binders for mixing and compaction, and compaction must be completed more quickly—before the mixture temperature drops below 240°F (115°C). Care must be taken not to overheat any asphalt binder, or damage may occur. Consult the binder manufacturer for recommended temperatures and handling practices.

The effective asphalt binder content of the mix also affects workability. As the asphalt content increases, the film thickness of the asphalt binder on the aggregate particles increases. At compaction temperatures, this increased film thickness increases the lubricating effect of the asphalt binder and up to a certain point makes compaction easier.

Mix Types

Three main types of mixes are used in HMA construction: dense-graded, open-graded, and SMA. These different mix types are discussed in Chap- ter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt Design.” Each has specific characteristics that will affect its ability to be compacted. In general, the more compactive effort required in the laboratory for the mix to achieve target air-voids require- ments, the tougher it will be to compact in the field. Open-graded mixtures generally do not require a great amount of compactive effort in the field.

Mix Temperature

The temperature at which an HMA mixture is produced affects both the ease of compaction and the time available for compaction. The time available for compaction increases with mix temperature, but there are limits as to how high the production temperature should be to avoid damaging the asphalt binder. Upper limits for mix temperature are discussed in Chapter 2, “Petro- leum Asphalts,” and more specifically in Asphalt Institute’s (EC-101), Best Management Practices to Minimize Emissions During HMA Construction, which covers best practices for storage and handling of asphalt binders. The upper temperature limits increase with higher PG grades and are generally higher for modified binders. The best temperature to begin rolling is the max- imum temperature at which the mix will support the roller without moving horizontally.

At the time of placement, the mix temperature is essentially uniform throughout the thickness of the lift. However, the top and bottom surfaces cool more rapidly than the interior portion of the mat. Extreme tempera- ture differences can lead to “heat checking” or other surface deficiencies, or to honeycombing near the bottom of a thick lift placed during cold weather. Careful surface inspection during compaction and observation of the condition of core specimens removed from the pavement is necessary to judge whether these problems exist.

Environmental Effects

The rate at which the mix cools affects the length of time during which density can and must be achieved. The following conditions all affect the rate of cooling of the HMA: ambient (air) temperature, the temperature of the sur- face upon which the mix is being placed, the wind speed, and the time of day. Cool air temperatures, strong winds, and cool surfaces all shorten the time for compaction. This may make compaction more difficult. Paving during the day will usually allow for a longer paving time because of the sun’s effect on the air temperature and on the underlying surface temperature. Conversely, night paving tends to allow less time for compaction.

Layer (Lift) Thickness

Generally, it is easier to achieve target density in thicker lifts of HMA than in thin ones. The thicker the lift, the longer it retains its heat and the longer the time for achieving compaction. This is beneficial when rolling lifts of highly stable mixes that are difficult to compact, or when paving in cool weather.

A generai rule of thumb for mixes above the maximum density line (see Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt Design”) would be to use at least three times the nominal maximum aggregate size for the minimum lift thickness. For example, a %-inch (12.5 millimeters) fine-graded mix would have a minimum compacted lift thickness of 1% inches (37.5 millimeters). A coarse-graded mix (having a gradation that passes below the maximum density line) requires a 4-to-1 rule or minimum lift thickness of four times the nominal maximum aggregate size. For example, a %-inch (12.5 millimeters) coarse-graded mix would have a minimum compacted lift thickness of 2 inches (50 millimeters). The reason for the different criteria is to provide sufficient room for the particles to realign and pack during compaction.

Increased layer thickness can permit lower mix temperatures to be used because of the reduced rate of cooling. For example, paving thick layers in the middle of the summer may lead to an excessively long wait for the mix to cool before the finish rolling can be completed. Additional rolling during this time may actually damage the mat.

Uniform lift thickness is important as well. In variable thickness layers (such as those used to correct cross-slope or to level the profile), it is best to use a prescribed rolling pattern rather than a density target, to ensure that the best possible uniform density is achieved. Use of a pneumatic-tire roller on uneven surfaces will also help. If the thickness of the layer is less than two and a half to three times the nominal aggregate size, it may be impossible to achieve the density requirements in the specifications.

Subgrade and Bases

The condition of the subgrade and/or base that is being paved upon will have a significant impact on the final density achieved. The stiffness of the underlying layer plays an important part in helping to provide the confinement necessary for compaction. Attempting to bridge a soft base or subgrade with HMA mix is not recommended. In addition, the tempera- ture of the base and the moisture condition can either help or hurt the compaction process. Cold, wet bases tend to pull heat out of the HMA mix, causing it to cool rapidly, thus making compaction more difficult.

The base and subgrade layers must be level and true to line and grade so that the HMA layers are uniform in thickness and section. As previ- ously mentioned, a soft or weak base or subgrade will create difficulties in obtaining the desired uniform density.

9.2.5 Cool Weather Paving

Placing and compacting HMA is difficult in cool weather and poses more challenges than just the reduced time available for compaction. It is particularly important to ensure that the equipment is properly main- tained and that the sequence of delivery, placement, and rolling is well coordinated. Special attention should be given to pneumatic-tire rollers to keep the tires warm to avoid pickup, which will be discussed later.

Also in cool weather, the temperature differential between the air and the mix is substantial, causing the surface to cool much more rapidly than the internal portion of the mix. This differential cooling may cause a thin crust to form at the surface. This crust might develop microcracks (called checking) that later may cause the pavement surface to ravel prematurely. Cold surfaces may also cause immediate solidification of the mixture that is in contact with the surface, resulting in “honeycombing” in thick mats and possibly poor adhesion or slippage in thin mats.

9.3 Compaction Equipment

Compaction is performed using any of several types of rollers that apply force (either static or dynamic) to the mat, reorienting aggregate particles to reduce the volume occupied by the mixture.

Self-propelled rollers are by far the most common type of compaction equipment used for HMA construction. Towed-type rollers should not be used for HMA compaction, but hand-operated walk-behind rollers, vibrat- ing plates, or even hand tamps are often used in small areas inaccessible to larger rollers. Steel-wheel rollers should be checked for wear on the surface of the drum. A sharp metal straightedge may be used for this check. The roller should not be used if grooves or pits have worn into the rolling drum. Scrapers should be routinely inspected and replaced if they are excessively worn.

Weight

Steel-wheel rollers can vary in weight from 3 to 14 tons (2.7 to 12.7 tonnes) or more. Most can be ballasted to increase the weight. Roller widths range from 40 inches to 54 inches (102 centimeters to 137 centimeters). A typical steel-wheel roller would have an unballasted weight of 8 tons (7.9 tonnes) and a ballasted weight of 10 tons (9.8 tonnes) with 50-inch (127 centime- ters) wide drums. Wider and heavier rollers are available from a variety of manufacturers.

Pounds per Linear Inch

Because steel-wheel rollers vary in width and weight, a simple means was developed to help quantify the compactive effort applied by static rollers. By dividing the weight of the roller by the width of both drums, the overall static linear load, expressed in pounds per linear inch (PLD or kilograms per millimeter of roller, can be determined. For example, an 8-ton roller with a 50-inch drum width would have an overall PLI of 160 PLI. The PLI can be used to match up rollers of different sizes and manu- facturers when establishing a rolling pattern.

Adjusting Compaction Force

The two ways to adjust the compactive force of a steel-wheel roller are to adjust the ballast weight) of the roller and the speed. Adding weight will increase the static linear load and apply greater force on the surface with each pass or coverage the roller makes. Adjusting the speed of the roller affects the dwell time that the drum has on the surface of the HMA. Speeding up a roller decreases the dwell time and lowers density.

9.3.2 Pneumatic-Tire Rollers

Pneumatic-tire rollers are also called rubber-tire rollers, or seal rollers. Self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers have two to eight wheels in front and four to eight wheels in the rear, though most will fall into the seven- or nine-total-wheels classes. The wheels on these rollers generally oscillate (axle moves up and down). An example of a pneumatic roller is shown in Figure 9.6.

Unlike a steel-wheel roller, the surface of a pneumatic-tire roller conforms to the shape of the underlying surface. This allows the roller to contact all points on the surface and also to manipulate particles differently than a steel-wheel roller.

Pneumatic-tire rollers have several advantages:

° They provide a more uniform degree of compaction than steel-wheel rollers.

They improve the seal near the surface, thus decreasing the perme- ability of the layer.

° They orient the aggregate particles for greatest stability, as high-pressure

The main disadvantage of a pneumatic-tire roller is the tendency for the tires to pick up fines from the surface of the HMA mix—unless the tires are kept clean and warm. This can be a serious problem with mixtures using polymer-modified asphalt binder and especially for SMA. Although pneumatic-tire rollers have been used successfully to compact SMA, their use is discouraged with this mixture classification.

Several self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers have a device to change tire inflation while the roller is operating. This inflation-on-the-run system auto- matically maintains any preset tire pressure or can raise or lower the infla- tion pressure while the roller is operating. Some compacting conditions and. requirements require different inflation pressures. However, tire inflation pres- sures should all be very close between tires and checked at regular intervals.

Weight

Self-propelled pneumatic-tire rollers vary in weight from 10.5 to 30 tons (9.5 to 27.2 tonnes). Ballast can be added to the machines to increase the weight. Widths of these rollers range from 60 to 90 inches (524 to 2286 millimeters).

7.2./ Dust-Return Equipment

Most plants are fitted with fabric filter collectors, allowing operating personnel to return the captured dust to the mix-production process. As mentioned above, fines collected from primary-type collectors are returned 100 percent to the process without any contemplation of partial return (see Figure 7.14).

Dust from a baghouse secondary collector, however, can be returned entirely or in part, depending on the requirements of the job-mix formula. Fines return is accomplished mechanically with screw conveyors or pneu- matically with compressed air or blower systems.

The general practice is to design mixes so all the fines collected in the baghouse are reused in the mix or so all the fines are wasted/removed from the process. This keeps the equipment options and operating principles simple.

When 100 percent of the dust collected in the baghouse is to be reused, the following operational best practices are important:

¢ The baghouse cleaning and fines return must stop when the dryer stops. This keeps large quantities of dust from being transferred to the mixing area of the drum or deposited in the fines bin on the batch plant when the aggregate feed stops.

* Large and rapid increases in production rate should be avoided. It takes time for the dust to be collected on the bags, cleaned off the bags, and then returned to the system. Large jumps in production rate can be immediately accomplished by resetting the cold feed bins, but it takes time for the dust corresponding to the production-rate increase to be collected on the bags and returned to the mix. The mix produced during that time will be short of -200 mesh (75 micron) material and high in voids. Large, rapid decreases in production have the opposite effect. Most modern control systems ramp production up and down in small intervals because of this.

¢ Fines-return equipment inspection should be done regularly. When the fines-return equipment wears, it may only be able to return a certain quantity of fines. Test results are characterized by a shortage of fines and an increase in voids above a certain rate on the plant system.

When partial dust return is required, several equipment options exist for both batch plants and drum-mix plants. Most plant owners purchase a storage silo for transferring the collected fines for reuse or for storage for later use. If the silo fills to capacity, accumulated fines are usually trucked off and wasted or used for other purposes, such as in the manufacture of slurry seals. Figures 7.14 and 7.15 show this type of equipment.

Baghouse dust stored in silos can either be reintroduced into the process volumetrically with a calibrated feeder valve (with output charted to the speed of the feeder valve, as similarly done in the calibration process of cold feed bins), or it can be weighed back into the process.

With a batch plant, dust can be returned with a calibrated feeder to the hot aggregate elevator, be weighed back through the aggregate weigh hopper as an additional hot bin draw, or be weighed in a separate scale and then deposited into the mixer. All these options are common.

In a drum-mix plant, material can similarly be returned through a calibrated valve, or a negative-weighing pod can be used to continu- ously weigh dust back. Figure 7.16 shows this type of negative weigh pod system.

Local specifications often determine which method of dust reentry is desirable or required. The important point is that many options exist for returning captured dust to the mix production process. Mix requirements and local specifications dictate the required equipment options.

7.2.9 Lime Additive Bulk Storage and Feed Silos

Some specifying agencies require certain aggregates to be treated with lime for antistripping control.

Bulk lime is usually delivered to the plant site in tankers, transferred pneumatically with a blower into a storage silo, and then introduced in ways similar to that of a bulk mineral filler additive. Depending on local requirements and preferences, lime is either introduced into the mixing section of the plant or mixed with the aggregate before the drying process. When the lime is introduced into the mixing area of the plant, the equip- ment shown in Figures 7.14 and 7.15 can be used. When lime is mixed with aggregate before the drying process, equipment such as that shown in Figure 7.8 is often used. Lime can also be premixed with the aggregate before shipment to the plant site.

7.2.10 Fiber Additive Equipment

Fiber additives are specified for some hot mix formulas to help ensure that a uniform film thickness is maintained between the aggregate par- ticles and to reduce the possibility of draindown of the binder in the mix. They are produced from both mineral fiber and cellulose fiber materials.

Fiber additives are popular with mixes that contain a high percentage of large-size or same-size aggregate and a low percentage of fine aggre- gates to fill the voids between these large aggregate particles—like Open- Graded Friction Course (OGFC) and stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixes.

Fibers are typically added pneumatically with a machine that shreds and blows the fibers into the mixing area of the plant. This machine uses a negative-weighing principle where the bundle of fibers deposited in the machine is continuously weighed, and the declining weight is calculated and displayed on the control system as it is separated and blown into the plant.

In a drum-mixer application, this machine runs continuously and there is a continuous weight display of the fiber additives. The flow rate of the machine is typically tied into the plant master controls and the aggregate belt scales.

In a batch-plant application, the machine starts and stops based on the target weight of fibers for each batch. With a batch plant, fibers can also be added by hand in bag form by dropping a pre-packaged bag of fibers down a chute directly into the pugmill with each batch.

7.2.11 Finished Product Storage Silos and Load-Out Equipment .

Finished product storage silos allow the plant to run continuously whether or not trucks are present. Silos are required for drum-mix plants, because the mix flows continuously from the mixing area when the plant is running and must be elevated for dispensing into trucks. Silos on batch plants allow the plant to run continuously, thereby increasing daily output.

Silos can be either the surge or the storage type. Surge silos are typically not insulated and are often open-topped bins. This type of storage unit is lighter and can be made portable so that it works well with portable plants. Storage silos are fully enclosed and heavily insulated. They generally have heated cones, and some have sidewall heat. Top and bottom seals are com- mon on storage silos. Storage silos allow longer storage times for mixes, without significant heat loss or oxidation to the mix. Storage silos are mas- sive units requiring large foundations, making them semi-permanent installa- tions. Regulatory agencies sometimes rate storage silos for storage periods or limit maximum storage times. Allowable storage times are sometimes based on the absorption characteristics of the aggregate, rather than on the features of the plant equipment.

The use of a batcher or a gob hopper is a good practice and is generally required by specification for silos of any type. Batchers collect mix in small quantities at the top of the silo and drop the mix in mass into the silo. This action minimizes the segregation that can occur when silos are fed continu- ously. Batchers and gob hoppers need to be adjusted so that the maximum size batch is dispensed into the silo each time. These devices typically control segregation on most traditional mix designs, but some mixes are especially prone to segregation, especially if they are “gap-graded” or have especially large rock in them. Segregation can also occur in the batcher/gob hopper itself if the device is not fed directly in the center, fed obliquely at an angle, or positioned significantly below the feeding device.

7.% The Batch Plant Production Process

7.3.1 Aggregate Gradation Control with a Batch Plant

Aggregate gradation is controlled in two places in a modern batch plant—in the cold feed and in the screening unit/hot bins (see Figure 7.19). Changes in the past few years have complicated the gradation control process in a batch plant.

Batch plants were originally designed to accept a crushed aggregate product and a sand. The screens at the top of the batch tower were used to separate the aggregate into fractions so that different mixes could be constructed, some with larger rocks and a larger percentage of stone for base or binder lifts and some with more sand and smaller stones for wear- ing surfacees. When batch plants were first designed, purchasing presized aggregates for hot mix production was not an option. The batch tower's first job was to size the aggregates.

In today’s world, sized aggregates are readily available. Mixes can be constructed with different percentages of sized aggregates at a ratio blended right from the cold-feed bins. This is one reason why drum-mixers came into being in the 1970s. It also eliminates some of the requirements for a batch tower. In fact, it is not uncommon today to find five or six different aggregates being blended at the cold bins to feed a batch plant. With an overwhelming majority (over 95 percent) of today’s batch plants having only four hot bins, ratio blending at the cold feed affects the hot- bin gradations dramatically in a batch-plant operation.

Therefore, in modern batch-plant production, the blend formula is usu- ally first established for the cold-feed bins to arrive at the target job-mix formula just like a drum-mix plant, and then the hot bins are sampled for their individual gradations and a new blend recipe/ratio is constructed with these hot-bin gradations to re-arrive at the job-mix recipe. The ratios of the individual hot bins become the recipe that is used to construct the final mix with the batching plant.

If the HMA producer has good quality control on the cold-feed bins, mixes can be proportioned completely with the cold feeds, eliminating the need to screen the material in the batch plant. This is often referred to as “screenless mixing.” Screenless mixing is done by opening a bypass chute, allowing the heated aggregate stream to drop immediately into the No. 1 bin without passing over the screen deck. This requires drawing aggregate from only one hot bin when weighing an individual batch, which speeds up production. Bypassing the screen deck also eliminates wear and tear on the screens and the screening mechanisms, which are both expensive and time consuming to repair and maintain.

See Chapter 3, “Aggregates and Aggregate Additives,” to review the math- ematical procedure for establishing these blend ratios. This procedure is first used in the design process to establish the required percentages of each material for the job-mix formula; it is then used against the individual gradations of the cold-feed materials to reconfirm the blend ratios from the cold-feed bins. It is used again with the individual hot-bin gradations to establish the hot-bin percentages required to re-arrive at the target com- posite gradation.

Once these individual hot-bin percentages are determined, establishing the actual weights of each hot bin is relatively simple. The percentage for that hot bin is multiplied by the total quantity of aggregate desired in the batch and this becomes the target weight for that hot bin for that batch size.

When sampling hot bins, it is important to take a full cross section of the flow from the gate because most hot-bin walls are sloped and materials can segregate slightly. Also, the flow of material across the screen lends itself to segregation. This does not cause any problems with the final mix because the materials are being mixed in the pugmill, but it is important to obtain an accurate gradation of each hot bin. Therefore, it is important to sample each bin across the full width.

After the sampling is done and the gradations from each hot bin are established, the ratio of each hot bin can be calculated to arrive at the job-mix formula.

Since material from each hot bin is weighed, the percentages of the hot bins need to be converted into weight values. Most batch computers make this job easy by allowing you to enter the formula in weights that total 1000 pounds (453 kilograms). For example, if hot bin No. 2 is 33.5 percent of the total mix, then the weight target in the batch formula will be 335 pounds (152 kilograms). The operator then selects the batch size—with the batch computer doing the calculations for the operator—to establish the weights for the entire batch. This procedure also allows the operator to change the batch sizes without changing the formula. It is common for batch plants to change batch size. This usually is close to the size typically run. Most computers make this adjustment. For example, if a plant is a 4- ton (3.6 metric tonnes) plant, but the desired load is 14 tons (12.7 metric tonnes), the batch computer will produce four drops at 3.5 tons (3.2 met- ric tonnes) to arrive at the target quantity.

The gradations in the final mix will only be accurate if the operator or the computer batches the proper quantity of material from each hot bin. The accuracy of the scale is critically important. Frequent calibration checks are advisable. ;

Each activity is required for gradation accuracy in the final mix produced with a batch plant. More aggregates in the job-mix formula and tighter mix tolerances require stringent management of the various factors that can affect final gradations.

7.3.2 Asphalt Content Control with a Batch Plant

Asphalt content control in a batch is simpler than gradation control. Asphalt is simply weighed to the target weight in each batch in a separate container called the asphalt weigh bucket. A fill valve opens, the computer watches the weight accumulate in the asphalt scale, and the computer stops the scale just before the target weight is reached, allowing some material to still drain from the valve and pipe until the target weight is reached. This early shutoff function is referred to as a free-fall compensation value, and is automatically adjusted by the computer so the exact target is reached with each batch. 7.3.0 Mixing/Coating with a Batch Plant

Aggregates and asphalt are mixed in a pugmill in a batch plant. The pugmill is a twin-shaft design, with adjustable hardened steel tips and hardened steel liners. Tips can be configured for different styles of mixing. Preferences vary, but all styles seem to be effective. The manufacturer’s manual provides suggestions for setting the tip configuration. The pattern shown in Figure 7.22 is referred to as an around-the-box pattern. Tips adjust outward so that the clearance between the tip and the liner is not excessive. Missing tips, excessive clearance between the tips and the liner, and improper pattern to the tips all negatively affect the mixing efficiency of the pugmill.

Overfilling or underfilling the pugmill also causes inadequate mixing. When the pugmill is overfilled, the asphalt binder and the aggregates that were drawn last do not have a chance to be pulled down into the rest of the materials. Both asphalt content and gradation will be negatively affected and inconsistent throughout the batch. Significantly underfilling a mixer may also result in less than desirable results and cause segregation in large-rock mixes. A reasonable batch size should be respected.

Batch mixing time should be long enough to produce a homogenous mixture of evenly distributed and uniformly coated particles. Mixing time can be established for a particular mix by the procedure noted in AASHTO T 195 (ASTM D 2489). This method bases the degree of mixing on the percentage of coarse particles that are completely coated with asphalt. The method is correlated with mixing time.

The test involves separating coarse aggregate particles from the mix on a selected sieve size and examining 200 to 300 particles under a strong light. Any speck of uncoated aggregate particle visible to the naked eye is classified as uncoated. Usually particles coated at 90 and 95 percent are minimums for base and surface mixes, respectively. The least time needed to achieve these minimum coating levels is the most desirable mixing time. Most mixes will undergo additional mixing and coating as they are handled through slat conveyors, storage silos, and paving equipment. The common total batch mixing time is 25-35 seconds.

7.3.4 Recycled Mix Production with a Batch Plant

With a batch-type plant, two basic systems for recycled mix production are popular: the weigh-hopper method and the bucket-elevator method. With both systems, virgin aggregate is super-heated in the dryer and then the RAP is introduced later in the plant process. The super-heated virgin aggregates heat and dry the wet ambient RAP. The steam and dust released from the RAP has to be ducted to the exhaust system and the dust col- lection equipment. Because the RAP is not introduced into the aggregate dryer (as it is in a drum-mixer plant), additional ducting has to be added to the plant to accomplish this goal.

With the weigh-hopper method, RAP is either introduced into the aggregate-weigh hopper in the batch tower as an additional ingredient, or an additional weigh hopper is added specifically for the RAP. The control system weighs the RAP. One must adjust the binder content in the final mix recipe for the usable binder in the RAP (see Chapter 4, “Hot Mix Asphalt GHMA) Design”).

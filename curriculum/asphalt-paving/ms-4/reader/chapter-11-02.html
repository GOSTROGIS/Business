Rather than apply an impulse load, steady-state dynamic deflection devices apply a large static preload to the pavement, causing a sinusoidal vibration that is measured in terms of amplitude and frequency. The mag- nitude of the load that can be applied using this type of device is limited by the amplitude of the dynamic force that is created. Additionally, the preload may alter the pavement properties so that the resulting deflec- tions may not be representative of the deflections that would occur under a moving wheel load. For these reasons, this type of equipment is not used as extensively as the FWD.

The original deflection devices applied a static load to the pavement sur- face and measured the resulting deflections. The most well-known static deflection device is the Benkelman Beam, which was developed in the early 1950s at the WASHO Road Test. The beam is placed on the pavement surface with its probe located between the dual tires of a loaded truck. The rebound of the pavement as the truck moves away is measured and reported. This device has also been replaced by the FWD.

Testing Approach

Pavement deflection testing requires traffic control because the test- ing device must be stopped at each test location. On flexible pavements, deflection testing is typically conducted in the outer wheelpath, but a testing configuration may test both the inner and outer wheelpath at staggered intervals. Additional information on recommended FWD test- ing frequencies is provided in ASTM D 4694, “Standard Test Method for Deflections with a Falling-Weight-Type Impulse Load Device.”

11.2.4 Use of Pavement Condition information

The results of a functional pavement evaluation provide the information needed for the pavement management system to identify and prioritize maintenance and rehabilitation needs. Typically, pavement distress and roughness information are used to calculate pavement condition indexes that can be used to compare the condition of one pavement section with another and to identify the level of repair that might be needed to address the deficiencies identified. Over time, pavement condition information helps establish rates of pavement deterioration that can be used to fore- cast future pavement conditions under different funding scenarios.

Pavement Condition Indexes

To facilitate the use of pavement distress information in a pavement man- agement system, pavement condition indexes are calculated based on the type, severity, and quantity of distress present. Pavement condition indexes typically range in value from 0 to 10 or 0 to 100, with the highest values corresponding to pavements in excellent condition. Points are subtracted from a perfect score based on the distresses observed in the field to cal- culate the index. The complexity of the pavement condition index calcula- tion varies depending on the type of survey being conducted.

Pavement condition indexes can be calculated for each type of distress present or a composite index can be used that aggregates all of the dis- tress into one index representing the overall condition of a pavement section. Some agencies use a combination of these two types of indexes; the individual indexes are used to identify appropriate maintenance and rehabilitation treatments and the composite index is used to report the overall network conditions.

In local agencies, a composite index is most commonly used. For exam- ple, the pavement rating system for low-volume asphalt pavements that is included in several Asphalt Institute publications requires the rater to assign a value between 0 and 5 for all functional distress present (such as longitudinal and transverse cracking) and 0 to 10 for distress representing structural forms of deterioration (such as alligator cracking and rutting) or other important deterioration mechanisms (such as deficient drainage or poor ride quality). The defects associated with each distress are summed and subtracted from 100 to determine the condition rating for a pavement section.

A more detailed approach to calculating a composite index was developed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for use in its Micro PAVER pavement management system. This approach, which is documented in ASTM D 6433, “Standard Practice for Roads and Parking Lots Pavement Condition Index Surveys,” uses a similar approach, but separate deduction values have been developed for each distress type, severity, and quantity calculation.

In addition to using a composite index, some agencies use individual indexes or raw distress information to identify feasible maintenance and rehabilitation treatments. For example, fatigue cracking, patching, and rut distress may be combined to determine a structural condition index while longitudinal and transverse cracking may be combined with block cracking to form a functional condition index. Pavement sections with a low structural condition index are not good candidates for preventive maintenance treatments although a section with low functional condition indexes and high structural indexes might be.

Reporting Current Conditions

The most common use of pavement condition information is to report overall network conditions. This information is reported in a number of different formats depending on the needs of the intended recipients. For some individuals, a report that presents the overall network conditions by functional class, surface type, or some other delineator may be appropriate. Several agencies regularly publish reports on the state of the road network after each data collection cycle. Other agencies publish limited pavement condition information on their web sites.

One of the most effective ways of communicating pavement condition information involves displaying pavement condition information on color- coded maps. This approach has a strong visual impact, especially when roads in poor condition are shown in red. This method of displaying pave- ment condition information is especially useful for presentations to elected officials and upper management.

Forecasting Future Conditions

Once a pavement condition index has been developed, statistical tools can be used to develop pavement performance models that reflect the rate at which the pavements deteriorate over time. The inclusion of models that forecast future pavement condition is one of the most important factors differentiating a pavement management system from a pavement database. Prediction models allow an agency to forecast future pavement condi- tions under any number of budget scenarios or under different treatment strategies so that the long-term impacts of these options can be more fully understood and compared.

11.3 Pavement Distress Surveys

Whether part of a formal pavement management system or a project-level pavement evaluation to select an appropriate maintenance or rehabilitation treatment, pavement distress surveys provide information on the type and the extent of deterioration present and the primary mechanisms causing the dis- tress. These considerations are both very important in selecting and designing an improvement. Pavement distress-surveys may be conducted in conjunction with other testing, such as destructive or nondestructive testing.

11.3.1 Distress Survey Approaches

The previous section introduced methods of conducting both functional and structural pavement evaluations. Pavement distress surveys are the most common type of functional pavement evaluation used by highway and public works agencies. As part of a network-level evaluation, a dis- tress survey may be conducted on selected samples to provide an overall condition for each pavement section in the network. As part of a project- level investigation in which a rehabilitation treatment is being designed, more detailed distress surveys are typically conducted. At the project level, distress maps showing distress location, severity, and extent are often created. Distress maps are time-consuming to prepare but provide an excellent way of estimating repair quantities or marking the location of drainage or geometric features. An example of a distress map is shown in Figure 11.8.

Depending on the types of distress survey being conducted, the following types of equipment may be required:

° A distress identification manual

° A map or utility layout sheet showing section limits

¢ A measuring wheel

¢ Survey forms and/or mapping sheets

° A clipboard

° Pencils

° A straightedge or string line

° Spray paint (to mark limits and section identifiers)

° A hand calculator

¢ A camera or other recording device

Pavement distress surveys are typically conducted using crews of at least two people so that while one individual measures distress quantities, the other serves as a recorder. Having two crew members is also important for safety reasons because survey crews often have to mingle with traffic.

To help ensure the reliability of a pavement distress survey and to minimize variations in survey results between raters, survey crews should follow the established survey procedures as closely as possible. Detailed distress surveys require raters to correctly differentiate between distress types and to use pre- scribed definitions outlined in a distress identification manual to distinguish between different severity levels. Distress identification manuals also provide guidance on how to measure different types of distress and often provide tips for recording a distress that is difficult to discern in the field.

Before beginning a pavement distress survey, each rater should be trained in accordance with the distress identification procedures to mini- mize discrepancies in the field. In addition to being able to identify each distress type included in the survey procedures, the raters should be famil- iar enough with the procedures to know whether all severities associated with a particular distress should be collected or whether only the highest severity is recorded. Other details, such as how distress in a patched area should be handled, ought to be discussed before the start of the survey. These considerations are important for maintaining consistency in the survey data since raters may differ in what they consider a “good” road.

Typical Causes of Deterioration

Some of the primary causes of HMA pavement deterioration are listed here:

* Traffic loading

° Environment or climate

° Drainage deficiencies

° Material problems

° Construction deficiencies

¢ External causes (such as utility cuts)

As pavements age, one or more of these distress mechanisms begins to take its toll. As cracking and other forms of disintegration begin to appear as the primary cause of deterioration, secondary factors often contribute additional amounts of deterioration. For instance, once cracking begins to appear in the pavement surface, moisture can enter the pavement structure and accelerate the deterioration caused by the initial distress mechanism. The timely appli- cation of pavement maintenance techniques serves to help prevent or slow down the effects of both primary and secondary distress mechanisms.

Cracking

A number of different types of cracking can occur in an HMA pavement and the appropriate type of repair depends on the type of cracking present. Some cracking, such as alligator cracking, indicates load-related deterioration that requires a different maintenance strategy than block cracking, which is typically caused by climatic forces. This section provides information on some of the most common types of cracks included in a pavement evaluation.

Alligator (Fatigue) Cracking

Alligator, or fatigue, cracking appears on a pavement surface as a series of interconnected cracks that resemble the skin of an alligator. Alliga- tor cracking indicates load-related deterioration because it is caused by fatigue failure of the HMA surface under repeated traffic loadings and is often related to weakening of the base course or subgrade, insufficient pavement thickness, excessive loading, or some combination of these fac- tors. An example of alligator cracking is shown in Figure 11.9.

Traditionally, alligator cracking starts at the bottom of the asphalt layer, where the tensile stress and strain under a wheel load is highest. The cracks propagate up to the pavement surface where they first appear as parallel longitudinal cracks in the wheelpaths. Under repeated traffic loadings, further deterioration takes place and the cracks begin to interconnect, forming many angular pieces whose appearance is similar to that of an alligator’s skin or chicken wire.

In recent years, it has begun to be recognized that top-down fatigue cracking may occur in HMA pavement. These commonly occur in thicker pavements that are subjected to large horizontal tensile stresses developing in the surface

Block Cracking

Block cracks are interconnected cracks that form a series of large, rectangular pieces on the pavement surface that are typically greater than one foot (0.3 meter) in each direction. Block cracking is usually found when the HMA surface has hardened and the daily temperature cycles cause shrinkage in the HMA layer; however, it may also be caused by volume changes in a fine-aggregate HMA mix containing low-penetration asphalt and adsorptive aggregates. Low traffic levels can aggravate this latter situation because the restorative working properties of asphalt require frequent traffic applications.

Block cracking typically covers a large area of pavement, including areas without traffic. The size of the blocks and the presence of block cracking in nontrafficked areas help to differentiate block cracking from alligator cracking in the field. An example is provided in Figure 11.10.

Edge Cracking

Longitudinal cracks within one to two feet of the outer edge of a pavement are referred to as edge cracks. Over time, as vehicles pass over the weakened area, further break-up of longitudinal cracks may take place, resulting in crescent-shaped cracks that branch to the edge of the pavement. Edge cracking is illustrated in Figure 11.11.

Slippage Cracking

Slippage cracks are easily identified by their distinctive crescent- or half- moon-shape cracks, as illustrated in Figure 11.15. They are usually caused by vehicular turning or stopping movements in pavements with a low-strength surface mix or a poor bond between pavement layers. The cracks are caused by the horizontal forces of stopping or turning vehicles, so the ends of the curved crack point in the opposite direction of the vehicle thrust.

Distortion

Distortions in the pavement layer are a result of instability in the HMA layer or weaknesses in the base or subgrade layers. The distortion may appear in a number of different forms, including rutting, shoving, corruga- tions, depressions, or upheavals. Cracking may or may not accompany the distortion. Each of the typical types and causes of distortions are discussed further in this section.

Corrugations and Shoving

Rutting

A rut is a surface depression in the wheelpaths that may also have trans- verse displacement along the sides of the rut. Rutting is caused by consoli- dation or lateral movement of any of the pavement layers or the subgrade under traffic. It may be caused by insufficient design thickness, lack of compaction, weaknesses in the pavement layers due to moisture infiltra- tion, or weak asphalt mixtures. Rutting is illustrated in Figure 11.17.

Settlement or Grade Depressions

Depressions and settlements are localized pavement surface areas with elevations lower than the surrounding pavement area. Minor depressions are often not noticed until after a rain when water ponds in the depression, caus- ing a “birdbath” that can be hazardous to motorists. In dry weather, depres- sions can be observed where staining is present. Depressions may be caused by settlement or failure in the lower pavement layers or by improper con- struction techniques. An example of a depression is shown in Figure 11.18.

Upheaval or Swell An upheaval or swell is a localized upward displacement in a pavement due to swelling of the subgrade or some portion of the pavement structure.

Utility Cuts and/or Patch Failure

A patch is a portion of a pavement that has been removed and replaced or where additional material has been added. Patches are a form of pavement distress regardless of how well they are performing, but the severity of the problem increases when the patch has deteriorated. The rate at which a patch deteriorates may be influenced by poor installation techniques, such as inadequate compaction, inferior materials, or failure of the surrounding or underlying pavement. An example of a utility patch is provided in Figure 11.20.

Disintegration

Disintegration includes the breakup of the pavement into smaller pieces that may become dislodged over time. It is important to address pavement disintegration early, before too much of the pavement surface has been lost. Weathering, raveling and potholes are examples of pavement disintegration.

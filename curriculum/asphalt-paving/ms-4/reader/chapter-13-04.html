13.7.2 Stabilization with Asphalt Materials

Asphalt works best where minimal plastic clay fines are present. If sampling and testing an existing pavement reveals that some plastic clay is present, it is may be possible to treat the material with lime to reduce the plasticity in preparation for asphalt stabilization. Stabilization with asphalt materials should not be used when the PI of the soils being stabilized exceeds 10 or the minus 200 (0.075 millimeter) material exceeds 25 percent.

Emulsified Asphalt

Various asphalt emulsion binders have been used for FDR. Mixing grade asphalt emulsions (slow set cationic or anionic) are usually used, with the specific type dependent on the characteristics of the materials on site. To increase the initial strength and resistance to water damage, small amounts (1-1.5 percent) of either lime or cement can be added to a mix using an asphalt emulsion. They can be added as dry material or as slurry (lime, cement, and water). The mix design procedures discussed for use with cold-recycling should be followed when asphalt emulsions are used for FDR.

Foamed Asphalt

Foamed asphalt, also used for FDR, is created when small amounts of water and compressed air come into contact with a hot asphalt binder in a specially designed foaming nozzle. As the water vaporizes, it expands to create tiny bubbles within the asphalt binder, decreasing the asphalt binder’s viscosity and providing an appearance similar to black shaving cream. Only 1-3 percent water by weight of the asphalt is required. to foam an unmodified PG asphalt binder. The tiny foam bubbles carry a thin film of asphalt that interacts with fine aggregates to create a mortar that coats large particles. The water flashes to steam immediately during the reaction and is not part of the asphalt binder.

Foam-stabilized material can be placed, shaped, compacted, and opened to traffic shortly after mixing. It remains workable for a period of time and can be stockpiled and placed at a later date by including additional mois- ture for compaction. Generally, the material being treated with foamed asphalt should have between 5 and 20 percent minus 200 materials. Fines that exist in most crushed-limestone base materials or that are created in the reclaiming process from soil-cement base are ideal for use with foamed asphalt.

The first step in the mix design process is to optimize the foaming characteristics of the foamed asphalt. The Iowa DOT has developed an excellent procedure for designing foamed asphalt mixes. It is described in a research report, “Development of a Mix Design Process for Cold In-place Rehabilitation using Foamed Asphalt.” They use a small laboratory asphalt- foaming plant (see Figure 13.25) to produce the foamed asphalt. This equipment simulates the foamed asphalt to be used during construction. The expansion and half-life characteristics of the foamed asphalt binder are determined for at least three asphalt binder temperatures and a range of water contents of 1-5 percent by weight of asphalt binder. The com- bination of the asphalt binder temperature and water content that maxi- mizes the expansion ratio for the longest half-life is selected for use.

The optimum moisture content for the reclaimed asphalt pavement/base material is determined using standard procedures (modified Proctor) for determining the optimum density and moisture content for an unstabilized material.

To determine the optimum asphalt-binder content, samples of the reclaimed material are oven dried and cooled to room temperature and then rewetted to 90 percent of the optimum moisture content. A range of foamed asphalt contents is added to the reclaimed material, mixed thoroughly at room tem- perature, and compacted using either 50 or 75 blows with a Marshall hammer. After compaction, the samples are extruded from the molds. After extrusion, they are further cured for 3 days in an oven at 100°F (40°C). The density of the specimens is determined by measuring the bulk specific gravity. The samples should be handled very carefully after compaction. Because of the fragility of the samples, the bulk specific gravity should be determined using the CoreLok procedure discussed in Chapter 4 “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.” As described in Chapter 4, the samples are tested to determine the Marshall stability and the indirect tensile strength of the samples.

13.7.0 Construction of a FDR Pavement

Pulverization of the Existing Pavement

Before the introduction of modern reclaimers, the FDR of a pavement was done by scarifying the existing pavement system with a motor grader and large rippers attached to crawler tractors. This scarification was followed by the use of rotary mixers that pulverized the ripped material. The gradation of the reclaimed material should be monitored to ensure that the gradation being produced matches the mix design. The gradation of the reclaimed material is influenced by these factors:

® Front- and rear-door opening of the pulverization/mixing chamber. The more the door is closed, the longer the pulverized material is retained in the pulverization/chamber, which allows the larger chunks to be broken down.

° Position of the breaker bar setting in the pulverization chamber. The closer the breaker bar is to the cutting drum, the finer the gradation will be.

° The speed of the cutting drum. For thick lifts, a lower cutting drum speed is used, and for thinner lifts, a faster-cutting drum speed is used.

° The forward speed of the reclaimer. The slower the forward speed, the finer the gradation of the material will be.

The physical properties of the asphalt pavement are affected by the ambient temperature. If the pavement is cool, the asphalt layer is stiff and brittle and can be pulverized into small pieces very easily (see Figure 13.27). Increasing temperatures cause the pavement to become more plastic, and slabbing (asphalt lifting or breaking off in large pieces) can occur. The most efficient temperature range for sizing the reclaimed material is 50°-90°F (10°-30°C).

If additional pulverization or remixing is required for uniformity, the reclaimed material should be lightly compacted and reshaped. This process accurately controls the mixing depth. The rear wheels of the reclaimer compact or reduce the thickness of the fluffed-up material dur- ing pulverization. With light compaction and reshaping, the reclaimed material will have uniform thickness, which will allow better control of the treatment depth.

When an asphalt emulsion is added to the reclaimed material, a nurse truck is connected to the reclaimer (see Figure 13.28). The reclaimer is equipped with a meter capable of controlling the rate of flow and the total amount of asphalt emulsion being added to the reclaimed material. The meter should be linked to the forward speed of the reclaimer so that the amount of asphalt emulsion added will change according to the opera- tional speed of the reclaimer. If asphalt emulsion is used, some agencies add lime or cement to improve the early strength of the mix and to speed up the curing process.

13.7.4 Shaping and Compaction Procedures

A motor grader is used to move and place the reclaimed material to the desired longitudinal grade and cross-slope (see Figure 13.29). The amount of motor grader work required to place the reclaimed material depends on the original shape of the roadway, the specified final shape of the roadway, and the type of wearing surface that will be used. The final grade should be similar to that required of an untreated base course. The smoothness of the final HMA surface is directly related to the smoothness of the pulverized and reclaimed layer. To ensure a smooth final surface, tight blading or trimming the reclaimed surface may be necessary.

The reclaimed material is generally not at the optimum moisture content for compaction. Either aeration will be required to dry the reclaimed mate- rial, or moisture will have to be added to aid compaction. A water truck can be used as a supply truck to the reclaimer’s onboard liquid additive, or it can be used to add water to the reclaimed material through a spray bar.

The level of compaction achieved in the reclaimed material is directly related to the performance of the final pavement. Figure 13.30 shows a reclaimed surface before compaction. Compaction of reclaimed mixes using an asphalt emulsion as a stabilizing agent must be accomplished soon after the emulsion begins to break. This is when the emulsion begins to change from brown to black, which also indicates that the moisture is evaporating from the reclaimed material. The compaction process becomes more difficult as the emulsion breaks.

Compaction of reclaimed mixes using foamed asphalt should occur shortly after placement of the mix. The mixes will remain workable as long as the moisture content stays at or above the moisture content at the time of foamed-asphalt injection and mixing. The compaction process should be completed before the foamed asphalt mix begins to dry out.

Compaction should be done with heavy pneumatic or steel-wheel rollers. The depth of the reclaimed material that must be compacted and the desired degree of compaction will influence the weight and the amplitude/frequency of vibration for vibratory compactors and the static weight and tire pressure of pneumatic rollers. The roller pattern should be established to determine the best mixture of roller types and number of passes.

13.7.5 Inspection

Prior to the start of any FDR project, the centerline of the existing road- way should be referenced by offset markers along the edge of the roadway

outside the limits of construction. Such markers will facilitate the construc-

tion of the new pavement without the need for a survey layout crew.

During the construction process, the following should be checked:

° The treatment depth should be physically checked by removing the reclaimed material on either side of the reclaimer.

¢ The gradation of the reclaimed material should be checked periodi- cally. This generally consists of checking the maximum particle size of the reclaimed layer. The sample should be taken from the full depth to ensure that the reclaimer is treating the entire depth of the layer being reclaimed.

° The yield rate of the emulsion or the foamed asphalt and additional aggregate should be computed and compared with the mix design.

° The FDR mix should be checked to ensure that it is homogeneous, with no segregation, and that the aggregate is coated with emulsion or foamed asphalt throughout its depth. It should also be checked to

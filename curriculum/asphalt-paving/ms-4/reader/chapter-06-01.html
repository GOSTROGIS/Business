Introduction

Thickness design for flexible pavements is an effort to economize the amount and the type of material used to support the projected traffic, on the given subgrade, under the specific climate conditions at the site. Whether thickness design is applied to major interstate highways, city streets, airports, or county roads, its objective is to strike a balance between two competing goals—structural capacity and life-cycle cost.

The state-of-the-art method in flexible pavement design is the Mechanistic- Empirical (M-E) method. The M-E method typically models the pavement as a multilayered elastic system and uses the principles of mechanics to calculate deflections, stresses, and strains at any point in the structure in response to an external wheel load. Calculated strains are then compared with observed pavement performance to determine the required pavement thickness. In the case of flexible pavements, two performance criteria are widely considered critical: limiting horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the hot mix asphalt (HMA) layer to avoid fatigue, and limiting vertical compressive strain at the top of the subgrade to avoid rutting.

The Asphalt Institute method encompasses two types of design proce- dures, both of which have been incorporated into SW-1, Asphalt Pavement Thickness Design Software.

° DAMA—Advanced Structural Analysis. DAMA is a_ powerful computer-based M-E program that allows engineers to analyze any pavement system having up to five layers in which the materials can be described in terms of elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio. Engi- neers using DAMA consider monthly temperature variation, including freeze/thaw, and its effect on pavement materials to estimate remain- ing life to failure. DAMA is embedded into SW-1, Asphalt Pavement Thickness Design Software.

° Design Curves. The Asphalt Institute publishes pavement design curves in the following manuals for specific transportation design applications:

° 1S-154 General Aviation Airports

° MS-1 Highways, Roads, and Streets

° MS-11 Air-Carrier Airports

¢ MS-23 Heavy Wheel Loads (Ports/Industrial Applications)

The curves presented in these publications were produced by presolving a large number of problems over a wide range of input values using DAMA. The resulting design curves provide users with a powerful solution based on M-E, in a handy manual that can be used in the office or the field.

In order to provide a single computer-based pavement design tool, the Asphalt Institute developed SW-1, a Windows-based program that offers a comprehensive suite of design solutions for highways, streets, airports, and heavy wheel loads, based on the design curves together with an improved

6.1.1 Design Principles

The following paragraphs describe the design principles that are at the core of the Asphalt Institute methods. These principles are coded into DAMA and are inherent in the various design curves and SW-1.

DAMA models the pavement as a multilayered elastic system with up to five layers, including the subgrade (see Figure 6.1). The material in each layer is characterized by two variables—a modulus of elasticity and a Poisson’s ratio. All layers in the model are assumed to extend infinitely outward in the horizontal plane. The subgrade layer is the lowest layer in the model and is assumed to extend infinitely downward (vertically). A condition of full- friction is assumed to exist at all layer interfaces.

A proposed pavement structure is subjected to traffic loading expressed in terms of the number of repetitions of a wheel load on either a single tire or a set of dual tires.

Temperature variability is known to affect the dynamic modulus of asphalt-bound materials and the resilient modulus of granular base and subgrade soils. DAMA allows for monthly variation of the mean monthly air temperature.

The response of the pavement system to the vertical wheel load, in terms of stresses, strains, and deflections, is calculated at a point in the structure using the principles of mechanics. Two strains are believed to be critical for design purposes:

° The horizontal tensile strain on the underside of the lowest asphalt- bound layer (either asphalt concrete or emulsified asphalt base). If the horizontal tensile strain at the bottom of the lowest HMA layer is excessive, fatigue cracking will be initiated.

° The vertical compressive strain at the top of the subgrade layer. Excessive vertical strain on top of the subgrade layer will result in permanent deformation (rutting) at the pavement surface.

Strain can be calculated, in both critical positions, as a response to

repetitive wheel loads.

A cumulative damage calculation is used to determine the remaining life of the pavement structure. Miner’s Rule is used to evaluate the ratio of the number of actual load repetitions to the number of allowable load repetitions. Miner’s Rule states that the total allowable damage is equal to the summation of damage done by individual load applications. When the number of actual load repetitions is equal to the number of allowable load repetitions, cumulative damage is calculated to be 1, and a state of fail- ure exists. Economics demands that pavement designers use lower-cost unbound granular base materials when possible to reduce the initial cost of the pave- ment structure. But while these materials are less expensive to produce and place, they are not as effective in distributing traffic-induced stresses to the subgrade. As a result, thick layers are required to support even low-volume truck traffic. Typically, conventional HMA pavements consist of thick aggre- gate base layers (6-30 inches) (150-750 millimeters) coupled with relatively thin HMA surface courses @-5 inches) (75-125 millimeters). In a conven- tional design, the HMA wearing surface acts to provide a smooth riding, all- weather, waterproof surface, while the majority of the structural capacity is derived from the granular base layer. For many agencies in the United States, conventional HMA pavement sections provide an affordable solution for low- to moderate-volume roads.

Full-Depth Asphalt

A full-depth asphalt pavement is one in which asphalt mixtures are employed for all courses above the subgrade or improved subgrade. HMA or asphalt-treated base layers are placed directly on the subgrade. Asphalt-stabilized base layers in a full-depth pavement need not be constructed as thick as the comparable aggregate base layer in a con- ventional HMA pavement. The HMA wearing course creates a strong, durable, smooth, and cost-effective pavement structure.

Full-depth asphalt pavements are able to resist traffic loads in an efficient “low-profile” pavement section. Asphalt-aggregate mixtures resist these traffic-induced stresses far better than unbonded aggregate layers, which have no tensile strength. Therefore, an HMA layer spreads the wheel load over a broad area. As a result, less total pavement structure is required. Additional benefits of using full-depth asphalt pavements include reduced permeability and subsurface drainage costs, increased speed of construc- tion, and an extended paving season when placing thick lifts.

Full-depth asphalt pavements have been in place in the United States since the 1960s and 1970s, especially in high-volume traffic applications. Our collective experience over 40 years of building successful full-depth asphalt pavements has served as an important step toward developing the Perpetual Pavement philosophy.

Perpetual Pavement

Perpetual Pavements are designed and built to last longer than 50 years without requiring major structural rehabilitation or reconstruction. Like full- depth asphalt pavements, Perpetual Pavements are designed using HMA or asphalt-stabilized layers in all layers above the subgrade. What sets Perpetual Pavements apart is the additional care taken in mix type and material selection at all layers within the pavement structure.

The Perpetual Pavement concept improves upon the full-depth asphalt design method by incorporating a fatigue-resistant base layer and rut- resistant intermediate and surface layers into the pavement structure. These mix type selection techniques will minimize fatigue and rutting in the pavement structure.

Perpetual Pavement design also requires careful attention to material selection. Hard angular aggregates must be carefully selected, processed, and blended together to form a strong “stone-skeleton” to support intended traffic loads. Properly selected Performance Graded asphalt binders will improve rut-resistance at high temperatures and diminish the effect of transverse low temperature cracking. The combined result of these mix type and material selection strategies is a pavement structure that requires only periodic surface renewal in response to distresses confined to the wearing course.

It should be noted that the Perpetual Pavement design concept is separate and distinct from the Asphalt Institute thickness design method. More detail on this method is provided in Section 6.2.3.

6.1.2 Input Variables

Data-input requirements for the Asphalt Institute design method depends on the procedure selected by the user. The design curves were produced and intended for everyday use and require minimal information as input into the procedure. DAMA, on the other hand, is intended for advanced structural analysis of pavement structures with the requirement of more- detailed user inputs.

With either procedure, there are four types of inputs required to use either AI method: climate, traffic, subgrade strength, and material properties. The following is a brief description of these input variables.

Climate

Air temperature is the climate-related user input in the AI method. Air temperature affects the design of the pavement structure in two impor- tant ways. First, the stiffness of HMA and other asphalt-stabilized layers is directly dependent on the pavement temperature, which can be calculated from air temperature. For example, high pavement temperatures result in a reduction of the stiffness of HMA layers. As layers with lower stiffness do not distribute loads as effectively as stiff layers, pavements at higher temperature are more susceptible to plastic deformation caused by con- centration of Joad than pavements that are at low temperature.

For granular base and subgrade soils, temperature determines whether freezing and thawing conditions will occur. Frozen materials become stiff, while thawing weakens unbound materials, particularly subgrade soils.

In DAMA, mean monthly air temperature is used to directly character- ize the climatic variation for a specific pavement structure using location- specific temperature data. For design-curve analyses such as MS-1, the user is asked to select a mean annual air temperature appropriate for a geographical location.

Traffic The AI method is based on an analysis of individual wheel loads and the response of the pavement system to the wheel loads.

Traffic input in DAMA is a single or dual wheel load. The basic user inputs are the magnitude of the load, the contact pressure of the tire, and the number of monthly load repetitions. For dual tires, wheel-to-wheel spacing is also required. Its ability to analyze single or dual wheels allows DAMA to be applicable to virtually any pavement section being considered.

The design curves published in MS-1 are specific to highway vehicles. Rather than analyzing individual wheel loads, the curves have been devel- oped based on the Equivalent Single-Axle Load (ESAL) concept. An ESAL is a single axle carrying an 18,000-pound load distributed to the pavement. Trucks, cars, and other highway vehicles with multiple axles and a broad range of loaded weights can be equated to a corresponding number of ESALs. By predicting the total number of ESALs expected over the design life of the pavement, an engineer can enter the design curves to calculate the required pavement thickness.

For air-carrier airport design MS-11, Thickness Design—Asphalt Pavements Sor Air Carrier Airports, traffic is expressed as the number of departures of a specific aircraft type. The design curves used for port and industrial appli- cations in MS-23, Thickness Design—Asphalt Pavements for Heavy Wheel Loads, are based on a single-wheel analysis but allow the user to convert a multiple-wheel vehicle into an equivalent single-wheel load. Industrial pave- ments must be designed with knowledge of the specific vehicle loads and wheel geometry.

Subgrade Strength

Subgrade soils form the foundation of pavement structures. Some knowledge of a soil’s suitability to resist deformation under loading is required to complete a pavement design.

The basis for subgrade-strength measurement in the Asphalt Institute method is the resilient modulus, M,. This applies for both DAMA analysis and for solutions using the design curves.

In order to facilitate the use of other widely used field and laboratory tests, correlations have been established between resilient modulus and the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) and the resistance (R) value. Proce- dures for performing CBR and R-value tests are detailed in MS-10, Soils Manual for the Design of Asphalt Pavement Structures.

Material Properties

As stated earlier, DAMA allows up to five layers in the pavement section, each represented by an elastic modulus and a Poisson’s ratio. Because Poisson’s ratio is not expected to vary for a layer, the emphasis should be on properly characterizing a layer’s elastic modulus.

When working in the DAMA advanced structural-analysis mode, the user can characterize the elastic modulus of a layer by a number of means. For asphalt-bound layers, the dynamic modulus can be entered directly for each month in the year or the user can use a modulus-temperature relationship to calculate the dynamic modulus based on the monthly aver- age temperature. For specialized situations, mix volumetric properties can be prescribed to further refine the modulus-temperature relationship.

For granular layers, the DAMA user is offered two methods of characterizing resilient modulus. The user can enter the resilient modulus directly for each month of the year. For the pavement design curves published in MS-1, Thickness Design— Asphalt Pavements for Highways and Streets, MS-11, and MS-23, certain simplifying assumptions have been made regarding the material properties in the pavement structure. 6.1.3 Highways, Roadways, and Streets

The Asphalt Institute method of thickness design for pavements sup- porting highway vehicles is covered in detail in MS-1, and is one of the methods included in SW-1.

Engineers accomplish thickness design by graphical solution of the appro- priate pavement design curve. Selection of the appropriate design curve is based on climate and anticipated material type (full-depth asphalt, asphalt- over-granular base, etc). These curves represent hundreds of presolved design problems over a wide range of input values, using the DAMA analysis tool. The result is an efficient design tool that is applicable to everything from interstate highways to residential streets and all roadway classifications in between. If the pavement supports highway-type vehicles, from automobiles to long-haul tractor semi-trailer vehicles (Figure. 6.2), then the MS-1 design curves apply. This method has also been incorporated into SW-1.

In order to simplify climate inputs for the MS-1 method, users are asked to select among three mean annual air temperature (MAAT) levels: 45°F, 60°F, and 75°F (7°C, 15.5°C, and 24°C). These MAAT levels bracket those found throughout much of North America and are representative of the climates in New York, South Carolina, and Arizona, respectively. 6.1.4 Airports

Air-carrier airport pavements support heavy commercial jets with gross loads well in excess of 60,000 pounds (27,000 kilograms). A vast second- ary network of thousands of smaller airports allows the nation’s fleet of general aviation (GA) aircraft to serve local communities. GA aircraft have gross weights up to 60,000 pounds (27,000 kilograms) (Figure 6.3).

Thickness design in this method utilizes design curves that were developed from numerous runs of the DAMA analysis tool; however, for ease of use, significant simplification has reduced the number of input values required in this method.

All pavement designs for GA airports are assumed to be accomplished for a MAAT = 60°F (15.5°C), so no climate input is required. ¢ Full-depth asphalt concrete

° 2-inch (50 millimeters) HMA-over-emulsified-asphalt base

e HMA over a 6-inches (150 millimeters) layer of unbound granular base

Design thicknesses contained in the GA design charts are based on main-gear wheel loads equal to 47.5 percent of the gross weight. Above a gross weight of 30,000 pounds (13,500 kilograms), equivalent single- wheel loads were determined for dual-tire gears. A minimum total thick- ness of 4 inches (100 millimeters) of asphalt structure is recommended for these pavements, regardless of subgrade strength.

Altogether, the design curves developed for GA airports offer an easy- to-use design system based on mechanistic-empirical design principles tailored for use at general-aviation airports.

Air-carrier Airports

Air-carrier airport pavements accommodate aircraft of 60,000 pounds (27,000 kilograms) gross weight or more (Figure 6.4). The comprehensive design manual MS-11, details the air-carrier airport design method, and it is incorporated into SW-1.

The air-carrier design method uses pavement design curves developed from numerous runs of a computer-based M-E analytical model. To account for the differences between aircraft gear and wheel geometry, design aircraft passes are converted to strain repetitions of an equivalent standard aircraft.

The design curves allow variation in MAAT from 40°F (4.4°C) to 75°F (24°C).

The air-carrier design method considers only full-depth asphalt pavements, and no consideration is given to pavement sections utilizing granular base materials. This conforms to Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) design requirements for stabilized base materials for design aircraft over 100,000 pounds (45,000 kilograms) gross weight.

The basic steps of the air-carrier pavement design procedure are as follows:

1. Climate—establishing the appropriate MAAT for the project

2. Traffic—identifying design aircraft and number of departures during the design period

3. Subgrade—describing subgrade strength in terms of resilient modulus

4. Solution—working through the curve (or software) to establish the design thickness for a full-depth asphalt section.

6.1.5 Heavy Wheel Loads

Design of HMA pavements for port and industrial facilities with heavy wheel loads is based on the methods detailed in Asphalt Institute Manual

Heavy wheel-load vehicles include log-hauling trucks, dump-body haulers, forklift trucks, straddle carriers, rubber-tire hoists, and many special vehicles hav- ing four or more tires (Figure 6.5). Heavy-ioad vehicles can reach gross vehicle weights in excess of 400,000 pounds (180,000 kilograms). Unlike the standard- ization found in the highway trucking industry, there is little standardization among heavy-vehicle manufacturers for port and industrial applications. Con- sequently, wheel geometry can vary greatly from one vehicle to another.

The heavy wheel-load design curves published in MS-23 were devel- oped by presolving numerous problems using DAMA. Due to the expected low number of design vehicle passes, HMA pavements in port and indus- trial applications are not expected to fail from fatigue cracking. The design curves are based only on subgrade rutting.

6.1.6 Planned Stage Construction

Planned stage construction is the construction of roads and streets by applying successive layers of asphalt concrete according to design and a predetermined time schedule. Two possible circumstances where stage construction is advantageous are the following:

e When adequate funds are not immediately available for a full design,

but anticipated at some later date

° When long-term traffic projections (20-25 years) are not considered

reliable. In this situation, traffic estimates for the second-stage construction can be made while the facility is in use after first-stage construction.

The procedure is based on the assumption that the second stage will be constructed before the first stage shows serious signs of distress. Gener- ally, it is held that the first stage should be designed such that 60 percent of the pavement’s remaining life is used during the first stage, leaving 40 percent to be used up during the second stage.

Planned stage construction should not be confused with major maintenance or rehabilitation of existing pavements. Readers are referred to MS-1 for fur- ther details about this method and example problems.

6.1.7 Overlay Design

Design of HMA overlays is based on either the effective thickness or the deflection method, as detailed in Asphalt Institute Manual MS-17, Asphalt Overlays for Highway and Street Pavements.

Effective Thickness Method

The effective thickness procedure for structural evaluation and overlay design is based on the concept that pavements deteriorate over time as a result of load repetitions imposed by traffic. Furthermore, as a pavement uses part of its total life, it behaves as if it were an increasingly thinner pavement. Consequently, the “effective” thickness of an aging pavement is always less than its actual thickness.

The effective thickness of an existing pavement layer can be estimated from its pavement condition and knowledge of its composition and actual thickness. Conversion factors ranging from 0.0 to 1.0 are assigned to the layer based on its composition and condition. The actual thickness of the pavement

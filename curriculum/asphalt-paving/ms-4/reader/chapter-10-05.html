Pay factor (%) = 55 + 0.5 X PWL

This pay factor would then be multiplied times the contractor’s payment for that particular bid item. Assume that the project specifications establishes that payment for HMA is by the ton completed and in place. For all of the tonnage represented by that lot, the price per ton would be paid at the bid price times the pay factor. This may cause the payment to increase or decrease depend- ing on the contractor’s accomplishment of the quality characteristics that were defined. Some agencies pay bonuses if the pay factor exceeds 100 percent. Other agencies restrict the total payment to 100 percent of the bid price. When such restrictions are in place, specifications can be written to allow positive pay factors to offset negative pay factors from previous paving lots.

In the pay factor equation above, the number “55” could be changed by the specifying agency. A closer look at the equation reveals that, if 55 were used, the highest pay factor that could be achieved would be 105 percent. This means a contractor could receive a 5 percent bonus if every lot achieved 100 percent within limits (PWL). Of course, if a PWL less than 90 percent were achieved on any given lot, the pay factor would be less than 100 percent, which would reduce the contractor’s payment for that lot.

One can see that the agency can control the possible bonus by changing this pay factor variable. If the variable were set at 60, for example, the pos- sible bonus would be 10 percent. A variable of 50 would mean that nothing greater than 100 percent pay could be achieved, but payment reductions would be assessed for any PWL less than 100 percent. Slight changes to this pay factor variable have severe financial impacts on the incentive or disin- centive being offered to prospective bidders. The agency goal is to select a pay factor variable that will motivate contractors to achieve higher quality standards represented by high PWLs. In the end, this will cause the agency to pay a little more for a roadway that will last a lot longer.

Many specifications consider the fact that when mixtures are out of speci- fication tolerances, they usually incur penalties based on more than one of the test requirements. Multiple non-compliances in one lot might unrea- sonably compound the penalties, or equal monetary consideration might be given to test parameters that do not hold equal value in performance. The percent passing the No. 8 (2.36 millimeters) sieve, for example, is not as important to pavement performance as air voids are. 10.9 Performance-Based

Specifications

Public agencies and other owners of asphalt pavements generally desire to construct smooth, long-lasting pavements that are cost effective when considering maintenance and all pavement costs. These needs have driven many innovative developments and are leading to different ways of pur- chasing HMA products.

Traditionally, HMA specifications have required measurement of basic quality characteristics that are believed to relate to long-term pavement per- formance. Pavement quality is defined by how well the measured properties of the constructed pavement match contract requirements. The specifications are focused on compliance with empirically derived quality parameters.

New “performance-based” specifications are being used that define quality based on predicted performance of the completed pavement. Pay- ment is determined by comparing the life-cycle costs of the constructed pavement with those used in the pavement design. Two types of models are needed to calculate the life-cycle costs. The first are performance pre- diction models that estimate the severity, extent, and timing of distresses that will occur in the pavement. The second are maintenance cost models. They estimate life-cycle costs, including those for maintenance, repairs, and rehabilitation based on the distresses predicted.

10.9.1 Specification Definitions

Agencies and other owners of HMA pavements have used various types of specifications throughout the years. Specifications that use perfor- mance measures can be explained in the context of some of the other specifications that have been used. The first four definitions are from the AASHTO Highway Subcommittee on Construction, Quality Construction Task Force.

Method specifications are specifications that require the contractor to produce and place a product using specified materials in definite propor- tions and specific types of equipment and methods under the direction of the agency.

End-result specifications are specifications that require the contractor to take the entire responsibility for producing and placing a product. The agency’s responsibility is to either accept or reject the final product or to apply a price adjustment commensurate with the degree of compliance with the specifications.

Performance-related specifications are specifications that use quantified quality characteristics and life-cycle cost relationships that are correlated to product performance.

10.9.2 Performance-Related Specifications

Performance-related specifications use quality characteristics already being measured and specified by many agencies. These characteristics are then correlated to the life-cycle cost of the pavement.

Three key points distinguish performance-related specifications from others. One is that the quality characteristics that are tested and used for acceptance are related to fundamental engineering properties of the pavement. Another is that the fundamental engineering properties use mathematical models to predict the extent and the timing of distress in the pavement. The third point is that the specifications use the distress predic- tion in mathematically predicted life-cycle costs for establishing contract requirements, including penalties and rewards.

10.10 Performance

Specifications—Warranties

Warranties are not new to construction contracts. In recent years, however, there has been a growing interest by many owners of asphalt pavements to get long-term warranties for road construction. Warranties, or performance specifications, require a contractor to warrant that an item will perform in a manner that has been agreed to in advance.

Successful performance specifications require the contractor to be respon- sible for those specific pavement characteristics that the contractor can con- trol. Specifications that require contractors to warrant everything, including things well outside of the contractor’s control or ability to estimate, are both unfair and unwise. For example, contractors generally have no control over traffic-loading, future development, etc. It is in the owner’s interest to have clear definitions of the contractor’s responsibilities under the warranty. The warranties are normally secured by bonds. For long warranty periods, the bonds can be very expensive, and become a significant part of the cost of the project. Because of this, warranty paving projects in the United States typically have been limited to about 5 years. Requiring a 20-year bond for full reconstruction costs may be unreasonable and/or very expensive.

Warranty contracts can require the contractor to provide project accep- tance and quality-control criteria to the owner. Typically a quality-control plan including documentation of certifications for the materials used on the proj- ect will be included. The plan could provide certifications of the contractor's testing laboratory and personnel, mix design methods, sampling methods, plant operations, lay-down operations, and density control methods. Some warranty contracts also require a documentation process that will provide the agency with acceptance-type test reports and other pavement data that they would normally collect on their own projects, use with pavement manage- ment or other asset management databases.

10.10.1 Performance Characteristics

Clear identification of the required performance characteristics is a critical aspect of warranty specifications. Agencies often use key parameters tracked in their pavement management systems for acceptance criteria; therefore, smoothness, skid resistance, and pavement distress parameters are included. Performance criteria are typically established for:

° Rutting

° Fatigue cracking

° Other cracking

10.10.2 Monitoring Performance Parameters

Specific methods used to monitor performance are included in the spec- ifications, along with requirements for when and how often measurements are to be taken. The owner generally makes these measurements, and it is critical that clear acceptance criteria and detailed descriptions of conse- quences of noncompliance are understood by all parties.

10.10.3 Dispute Resolution

Many warranty specifications have alternative dispute resolution terms for resolving disagreements. A method commonly used involves selec- tion of a panel to evaluate and render an opinion on the specific item of the disagreement. The parties with the disagreement first must meet and clearly establish what the points of disagreement are. This keeps the panel on-track with the correct issues and reduces the amount of time needed to come to resolution. Each party selects one person who is independent and unaware of the dispute but has special knowledge or expertise relative to the issues disputed. A geotechnical engineer may be selected, for example, if the dispute is related to soil conditions. After each party selects one person, the two selected panel members meet and select a third. All three panel members agree to be unbiased and their costs are shared evenly. Information describing the disagreement and the position of each party is presented to the panel, which has an established time for evaluating the information. The panel then renders a decision, including any needed corrective action and an allocation of cost responsibility.

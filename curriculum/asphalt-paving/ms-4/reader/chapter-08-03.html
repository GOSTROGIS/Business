Automatic Slope Control

An HMA paver equipped with an automatic slope control can hold the screed at a different cross-slope from the slope of the surface that the paver is riding on. The slope control uses a pendulum or bubble level device to establish a perfectly vertical reference, from which a perpendicu- lar plane will establish a true horizontal line. Using this true horizontal line, a cross-slope can be created and maintained across the screed width to build pavements with a cross-slope, such as superelevated pavements on curved sections of roadway. Because the slope can be adjusted while the paver is moving, the cross-slope can be “dialed” in or out and held con- stant as the paver enters and exits a transition from flat to superelevated pavements. When automatic slope control is used in conjunction with an automatic grade control setup, the side of the screed closest to the refer- ence line will be the pivot side and the cross-slope will be set positively or negatively from this side as required by the project specifications.

References Available for Grade and Slope Control

Longitudinal reference lines for the automatic grade and slope-control sen- sors to follow allow a pavement to be built to a specific vertical and hori- zontal alignment. Fixed reference lines that are surveyed and set in advance of the paving operation are the most accurate and used mainly on new con- struction. Mobile references are devices attached to and travel along with the paver as they reference off of the existing surface that the paver is traveling on. Consequently, their use is limited to projects that have prepared surfaces that are acceptable to use as a reference. Whichever reference system is used, it is important to remember that the paver’s automatic controls will follow the reference line accurately and closely duplicate the lines and grades in the HMA pavement. Consequently, the finished HMA pavement will be no better than the reference in terms of line, grade, or smoothness.

The stringline is a wire or nylon cord supported at intervals of 25 feet (8 meters) on metal posts and rods. The wheelbase of the paver develops what is called the “line of pull,” which can be thought of as the vertical center of gravity of the paver traced longitudinally along the path of the paver. Vertically lifting only the front or the back of a paver moves the center of gravity of the paver up approximately one-half the distance lifted. Distance for Screed Change to Occur

When adjusted properly, the forces acting on a free-floating screed are balanced and it will lay a smooth, high quality HMA pavement over a rough base. As previously discussed, when the forces acting on the screed become unbalanced, the depth of the HMA pavement changes gradually as the paver moves forward and the screed again seeks equilibrium with the forces acting upon it. The distance the paver must travel to reach this equilibrium is generally 4 to 5 times the length of the side arms of the paver (see Figure 8.23). With the typical side arm length of 10 feet (3 meters), the paver will move 40 to 50 feet (12 to 15 meters) before the screed is again in equilibrium. This gradual transition over an extended length should eliminate the need for the screed operator to crank in large adjustments on the manual depth-screw mechanisms. When a paver is properly set up and paving continuously without stopping, the screed will remain in balance and the depth screws on the screed will not need man- ual adjustments. When paving with automatic grade controls and when a depth change is needed, the change can be “dialed in” by changing the depth setting in the grade control box.

Establishing the Proper Paving Speed

Planning is necessary for achieving a high quality asphalt pavement. Planning begins with establishing the amount of material to be produced, the number of trucks needed, the hauling distance, and the amount of HMA that can be placed and compacted before the temperature falls below compaction levels. The most common paver operator error is exces- sive paver speed. The paver operator must monitor the arrival rate of the material to the job site, the flow of material through the paver, and the pace of the compaction train. Paver speed should be closely monitored to match the consumption rate of material through the paver with the HMA production and delivery.

Calculating Paver Yield

During HMA placement, the paving supervisor and/or the inspector should periodically check the yield of the paving operation. The purpose is to cross-check the thickness measurements to ensure that the HMA is being placed at the proper average thickness. Some agencies use paver yield (or spread rate) as an acceptance criteria in lieu of thickness. This check can be made on each truckload of HMA, but adjustments to the thickness should not be made that often. A longer distance (1,000 feet; 300 meters) may be more appropriate.

The first step in calculating paver yield is to determine the post- compaction unit weight (pounds per square yard; kilograms per square meter) of the HMA. This can be determined from the bulk-specific grav- ity from previous cores or from nuclear density measurements. The post- compaction unit weight of the HMA and the mat’s thickness, width, and length can be used to calculate the yield. The following is an example problem and its solution:

Problem

A series of trucks has delivered 425.5 tons (386.0 tonnes) of HMA to the project. This HMA covered 3,500 feet (1,066.9 meters) of pavement. The paver is placing the HMA 12 feet (3.66 meters) wide and 1% inches (38.1 millimeters) thick (compacted). The HMA has a unit weight of 151.5 pounds per cubic foot (2,426.8 kilograms per cubic meter).

Solution (English units)

Calculate the required HMA weight:

(length x width x depth x in-place density) (2,000 pounds/ton)

(3,500 feet) x (12 feet) x (0.125 foot) x (151.5 pounds per cubic foot) (2000 pounds/ton)

= 397.7 tons of HMA

Therefore, 7.0 percent more HMA than needed is being placed (425.5/397.7) or an average of 1.60 inches (versus the 1.5 inches required).

8.4.6 Fumes Control

Fumes control systems are designed to draw the excess heat and fumes generated in the auger area away from the workers and exhaust it above their heads or draw it into the intake manifold of the paver’s engine. Baffles, ductwork, and fans are used to capture the excess heat and fumes and move them up and out of the work area. Eighty-five percent of fumes generated in the auger area are captured and exhausted out of the work area. Maintaining this level of control demands that the fumes control sys- tem be properly maintained and operated.

8.4.7 Observation of the HMA Pavement Behind the Screed

The asphalt in the head of material in the auger area must flow without hesitation under the screed into the HMA pavement being laid. Any cooler or slightly compacted chunk of asphalt that finds its way into this area must be broken up before it passes under the screed. This breaking-up process disrupts the normal flow of HMA under the screed and results in a dragging or tearing of the HMA pavement surface. Identifying and eliminating the source of the chunks is the only solution to eliminating these HMA pavement blemishes. Segregation is another HMA pavement fault revealed by observation. Longi- tudinal streaks of segregation usually indicate an auger-feeding problem. An accumulation of segregated material in and around the center auger drive box can result in a streak of segregated material 6 to 8 inches (150 to 200 millime- ters) wide appearing in the longitudinal center mat. Readjusting the volume of HMA in this area or slightly raising the augers can improve or minimize these segregated streaks. Excessive material pulled under the paver can cause similar segregated streaks on the surface or on the bottom of the mat. Properly setting the material sensors and reducing the volume of the HMA in the auger area can reduce the amount of material pulled under the belly of the paver.

Roughness

Roughness is difficult to troubleshoot. The type of roughness must be determined in order to find a solution. Examples of roughness include short or long swells, ripples, load-to-load swells, or random roughness.

Short swells or ridges, sometimes referred to as ripples, are usually the result of the screed’s angle of attack changing on its own during the paving operation. If the screed is worn or otherwise shows signs of being loose at its connections to the side arms, it can allow the angle of attack to change uncontrollably and cause these ripples. The screed- depth screws should be checked for looseness—there should be no more than one-half turn of free rotary motion when the screed is resting on a flat surface and both depth screws are in the null position (zero angle of attack). The bearings, fasteners, and screed pivot pins should also be checked for looseness. Corrections should be made to eliminate angle- of-attack changes that are not introduced by the automatic grade and slope or by the manual adjustment of the depth screws.

Swells that are longer than the length of the paver can usually be attributed to a change in the volume of the head of material. If the head of material in front of the screed is allowed to vary by more than £1 inch (25 millimeters), this type of roughness can appear. Truck exchanges should be watched carefully. The time it takes one truck to exit the paver hopper and the next one to get into the discharge position can be long enough that the flow of material going to the auger area can be interrupted. This happens because the head of material is not being replenished, allowing the screed to drop. If this happens, roughness will result with each truck exchange. This type of roughness is also known as long swells. The solu- tion is to train the truck driver and the paver operator how to exchange trucks without varying the head of material. If everyone works together and communication between paver operator, the dump operator (if used), and the truck driver is good, it can be done. The screed operator should monitor the head of material and let everyone know if the volume var- ies more than +1 inch (25 millimeters). Long swells can also occur if the stringline being used as a grade reference is allowed to sag between sup- ports or the grade sensors get out of adjustment. Constantly checking the tension in the stringline and monitoring the referencing system being used will help eliminate these issues.

Random roughness has no pattern and its cause can be difficult to iden- tify. The first step is to mark the rough areas in the HMA pavement and look for some type of pattern. Material being spilled on the grade and left in piles might be one cause. This HMA can segregate and cool enough to cause the screed to rise as it paves over the spilled material. Any changes in the speed of the paver should be noted. If the paver speeds up when material is available and slows down when it is not, roughness in the HMA pavement can result. Reflective roughness should always be a concern while paving over a poor base. A yielding subgrade can result in random roughness during compaction.

8.5 Joint Construction

3.5.1 Transverse or Construction Joints

Good transverse construction joints, like all good HMA joints, are straight, with a vertical face on the cold side of the joint. The cold face of the joint should be vertical. There are several ways to establish and maintain the vertical face on the cold side while traffic is rolling off the edge. The easiest and most economical method is to build a wedge of sacrificial material that ramps down to the underlining surface and stays in place until the paving operation is resumed, at which time the material in the wedge is removed. There are three methods of properly building the sacrificial wedge:

° In the most widely used method, the paver is stopped and the screed is lifted over the head of material in the auger area. The excess mate- rial, which is to be used to build the wedge, is then separated by shoveling a trench in the hot material just downstream from the joint. The side of the joint that remains in place should be pushed back with a lute and a sharp vertical edge built. A bond-breaking material (plastic, Kraft paper, or sand) should be used to line the bottom of the wedge area and cover the vertical face (see Figure 8.24). The following precautions apply when constructing a transverse joint:

° Wet diamond sawing should not be used because a slurry is pro- duced, leaving a dust film on the joint’s vertical face and staining the pavement.

° Cold milling in the longitudinal direction to remove the wedge should not be allowed because the cutting mandrel cannot produce a vertical face.

° Bond breakers and wooden blockouts should not be used on airfield pavements opened to aircraft traffic.

To resume paving from a transverse joint, the wedge and any bond breaker material must be removed and properly disposed. If the bond breaker worked well, this should not be a difficult task. Once the wedge is removed, the area must be clean and dry before tack is applied to both the vertical face of the joint and the surface to be paved (see Figure 8.25). The tack on the vertical face will allow the hot side of the joint to adhere to the cold side and seal out moisture and contaminants that could cause the joint to ravel.

When constructing a transverse joint, to both stop and resume paving, it is good practice to use a 10-foot (-meter) straightedge to check the pave- ment leading into and away from the joint for smoothness before compaction begins. If this check is done while the material is still hot, corrections can be made by adding or removing material to achieve acceptable mat smoothness.

If the temporary wedge is going to be used by traffic for only a short time (overnight), the application of the tack coat for the wedge can be eliminated, which will greatly facilitate removal.

8.5.4 Notched-Wedge Joints

The notched-wedge joint was developed to help prevent premature failures of longitudinal joints. The notched wedge (see Figure 8.27) can improve the joint structure over the vertical edge joint by increasing the bond surface area between the two mats. The initial pass is laid with a 1-foot (300-millimeter) taper with a built-in 4- to 1-inch (19-25 millimeter) notch or vertical face at the end of the taper. The notch prevents the frac- turing of aggregate when the joint is rolled by preventing the wedge from being tapered down to zero.

Another plus for the notched wedge is traffic safety after the first pass is laid. The notched wedge provides a ramp for traffic to get safely from one side of the joint to the other. Because of this safety feature, paving can continue the entire day on one side of the pavement and move back the next day to pull the lanes even, instead of moving back at mid-day. The screed extension that lays the notched wedge is available from several manufacturers whose operational procedures must be followed to pro- duce a sound, durable longitudinal joint.

Related Paving Operations

8.6.1 Hand-Spreading Operations

When the major portion of a project will be placed manually without the aid of a paving machine, mixes should be designed for good workability. A good mix design technician has several options for making mixes that are easier to place manually.

Placing and spreading by hand should be done very carefully, with the material distributed uniformly to avoid segregation of the coarse aggre- gate. When the HMA is dumped in piles, it should be placed in a man- ner where the shovelers have to move the least amount of material the least distance. Sufficient space should be available so that workers are not required to stand in the HMA.

HMA should not be broadcast or spread from shovels because segregation will result. Rather, it should be deposited from shovels into small piles and spread with lutes. Any part of the HMA that has formed into lumps and does not break down easily when struck with a shovel edge should be discarded. After the material has been placed, and before rolling starts, the surface should be checked with straightedges, and all irregularities should be corrected. On cool days, careful attention must be paid to the HMA temperature. Rolling should begin as soon as possible to ensure adequate compaction.

8.6.2 Supplemental Operations and Appurtenances

Items such as roadway widening, shoulders, superelevated curves, curbs, ditches and revetments are considered supplemental paving opera- tions. In some cases, such as roadway widening and shoulder construc- tion, specially designed pieces of equipment and attachments allow the work to be done easily and efficiently. Certain roadway appurtenances, such as asphalt curbs, dikes, ditches, spillways, and slopes, are increas- ingly becoming part of paving contracts. For these jobs, special paving equipment or special HMA mixtures (or both) may be required.

Roadway Widening and Shoulders

Two-lane pavements today should be 24~28 feet (7.3-7.9 meters) wide. Many thousands of miles of primary routes are now inadequate in width and thickness. Narrow roads should be widened before they are strength- ened with overlays. Skillful planning can greatly improve the original alignment.

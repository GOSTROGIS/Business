4.6 RAP Mix Design

Hot mix recycling consists of combining reclaimed (or recycled) asphalt pavement (RAP) with new or “virgin” aggregates, and new asphalt binder and/or a recycling agent in a central plant to produce a new HMA mix. The amount of RAP used in an HMA mix is related to the plant type being used, the gradation of the RAP aggregates, and the properties of the asphalt binder in the RAP. The ratio of RAP to virgin aggregates will vary. It is typi- cally 15 to 25 percent for batch plants, and up to 30 to 50 percent for drum plants. The manufacture of an HMA mix using RAP is discussed in detail in Chapter 7, “Manufacture of Hot Mix Asphalt.” This section will discuss the mix design procedures. The procedures outlined below are based on AASHTO M 323, Superpave Volumetric Mix Design, and the NCHRP report Recommended Use of Reclaimed Asphalt in Superpave Mix Design Method: Guidelines.

4.6.1 Materials Evaluation

The first step in the evaluation of the materials for a project that will include RAP in the HMA is to obtain a representative sample of the RAP to be used. A major concern with the use of RAP is the variability of the material. This is because the RAP may come from different layers in the pavement structure or from stockpiles of materials from different sources. Good stockpiling prac- tices or processing the RAP by crushing and/or screening will reduce the vari- ability. RAP can be sampled from the roadway (by coring or bulk samples), from a stockpile, or from the haul trucks. Good sampling practices should be followed. Sampling methods are discussed in detail in Chapter 10.

Aggregate Gradation

The properties of the aggregate after the asphalt has been extracted from the RAP should be determined. The ignition oven (AASHTO T 308 or ASTM D 6307) can be used to determine the RAP composition (asphalt content and gradation) if further testing of the asphalt binder will not be accomplished. If the extracted asphalt binder is to be recovered and tested for future evaluation, AASHTO T 164 or ASTM D 2172 should be used for the extraction process. After extraction of the asphalt binder from the mix, the gradation of the aggregate is determined. Any deficiency in the grada- tion can be corrected by blending the appropriate fractions of virgin and reclaimed aggregate to meet the gradation requirements of the final HMA mixture. It is also recommended that the angularity of the fine and coarse aggregates be determined. This information may influence the percentage of RAP to be used for a particular project.

To calculate the VMA, it is necessary to determine the combined bulk specific gravity of the aggregates (RAP and virgin aggregates). Two approaches are used. The first approach consists of splitting the extracted aggregate into fine (minus No. 4 [4.75 millimeters]) and coarse fractions (plus No. 4 [4.75 millimeters]), and determining the specific gravity of each fraction. The advantage is that this provides a direct measure of the specific gravity of the aggregate. The disadvantages are that the extraction process (chemical or ignition) will damage the aggregate or that the samples will contain asphalt or solvent, which will make it difficult to accurately mea- sure the specific gravity. The second approach consists of determining the maximum specific gravity of the RAP (G__) using AASHTO T 209 or ASTM D 2041, determining the asphalt content of the RAP, and then calculating the effective specific gravity (G,.) of the aggregate. The advantage of this method is that it is more accurate than the determination of the bulk specific gravity of the individual fractions. The disadvantage is that using G,, rather than G,, of the RAP aggregate will result in an overestimate of the combined bulk specific gravity and will artificially raise the VMA of the HMA mix. The error will increase with higher percentages of RAP.

Asphalt Properties

The extracted asphalt obtained using AASHTO T 164 or ASTM D 2172 is recovered from the extract solution using either AASHTO T 170 or ASTM 1856, Recovery of Asphalt from Solution by Abson Method, or by ASTM D 5404, Recovery of Asphalt from Solution Using the Rotavapor Apparatus. The Rotavapor procedure is the preferred method because it results in less severe changes in the binder properties.

This recovered binder is used to construct blending charts and to char- acterize the high-, intermediate-, and low-temperature properties of the binder. Approximately 50 grams of recovered binder is needed to conduct these tests. The testing should include dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) tests on the recovered asphalt before and after aging in the rolling thin film oven (RTFO). Bending beam rheometer (BBR) tests should also be conducted on the recovered asphalt after aging in the RTFO.

4.6.2 Mix Design

Determine Combined Aggregate in Recycle Mix

The combined gradation for the HMA mix using RAP must meet the specifying agency’s requirements. To accomplish this, the contractor will blend the aggregates as discussed in Chapter 3. The gradation to be used for this analysis should be the extracted aggregate gradation.

Asphalt Demand of Combined Aggregate

The asphalt demand for the HMA mix using RAP will be determined using one of the standard design procedures: Superpave, Marshall, or Hveem. The final asphalt content will consist of the asphalt in the RAP and the virgin asphalt to be added during the production process. A major advantage of the use of RAP is that it reduces the need for new, virgin asphalt binder.

4.7 Aggregate Packing Analysis Using the Bailey Method

A procedure to evaluate the aggregate packing properties in HMA, called the Bailey Method, is able to predict volumetric and compactability charac- teristics of HMA. The system was first developed by Robert D. Bailey, who worked with the Illinois District 5 Materials Bureau. Further refinements have been developed by the Heritage Research Group of Indianapolis, Indiana.

The procedure is based on the following four main principles:

1. % PCS—Percent passing the Primary Control Sieve 2. CA Ratio—The Coarse Aggregate Ratio

3. FA, Ratio—The Fine Aggregate Coarse Ratio

4. FA, Ratio—The Fine Aggregate Fine Ratio

Percent PCS

The Bailey Method is based on the concept that the coarse Cargest) particle sizes of any mixture creates voids that are occupied by finer aggre- gates, asphalt, and air. The Primary Control Sieve (PCS) is the aggregate size that determines what is coarse and what is fine for any aggregate or aggregate combination. The volume of coarse aggregate (retained on the PCS) determines if a mix is a “coarse graded mix,” a “fine graded mix” or a stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mix.

A coarse graded mix is defined as mixture that has a volume of coarse aggregate great enough to develop stone-on-stone contact. The voids, in the coarse aggregate then determine the amount of fine aggregate that can be accommodated in the mix.

An SMA mix is a coarse graded mixture that contains a volume of fine aggregate that is significantly less than the volume of voids created by the coarse aggregate.

A fine graded mix is defined as a mixture that contains a volume of fine aggregate that exceeds the volume of voids that exist in the coarse aggregate when stone-on-stone contact is achieved. The volume of voids in the coarse aggregate is determined utilizing methods described in AASHTO T 19.

The Bailey Method has the ability to estimate the change in VMA (voids in the mineral aggregate) based solely on the change in gradation between two samples. It will not calculate VMA prior to laboratory testing. Once a known VMA is determined on a compacted specimen, the amount and direction of change in VMA can be estimated by the Bailey Method based on gradation changes. However, it is extremely important to remember that the rate of change and direction of change is different for each of the three mix types.

CA, FA, and FA, Ratios

The CA, FA, and FA, ratios are factors used in the Bailey Method that are calculated on specific fractions of the combined aggregate used in HMA. The CA ratio describes the distribution of large coarse aggregate particles and smaller coarse aggregate particles. This distribution of par- ticles retained on the PCS determines the number and sizes of voids cre- ated by the coarse fraction of the mix. The FA, ratio provides a comparison of coarse sand (material passing the PCS) and fine sand. The coarse sand fraction of the mix creates voids within the coarse aggregate structure that are filled by fine sand. The FA, ratio is very similar to the FA, ratio but deals with the finest fractions in the mixture. VMA movement and compaction characteristics due to gradation change are influenced, described, and pre- dicted by the CA, FA, and FA, ratios.

The Bailey Method is a very powerful tool to analyze VMA and com- pactability of HMA mixtures that cannot be covered in depth in this man- ual. Transportation Research Board Circular #E-C044 provides in-depth discussion of the method.

4.8.1 Stone Matrix Asphalt Mix

Stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixtures have been used in the United States since 1991. These mixtures have performed well in Europe for many years, and experience in the United States is similar. SMA is a rut-resistant, gap- graded HMA. The mix relies on stone-on-stone contact to provide its internal strength. The mix uses a high asphalt binder content of 6 to 7 percent or more. This high binder content provides improved durability. In most situations, the asphalt binder is a polymer-modified asphalt. Mineral filler and either mineral or cellulose fibers are added to form a stiff mortar and prevent draindown of the asphalt cement. The in-place air-voids are kept low—to less than 6 per- cent to insure that the mix is impermeable to water. SMA is routinely used in many states as the surface of choice for high-volume roadways and intersec- tions. Figure 4.24 shows an SMA mix in Colorado.

Materials Selection

The materials required for an SMA mix include aggregates, asphalt binder, mineral filler, and a stabilizer, which is usually either a cellulose or a mineral fiber.

SMA mixtures require the use of a high-quality aggregate. AASHTO speci- fications require that 100 percent of the coarse aggregate have at least one crushed face and at least 90 percent must have two or more crushed faces. The strength of an SMA mixture is achieved through stone-on-stone contact. Therefore, most agencies have placed more stringent requirements on aggre- gate used in SMA. AASHTO requires a maximum Los Angeles Abrasion loss of 30 percent. But some states—because of a lack of high-quality aggregates— have built successfully performing projects with a less-stringent requirement.

Note that for the 19 millimeters, 12.5 millimeters, and 9.5 millimeters mixes, the mixtures have a low percentage passing the No. 4 sieve (4.75 millimeters) and a high percentage passing the No. 200 sieve. Selection of Desired Gradation

Once satisfactory materials have been identified, the optimum aggregate gradation and binder content must be determined. This is accomplished by first selecting an appropriate aggregate blend. This blended gradation should provide an aggregate skeleton with stone-on-stone contact and furnish a mix- ture that meets or exceeds the minimum voids in mineral aggregate. The con- dition of stone-on-stone contact is defined as the point at which the voids in the coarse aggregate fraction of the blend of the compacted SMA mixture (VCA, ,.) is less than the VCA of the coarse aggregate fraction of the aggregate blend alone (VCA,,.) in the dry-rodded test (AASHTO T 19 or ASTM C 29).

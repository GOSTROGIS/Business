15.1 Cold Mix Asphalt

15.1.1 Introduction

Cold mix asphalt preparation is not as complicated as that described for hot mixes in Chapter 7. Generally, placing and compacting does not usually call for equipment as sophisticated as that required for hot mix asphalt. However, this type of pavement construction is no less important, because a large percentage of the nation’s roadways have cold mix asphalt bases and even surfaces.

A less-controlled, simplified process is an advantage during in situ cold mix preparation with a distributor and a motor-grader operation. But the same quality control as that found in hot mix asphalt preparation is possible when a cold mix is prepared in a plant-mix operation. However, many plants used for producing cold mixes do not use, or have, all the means for maintaining close uniformity of control. Wherever suitable materials are available, such plant-mixing procedures are employed for high production and low cost.

Cold mix recycling or reworking of existing asphalt pavement or aggre- gate structures is another important pavement construction alternative. Cold mix recycling is the process by which reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), reclaimed aggregate materials (RAM), or both are combined with new asphalt in place or at a central mixing facility to produce cold mix base mixtures. For more complete information on cold mix recycling, see Chapter 13, “Rehabilitation Methods for Asphalt Pavements.”

15.1.2 Description

Cold-placed asphalt pavement mixtures (cold mixes) are generally made with emulsified or cutback asphalt. Emulsified asphalt may be anionic or cationic MS and SS grades. Cutback asphalt may be MC or SC grades. Special additives to the emulsions add a High Float (HF) designation to the grading system and will slow the breaking of the emulsion when the reclaimed material has a high percentage of fine material or minus No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. The Asphalt Institute’s MS-19, Asphalt Emulsion Manual should be consulted for more information.

The aggregate material may be anything from a dense-graded crushed aggregate to a granular soil having a relatively high percentage of dust. The aggregate at the time of mixing might be damp, air-dry, or artificially heated and dried.

Mixing occurs either in the roadway, along the roadway, or in a stationary mixing facility. The resulting mixtures are usually spread and compacted at ambient temperature; however, there are exceptions. Some asphalt mixes

15.1.3 Suitability for Pavement Construction

Cold-laid asphalt mixes may be used for surface, base, or subbase courses if the pavement structure is properly designed. Cold-laid surface courses are suit- able for light and medium traffic. When used in the base or subbase, they may be suitable for all types of traffic. Also when used on large jobs, cold-laid paving mixtures reduce construction costs, but not necessarily the strength or quality of the pavement structure. High quality cold mixes, if properly designed and constructed, can provide a structural equivalency equal to that of hot mixes.

Asphalt pavements made by cold-laid methods can be designed and built to meet current traffic requirements. A thicker pavement can be achieved by adding additional aggregate material if required, during in-place mixing or by adding additional pavement courses.

15.1.4 Mix Composition

A wide variety of aggregates and soil-aggregate combinations can be used in cold-mixes. In many cases, material from road cuts, pits, or nearby quarry sites may be economically crushed or used. Often the entire output of a rock crusher less than a certain maximum size is used.

Suitable local materials also include granular soils having up to 20 percent passing the No. 200 (75 microns) sieve, as well as clean sands and gravel.

Granular materials that are mostly fine aggregate and have a relatively high percentage of mineral dust may cause mixing, aeration, and compac- tion problems.

Well-graded aggregates are always desirable for any course of the asphalt pavement structure. However, poorly graded and gap-graded aggregates can be adequate for base courses when they are properly prepared by road mixing methods and procedures.

Sand Mixes

Except for aggregate gradation, the same basic principles apply for production of sand-emulsion plant mixes as for dense-graded, coarse- aggregate emulsion mixes. Sand mixes may be used for either base or surface courses.

The addition of 1 to 2 percent Portland cement will aid in the develop- ment of early initial strength. Thorough mixing is essential to uniform distribution of the cement throughout the mixture.

A wide variety of fine aggregates throughout the country have produced satisfactory results. Blending of two or more aggregates may be necessary to produce the desirable mix characteristics. Typical gradations are listed in Table 15.4.

The emulsified-asphalt content usually varies from 6 to 10 percent. Emul- sion grades SS-1, SS-1h, CSS-1, CSS-Ih, or HFMS-2s may be used, along with a laboratory-determined amount of mixing water added to the sand.

15.1.8 Construction Methods

Roadbed Preparations

The roadbed on which cold-mixed material is placed must be shaped and compacted. The surface must be swept with a power broom to remove dirt and other foreign matter. Depending upon the condition or type of base or subgrade, a prime coat of MC-30 or MC-70 may be necessary. In some regions, environmental regulations have restricted the use of MC and RC cut- back asphalts. After application, the prime coat is allowed to cure, and after 24 hours, any excess asphalt remaining on the surface is blotted with sand.

Windrows

In several types of road-mix construction, it is necessary to place the aggregate into windrows. Windrow sizers are used to ensure that the cor- rect volume for the desired depth of pavement is placed. Usually, loose material on the unpaved road surface is of too small a quantity and not uniform enough in size for use in the road mix. In such cases, the mate- rial is bladed onto the shoulders. If the material from the roadway can be practically used, it is bladed into a windrow and measured. Additional aggregate may be added as necessary to improve the gradation or to pro- vide the required volume.

If two or more materials are combined on the area being surfaced, each is placed in its own sized windrow. These windrows are then bladed back and forth to thoroughly mix them and combine them into a single wind- row before asphalt is added.

15.1.9 Methods of Preparing Mixes

The four techniques for preparing paving mixtures for cold-laid pave- ments are the following:

° Blade mixing

° Rotary mixing

° Travel-plant mixing

¢ Stationary mixing facilities

All but stationary plant mixing are considered mixed-in-place tech- niques. The principal advantage of using mixed-in-place techniques for construction is that they use aggregate already on the site or available from nearby sources. The aggregate and asphalt can be mixed in place with minimal equipment.

The in-place mixing systems provide low-cost techniques for production of cold mixes. It is not possible to achieve 100 percent asphalt coating of the aggregate. Sufficient coating can be achieved to produce a tightly bonded mixture. The mixing procedure selected should be one that mini- mizes the amount of uncoated aggregate particles.

Stationary mixing facilities provide opportunities for better control of propor- tioning and mixing. In some cases, such as those mixes made with emulsified asphalt, no heating or drying of the aggregate before mixing is necessary.

Whenever asphalt emulsions are used, the aggregate should be damp- ened before introducing the emulsion. Dampening slows the breaking of the emulsion. Otherwise, the dry aggregate absorbs the water in the emulsion, which makes coating the aggregate particles very difficult. A good practice is to determine the optimum fluid content (a combination of moisture and emulsion, equal to the optimum moisture of the aggregate) for the mix. That amount of moisture (optimum moisture content minus the emulsion to be added) plus about 2 percent should be applied to the aggregate before introducing the emulsion. This process yields good uni- form mixing, with well-coated aggregate particles, and provides the added moisture (which will evaporate during handling and placing of the mix) needed to ensure proper fluid content at the time of compaction.

Blade Mixing

Using motor graders or some type of multiple-blade drag to blend the asphalt and the aggregate usually accomplishes blade mixing (Figure 15.2). The aggregate is first air dried by manipulating it across the roadway with the grader to expose it to the sun and air. After the moisture content has been reduced to 3 percent or less, the windrow is spread uniformly over about half the roadway.

Asphalt is then applied from an asphalt distributor in two or three spraying passes. Immediately following each application, the asphalt and aggregate are partially mixed to leave as little free asphalt as possible on the surface and to prevent pools of asphalt from forming.

After receiving the full predetermined amount of asphalt, the partially mixed material is formed back into a windrow. It is then worked back and

Rotary Mixing

A rotary-type mixer consists of a mobile mixing chamber mounted on a self-propelled machine (see Figure 15.3). The chamber is open at the bot- tom and has widths ranging from 6 to 10 feet (1.8 to 3.0 meters). Inside the chamber are one or more transverse rotating drums that contain attached carbide-cutting teeth. This type of equipment is particularly useful for mixed-in-place cold recycling when old hot or cold mix surfaces are to be incorporated into the mix. The blades or cutting teeth serve a twofold purpose—cutting the in-place material to a specified depth and mixing it with asphalt. As the machine moves forward, it strikes off the freshly mixed material to a predetermined level. The asphalt can be introduced in one of two ways. 4, Make one or more passes of the mixer between applications of asphalt, as necessary to ensure thorough mixing.

5. Maintain the surface true to grade and cross section by using a motor grader during the mixing operations.

6. Aerate the mixture by additional manipulation, if necessary.

Travel plants should be capable of proportioning and thoroughly mixing asphalt and aggregates, uniformly dispersing the asphalt, and adequately coating the aggregate particles.

The asphalt application rate for the windrow pickup plant must be accu- rately matched with the width and thickness of the course, and the forward speed of the mixer. If all the asphalt cannot be incorporated in one pass, the windrow should be split into two or more windrows and the asphalt added to each windrow for mixing. Depending on the type of travel plant and mix components, further mixing of the recycled material may be nec- essary with a motor grader. This ensures that all the windrowed material is incorporated into the mix. It also aerates the mixture. The number of passes with the motor grader varies with job conditions.

Travel-plant mixing offers the advantage of achieving closer control of the mixing operation than is possible with blade mixing. The proportion- ing of the asphalt with the aggregate, as well as the uniformity with which the two can be incorporated, is of great importance.

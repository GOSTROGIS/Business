14.4 Rubblization

Rubblization is the process of fracturing the existing PCC pavement in-place into small, interconnected pieces that serve as a base course for a new hot mix asphalt (HMA) overlay. The rubblization process destroys slab action, eliminating the possibility of reflection cracking.

14.4.1 Project Selection and Overview

Whether a pavement is a good candidate for rubblization is a judgment to be made by a qualified engineer. Generally, PCC pavements that require reconstruction or unbonded PCC overlays may also be candidates for rub- blization. The Wisconsin Department of Transportation considers the use of rubblization when one or more of the following conditions are met:

¢ More than 20 percent of the concrete pavement joints needs repair.

* More than 20 percent of the concrete pavement surface has been

patched.

° More than 20 percent of the concrete slabs exhibits slab breakup

distress.

* More than 20 percent of the project exhibits longitudinal joint distress

greater than 4 inches wide.

Unlike other fractured slab techniques, rubblization is suitable for any type of PCC pavement. It is the only method that can be used on continuously reinforced concrete (CRCP). CRCP exhibiting more than five punch-outs per mile should be considered as candidates for rubblization and overlay.

The equipment and processes that have evolved for rubblization were developed for use on relatively thick PCCP slabs constructed over an improved subbase. Thin (less than 6 inches, 150 milimeters) slabs placed directly over poorly drained fine-grained subgrade soils are not good can- didates for rubblization.

14.4.2 Design Considerations

The thickness of the HMA overlay should be determined according to the principles outlined in Chapter 6. Falling-weight deflectometer (FWD) testing on rubblized pavements and subsequent back-calculation analysis has consis- tently shown that the layer coefficient of a rubblized concrete pavement layer is greater than that of a crushed-aggregate base course. Most airfield agencies have structurally characterized a rubblized layer conservatively by treating it as equivalent to a crushed-aggregate base having a CBR of 100. 14.4.3 Construction

The procedural steps in the rubblization technique are essentially the same for airfields and highways. These steps are the following:

° Prepare the surface and remove any existing overlays.

¢ Install a subdrainage system (if necessary).

° Rubblize the concrete pavement.

° Cut off and remove any exposed steel reinforcement.

* Remove exposed joint-sealing material.

° Roll the rubblized concrete pavement.

° Remove and patch any unstable areas.

° Place asphalt HMA leveling course/HMA overlays.

Prepare the Surface

For the rubblization equipment to work properly, any material from a previ- ous overlay must be removed before rubblizing the underlying concrete.

Adjacent pavements that are not to be rubblized must be isolated from the rubblized pavements. This is best accomplished by a full-depth sawcut or by cutting a relief trench with a wheel saw. Either method allows the rubblizing equipment to work up to the project limits without damaging the structural integrity of the pavement that is to remain in place.

Underground structures, utilities, and in-pavement fixtures must be identified as needing protection during the rubblization process. Isola- tion or removal and replacement may be required during the rubblization process. Provisions must also be made to raise all in-pavement features to match the grade of the new HMA pavement.

install a Subdrainage System

A key element to a successful rubblization project is to assure that no free water exists immediately under the PCC pavement. This often requires the installation of a properly designed underdrain system. Underdrain systems serve two purposes: before rubblization, a subsurface drainage system dries and stabilizes the subbase or subgrade, and during the service life of the new pavement it prevents water from becoming trapped within the pave- ment structure.

Any free water trapped in the subbase or subgrade will weaken the subgrade and cushion the impacts from the pavement breakers. This loss of subgrade support reduces the ability of the equipment to properly frac- ture the concrete. To ensure proper subgrade drainage, many rubblization specifications require drainage to be installed a set period of time (often two weeks) before commencing rubblization.

During the service life of the pavement, the underdrain system must have sufficient capacity and depth to collect and carry the water being discharged from the subgrade, the old permeable base, and the top or “permeable zone” of the rubblized layer (see Figure 14.10). The upper 2 inches of the rubblized concrete is porous, but the remainder of the layer does not appear to be highly permeable. The edge drainage system should be designed to collect and dispose of moisture directly below the interface between the rubblized PCCP and the new HMA layer. The trench depth must also be deep enough to accomplish the intended drainage function. It is recommended that the trench be deep enough to allow the top of the pipe to be located a minimum of 2 inches (5 centimeters) below the bot- tom of the permeable base material. The filter fabric and trench backfill material must also be designed with sufficient capacity to handle the out- flow from the new pavement structure. Erosion of fines into the side-drain system should not pose problems because the base, or rubblized concrete, should not contain erodable fines that might clog the system.

Polyethylene tubing used in agriculture and thin “fin-style” drains like those used along highways do not have sufficient strength or capacity to handle the outflow and should not be used for airfield underdrains. Pipe underdrains, such as rigid corrugated PVC with smooth interior walls, are recommended because of their strength, high flow capacity, and configuration for easy maintenance.

Installation of the outlet pipe is critical to the edge-drainage system. It is recommended that a metal or rigid (PVC) nonperforated pipe be used for the outlet pipe because of its strength and capacity. A grade of at least 3 percent is recommended so that the pipe will continue to drain if there is a slight variance of the pipe grade. Because the purpose of subsurface drainage is to remove water as quickly as possible, it is recommended that the outlet spacing be a maximum of 250 feet (75 meters). The edge- drainage design should be coordinated with surface drainage to handle removal of all sources of water.

Rubblize the Concrete Pavement

There are two basic types of rubblization equipment: the Resonant Pave- ment Breaker (RPB) and the Multihead Breaker (MHB). These two machine types are described below.

Resonant Pavement Breaker

The concept for resonant rubblization was developed by Ray Gurries as a means to facilitate the removal of reinforced PCC slabs. High-frequency (42-46 Hz), low-amplitude (less than 1 inch; 25 millimeters) blows are generated by an oscillating beam and applied through a steel-breaking shoe. Diagonal cracks are induced through the entire thickness of the slab, while the PCC near the surface is crushed, resulting in smaller particles near the surface. The broken concrete is debonded from any reinforcing steel and remains tightly interlocked.

The RPB rubblizes the PCC pavement in narrow strips as the machine moves forward adjacent to the edge of a previously fractured pavement. It continues with successive passes until it has moved transversely across the width of the pavement. The forward speed of the machine depends on the size of the machine, slab thickness, stiffness of the PCC, and the support provided by the subgrade. The machine is operated at a forward speed of less than 5 miles per hour (8 kilometers per hour). One machine can be expected to rubblize 6,000-8,000 square yards (5000-7000 square meters) per workday shift, with thick airfield pavement work being on the low end of this range. It is not unusual for multiple machines to be employed to expedite the process on major interstate projects.

As an RPB fractures an existing PCC pavement, a small amount of dila- tion takes place. For extremely wide pavements, such as airport runways and taxiways, relief trenches sometimes have to be cut full depth through the pavement at fixed intervals to allow for horizontal expansion and to reduce the confining pressure at interior locations on these pavements. This is usually not necessary for highway pavements. To remain intact, any exist- ing PCC pavement must be isolated by using a full-depth sawcut to avoid damage. No damage has been observed from operating a resonant breaker adjacent to new HMA placed during shoulder or edge-drain installation.

Multiple-Head Pavement Breaker

The multiple-head pavement breaker is a rubber-tire, self-contained, self- propelled unit (see Figure 14.12) that breaks the PCC using a series of drop hammers. It consists of six pairs of hammers—each weighing 1,200 pounds (544 kilograms) measuring 8 inches (20 centimeters) in width—mounted laterally in pairs, with half the hammers in a forward row and the remainder diagonally offset in a rear row so the breakage is continuous from side to side. Wing units, carrying two 1,500-pound (680 kilograms) hammers, can be added to each side for a total breaking width of up to 13 feet (4 meters). In the 16-hammer configuration, the MHB weighs 57,000 pounds (25,855 kilograms). Each pair of hammers is attached to a separate hydraulic lift cylinder with an adjustable drop height from 60 inches (0-1.5 meters). Each pair operates as an independent unit, and develops 1,000-8,000 foot-pounds of energy, depending on the drop height selected, and cycles at a rate of 30-35 impacts per minute.

By controlling the individual lift cylinder for each pair of hammers, the MHB can rubblize in widths of 2-13 feet (0.6-4 meters) at a produc- tion rate of 500-1500 square yards (418-1254 square meters) per hour. This allows the multiple-head breaker to rubblize lanes that are 12.5 feet (3.8 meters) wide, while traveling at speeds of 0.07-0.20 miles per hour (0.11-0.32 kilometers per hour). Production rates are comparable to the RPB and depend on the strength and thickness of the slab and the underly- ing subgrade.

Rubblization using an MHB requires a single pass over an area to be rubblized. The tractor or power unit travels on the unbroken slab as it moves forward and tows the breaking unit behind. Because it is a single- pass technique, the MHB does not have to travel on the rubblized surface. Fracturing of the slab is controlled by a set combination of drop height, frequency of the impacts, and the forward speed of the machine, as deter- mined by a test strip/pit.

Part of the process of using an MHB is to roll the broken pavement using a smooth steel-wheel roller onto which a Z-grid pattern has been attached. This breaks any elongated surface particles into a more desir- able, cubical shape. Its operation is described later in this section.

Cut Off and Remove Any Exposed Steel Reinforcement

Reinforcing steel or wire mesh that emerges above the surface during rubblization must be removed by cutting with a torch that is flush with the sur- rounding surface. Otherwise, reinforcing steel, wire mesh, and load-transfer devices should be left in place.

Remove Exposed Joint-Sealing Material

Joint sealant materials such as rubberized asphalt and low modulus silicone are usually loosened during rubblization but often left in place. Any wood joint fillers should be removed before rubblizing the PCC pavement.

Roll the Rubblized Concrete Pavement

The PCC pavement must be rolled after rubblizing. Rolling tightens the surface by seating loose particles as well as by smoothing the surface in preparation for paving. This rolling is not intended to achieve additional densification of the rubblized material or underlying layers. Excessive roll- ing could reduce particle interlock and cause a weakening of the rubblized layer.

When the rubblization is done with an RPB, the rubblized concrete should be rolled with two to four passes of a smooth tandem vibrating roller Gninimum of 10 tons; 9,000 kilograms) for surface smoothing and further alignment of the fractured concrete pieces. The roller should be operated at the highest-frequency setting available at a speed that will produce at least 10 impacts per foot (33 impacts per meter).

When the rubblization is done with a multiple-head breaker, the rubblized concrete should be rolled with two passes of a vibratory steel-drum roller fit- ted with a special “Z” pattern grid on the drum face (see Figure 14.13). The grid roller should weigh a minimum of 14 tons (12,700 kilograms). This pro- vides a uniform surface and reduces the size of the surface pieces. The grid roller is followed by a 25-ton (22,680 kilograms) pneumatic-tire roller and often a tandem vibratory roller for final seating.

Remove and Patch Any Unstable Areas

During rubblization and rolling, any area found to be unstable must be removed and replaced. It is recommended that the section be replaced with a high-quality aggregate base or with HMA.

Place Asphalt HMA Leveling Course/HMA Overlays

Rubblized surfaces cannot be trimmed or fine graded like conventional granular surfaces. Larger pieces will be disturbed by fine-grading equip- ment, resulting in the loss of particle interlock and an overall reduction in the strength of the rubblized layer. Leveling courses may be necessary to accommodate grade and profile corrections.

Placement of the HMA overlay on a rubblized surface is accomplished according to the procedures discussed in Chapter 8, “Hot Mix Asphalt Paving.” Tack coats are not used because of the pickup of the rubblized surface by the construction equipment. Belly-dump trailers and windrow elevators for transferring the HMA into the hopper of the paving machine should not be used to place the first lift of HMA over rubblized PCC. It is suggested that the first lift be placed with a tracked paving machine. It is also recommended that traffic be kept off the surface until the entire HMA overlay has been placed.

14.4.4 Specification Criteria and Inspection

The rubblization process will result in smaller particles near the surface of the pavement; these particles will range from sand size to 3 inches (75 milli- meters) to 12-15 inches (300-380 millimeters) at the bottom of the rubblized layer on very thick PCC pavements. The actual size will vary depending on the slab thickness, the type of reinforcement, the subgrade strength, and the equipment used to rubblize. The specifications usually require that the con- tactor dig a test pit on the first day of production to verify the efficiency of the rubblization process. The specification requirements vary. Airfield proj- ects funded by the Federal Aviation Administration require at least 75 per- cent of the particles smaller than 3 inches (75 millimeters) at the surface and 12 inches (300 millimeters) in the bottom half. Other highway projects require all the particles to be less than 10 inches (250 millimeters) on the surface.

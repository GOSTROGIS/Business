Many quality-control specifications require aggregate-angularity testing.
This is because it is necessary to maintain or control the surface texture of
the aggregate particles to control the mixture volumetrics. Test procedures
for coarse aggregate angularity (CAA) or fractured faces can be found
in ASTM D 5821. Test procedures for fine aggregate angularity (FAA) or
uncompacted voids can be found in AASHTO T 304 or ASTM D 5821.

10.3.3 Asphalt Content

In the past, testing for asphalt content was done with chlorinated indus-
trial solvents such as trichloroethylene or methylene chloride to wash the
asphalt out of an HMA sample. This provided an asphalt cement content
and left the washed aggregate for gradation testing. Because of the high
cost of purchasing and disposal, and the human health risks to those han-
dling the solvents, the industry is increasingly turning to different methods
of testing asphalt content.

One method that has been developed is the nuclear asphalt content
gauge. These gauges have a radioactive source and backscatter detectors
that are sensitive to the presence of hydrogen ions. Asphalt binder con-
tains a significant number of hydrogen ions and therefore the gauges can
be calibrated to provide asphalt content measurements. These gauges are
currently used by some agencies as a replacement for solvent extractions.
They also have an added benefit for quality-control purposes in that they
provide results within minutes of sampling. In order to own and oper-
ate these gauges, however, users must have radiation safety licenses with
their respective regulatory agencies and the operators must be trained and
certified to use the equipment. Recent changes to licensing rules in the
United States have added additional security requirements to the already
stringent radiation safety rules.

Another recent development for testing asphalt content is the ignition
method. This method uses a large muffle furnace to heat the asphalt mix-
ture specimen to temperatures exceeding 1000°F (538°C); at these temper-
atures, the asphalt cement is completely burned up and all that remains
is a light ash. The ash can be easily washed from the remaining aggre-
gate and the aggregate tested for gradation. This method also requires
calibration because some of the rock particles can burn at the elevated
temperatures. Therefore, calibration factors for asphalt cement content
and for gradation must be applied. For most aggregates, the calibration
factors are small and manageable, but certain aggregates, especially those
containing dolomites and limestone, require special handling and modifi-
cations to the calibration and testing methods.

In today’s world, sized aggregates are readily available. Mixes can be
constructed with different percentages of sized aggregates at a ratio
blended right from the cold-feed bins. This is one reason why drum-mixers
came into being in the 1970s. It also eliminates some of the requirements
for a batch tower. In fact, it is not uncommon today to find five or six
different aggregates being blended at the cold bins to feed a batch plant.
With an overwhelming majority (over 95 percent) of today’s batch plants
having only four hot bins, ratio blending at the cold feed affects the hot-
bin gradations dramatically in a batch-plant operation.

Therefore, in modern batch-plant production, the blend formula is usu-
ally first established for the cold-feed bins to arrive at the target job-mix
formula just like a drum-mix plant, and then the hot bins are sampled for
their individual gradations and a new blend recipe/ratio is constructed
with these hot-bin gradations to re-arrive at the job-mix recipe. The ratios
of the individual hot bins become the recipe that is used to construct the
final mix with the batching plant.

If the HMA producer has good quality control on the cold-feed bins,
mixes can be proportioned completely with the cold feeds, eliminating the
need to screen the material in the batch plant. This is often referred to as
“screenless mixing.” Screenless mixing is done by opening a bypass chute,
allowing the heated aggregate stream to drop immediately into the No. 1
bin without passing over the screen deck. This requires drawing aggregate
from only one hot bin when weighing an individual batch, which speeds
up production. Bypassing the screen deck also eliminates wear and tear
on the screens and the screening mechanisms, which are both expensive
and time consuming to repair and maintain.

See Chapter 3, “Aggregates and Aggregate Additives,” to review the math-
ematical procedure for establishing these blend ratios. This procedure is
first used in the design process to establish the required percentages of
each material for the job-mix formula; it is then used against the individual
gradations of the cold-feed materials to reconfirm the blend ratios from
the cold-feed bins. It is used again with the individual hot-bin gradations
to establish the hot-bin percentages required to re-arrive at the target com-
posite gradation.

Once these individual hot-bin percentages are determined, establishing the
actual weights of each hot bin is relatively simple. The percentage for that
hot bin is multiplied by the total quantity of aggregate desired in the batch
and this becomes the target weight for that hot bin for that batch size.

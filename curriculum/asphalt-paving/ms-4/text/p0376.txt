The driving force behind this design innovation in the 1980s was a desire
to create a drum-mix facility that was more environmentally friendly by
introducing the new liquid-asphalt binder outside of the main gas stream
used to dry and heat the aggregate materials. This reduced the discharge
of very small (1-5 micron) hydrocarbon particles in the stack gases, which
sometimes created visible emissions or odors from the drum-mix facilities.
These new drum-mixers eliminate this problem.

Figures 7.23 through 7.26 show the different styles of drum-mixers
commonly found in the field at this time. It should be noted that many
batch-plant operators are now converting their plants from batch plants
to drum-mixers by replacing the tower with a mixing drum or continuous
pugmill mixer or by bypassing the tower and using a small mixing drum
mounted beside the tower, resulting in a hybrid system known as a com-
bination plant. This allows operators to use the same dryer and baghouse
to dry the material. As noted previously in this chapter, the cold-feed
bins were probably already converted to belt feeders with variable-speed
drives. By installing a continuous mixing device, a drum-mix liquid asphalt
pumping and metering system, and drum-mix controls, the batch-plant
operator now has all the advantages of drum-mix operation. Of primary
interest to producers is the ability to run at the production rate possible
with the dryer and not be limited by the capabilities of the screening unit,
being able to run higher percentages of RAP, and reducing maintenance
costs, thereby becoming more competitive in the marketplace. Having
consistently sized aggregates available makes this conversion possible.

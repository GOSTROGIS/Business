optimized within existing constraints, such as funding limitations. The most
common methods of optimizing pavement maintenance and rehabilitation
needs are described below:

° Ranking. A ranking formula is established giving higher weight to proj-
ects that reflect the agency’s priorities. For instance, higher volume roads
may have a higher priority than lower volume facilities.

° Benefit/Cost Analysis. Under this approach, the benefit associated with
each feasible treatment (usually defined in terms of additional perfor-
mance) is divided by the cost of the treatment to determine a benefit/cost
ratio. Projects are prioritized so that those with the highest benefit/cost
ratio are funded first.

To support the analysis, pavement performance models and treatment rules
must be established. Pavement performance models describe the deterioration
patterns of different types of pavements, using historical pavement condition
data when available. In addition, treatment rules must be defined to describe
the conditions under which each treatment is considered feasible and the
expected cost of the treatment. For instance, a treatment rule might be estab-
lished to apply an HMA overlay on roads in fair condition. These rules are
used to identify all feasible treatment options for existing and predicted con-
ditions during the analysis period. Finally, any budget constraints that limit
the number of projects that can be funded each year must be defined.

Reporting Tools

The results of a pavement management analysis, and the information
contained in the database, can be reported in a number of different formats,
including text, tables, and graphics. Many agencies link their pavement man-
agement databases to geographic information systems (GIS) or other map
packages to visually display the information. GIS displays of pavement
management information are very effective tools when reporting pavement
conditions to decision makers, elected officials, or the public.

Differences Based on Agency Size

Pavement management systems vary considerably in terms of the capa-
bilities they provide, the user-friendliness of the program, and the degree
to which the program can be customized to fit the particular needs of the
agency. Both public domain and proprietary software packages are avail-
able. In general, public domain programs cost less to install but have limited
capabilities and/or flexibility in meeting the needs of individual agencies. On
the other hand, proprietary software packages are typically more expensive
but also more sophisticated, with the flexibility needed to be tailored to meet
the needs of an individual agency. When selecting pavement management
software, an agency should first define its needs and then select a software
package that meets those needs. Otherwise, the agency may incur expenses
for features that are not needed and will not be used. For a pavement manage-
ment system to be useful to an agency, it should addresses the agency’s needs
and be maintained over time according to the available resources.

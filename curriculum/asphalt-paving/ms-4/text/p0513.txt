thing to investigate is the reliability of the sampling and testing for the
particular result. It is common to immediately take additional samples and
get retesting under way while the further investigation is ongoing. Other
related test results should also be evaluated to see what other parameters
have changed. Key test parameters related to the air voids would be asphalt
content, percent passing the No. 200 (0.075 millimeter) screen, aggregate
gradation (especially on screens between the No. 8 (2.36 millimeters) and
No. 40 (0.425 millimeter) sizes), and fine aggregate angularity. The investiga-
tion would also question whether any adjustments were made to other plant
processes such as percentages of aggregate stockpiles fed into the plant or
sources of asphalt cement. Any one of these things, or a combination of
some of them, might cause a change in air voids. An understanding of the
cause of the initial change in air voids is needed to make a good decision
about what adjustments would be appropriate.

Regardless of the actions taken, it is imperative that complete and accu-
rate records of the production and quality-control activities be kept while
the work is ongoing. This information and these records will be needed in
order to investigate difficult mixture issues as they come up. The investi-
gation can be rapid and accurate if proper records are kept, but it will be
long and drawn out if the quality-control manager and the plant operators
have to rely on the recollection of a variety of people to attempt to piece
together specific information about what happened.

Quality-control plans usually describe the documentation that will be
provided during the project. The use of computer spreadsheets, e-mail,
and the Internet mean these reports can contain large amounts of data,
and the information can be distributed to the appropriate parties in a
timely way. Because of the tremendous amount of data generated for
quality-control of HMA production, summaries of the daily average values
can be useful in analyzing and understanding how the materials are chang-
ing over time and the relative level of control that is being accomplished.

It is impossible to plot all the relationships in the quality-control data that
may become useful. Storage of quality-control data in spreadsheet applica-
tions can be helpful so that specific plots can be rapidly developed while
investigating noncompliant items. An example of this is shown in Figure
10.12. Consider a quality-control manager investigating high air voids with
the data presented in the quality-control summary (Figure 10.11). A plot of
air voids versus minus No. 200’s reveals a strong relationship between the
two parameters. As the minus No. 200’s decreased, the air voids decrease
as one would expect. The plot verifies that this is in fact what is happen-
ing and suggests that better control of the minus No. 200’s would affect
better control of the air voids. Further observation of the data reveals that
the mixture is essentially achieving the design air voids if the mix design
minus No. 200’s is achieved. In this example, changes in the job-mix for-
mula would not contribute to correcting the high air voids.

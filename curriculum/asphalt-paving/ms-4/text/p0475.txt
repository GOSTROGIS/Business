Boards of proper thickness should be placed at the edge of the pavement
to provide for off-the-pavement movement of the roller. The thickness of
the board is equal to how much the HMA will compact or roll down—
normally 20-25 percent. These “rolldown” factors will vary depending on
mix type. SMA/gap-graded mixtures and open-graded mixtures will roll
down less than dense-graded mixtures. If boards are not used, transverse
rolling must stop 6-8 inches (150-200 millimeters) short of the outside
edge in order to prevent damage to the edge. The outside edge then must
be rolled out later during longitudinal rolling.

If lane restrictions prohibit cross rolling, it is possible to roll straight onto
the new mat very slowly so that the mix will be pinched down into the joint
area instead of being pushed away from it. Other options include pinching
approximately 6 inches (150 millimeters) by transverse rolling, and then
rolling in straight. The goal is to make a tight, level joint flush with the
existing edge. This joint should be checked with a straightedge, typically
before, during, and after rolling to ensure that it meets specifications.

Longitudinal Joints

Just as there are options for rolling transverse joints, options for rolling
longitudinal joints also exist. The method chosen should be maintained to
ensure uniformity. Longitudinal joints should be rolled directly behind the
paving operation as illustrated in Figure 9.13.

Hot-side rolling. For lifts under 3 inches, it is best to roll from the hot
side and place most of the roller on the hot mat with only about 6 inches
(150 millimeters) of the drum overlapping on the adjacent cold mat. This
approach places the roller on the uncompacted mat, and the roller is both
compacting the mix and the joint at the same time. This is also the only
realistic way to roll a longitudinal joint when paving with traffic present in
the adjoining lane. This is generally more efficient than cold-side rolling.

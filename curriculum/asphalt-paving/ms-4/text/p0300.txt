The data recorded during the testing process include the number of
blows and the depth of penetration. The rate of penetration is defined
as the depth of penetration per blow, called the penetration index or the
DCP index—expressed as millimeter/blow or inch/blow. The penetration
rate is determined as the slope of the curve relating the number of blows
to the penetration depth. The device can be operated manually or can be
installed on a vehicle and automated. The automated DCP (see Figure 5.8)
has a fully developed software tool to determine the penetration index. In
either case, the equipment is economical, simple to use, and amenable to
many types of evaluations and materials.

Presentation of Data

The data is presented as a plot of the number of blows versus the depth
of penetration. The penetration rate has been correlated to engineering
properties of soils or other derived measurements of strength and modu-
lus. Specifically, studies have correlated CBR to the penetration rate for a
wide variety of cohesive and granular materials. The penetration rate can be
correlated to resilient modulus through correlations between CBR and resil-
ient modulus; however, such secondary correlations should be used with
caution. Several studies have also correlated penetration rate, or index, to
the elastic modulus of soils.

Falling Weight Deflectometer

General

The FWD, as shown on Figure 5.9, is an impulse load-deflection device
that delivers a transient impulse force to the pavement. A weight is lifted
to a given height on a guide system and then dropped onto a buffering
plate on the pavement. A transient impulse force, which can be changed by
varying the magnitude of the falling weight or by varying the drop height,
is generated in the pavement by the impact of the falling weight. A series of
sensors located at various distances from the load plate are used to measure
pavement deflections. Commercial impulse load-deflection devices include
the Dynatest, KUAB, JILS, and Phoenix.

A major advantage of the impulse loading devices is their ability to more
accurately model a moving wheel load in both magnitude and duration,
thus producing a deflection that simulates the pavement deflection caused
by a moving vehicle. The advantages of the FWD are the following:

¢ The ability to better simulate a moving wheel load

© The ability to measure deflections at varying loads over time

® The ability to record a deflection basin

° The speed, repeatability, and robustness of the equipment

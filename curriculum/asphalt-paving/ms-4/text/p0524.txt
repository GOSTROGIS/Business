Lifts that exceed the minimum lift thicknesses are especially important
with very coarse-graded Superpave mixtures. See the Asphalt Institute’s
SP-2, Superpave Mix Design for an explanation of aggregate gradations
and the restricted zone. These coarse-graded Superpave mixtures require
thicker lifts than fine-graded mixtures, and they can tend to require more
compaction energy than finer mixtures. In order to control and achieve
required compaction, proper lift thicknesses must be maintained.

It is also important to realize that adjustments to compaction opera-
tions are required any time the lift thickness changes. New lift thicknesses
necessitate revisions to nuclear density calibrations and rolling patterns,
as well as reconsideration of timing and temperature.

10.6.4 Thickness and Spread Rates

Quality-control testing for pavement thickness is accomplished using
depth gauges (Figure 10.13) that are pushed into the HMA after the paver
has placed the hot mix but before the compaction equipment has rolled it
down. For most dense-graded mixes, a rule of thumb is that the thickness of
the uncompacted hot mix should be 1.25 times the final thickness. The actual
decrease in thickness could be more accurately determined for a given mix
design using core thicknesses measured and compared with the initial depth
gauge thicknesses.

Thickness is also measured by cutting cores from the completed pave-
ment surface. Core thicknesses are considered the most accurate method,
but because they are taken after the work is complete, they do not lend
themselves well to quality-control purposes. These values could be used
to adjust thicknesses for subsequent paving days, but the paving equip-
ment is usually controlled with thickness readings behind the paver with
a depth gauge.

In measuring and adjusting lift thicknesses, it is important to consider that
paver screeds are self-leveling. They are designed to fill in the low spots and cut
down the high spots, making it impossible to lay a uniform lift thickness on an
uneven surface. If the depth checks were exactly equal all of the time, the new
surface would have the same rough profile as the underlying pavement or base.
In reality, many quality-control “depth checks” will be made behind the paver,
but these checks should lead to very few adjustments to the paving machine.
Consideration of this natural variation in lift thickness, especially for overlays
on rough pavements, is necessary for evaluating core thicknesses for accep-
tance purposes as well.

Some agencies specify spread rates (yield) in lieu of thickness. Spread rates
can be checked as a quality-control measure by calculating the weight of mate-
rial placed over a measured area. If measurement of spread rates is required as
part of the quality-control plan, a technician can keep a log of beginning and
ending stations for each truck delivered, along with the weight of each load.
On this log, running values of spread rates can be calculated and reported to
the paving superintendent on a periodic basis throughout the day.

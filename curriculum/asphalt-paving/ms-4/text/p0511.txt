Significant equipment and material differences exist between the small-scale
operation of the laboratory mixing bowl and an asphalt mixing facility, which
could lead to the mixture property changes noted. Many causes for volumetric
changes in field-produced mixture have been proposed:

¢ Moisture removed more effectively in the laboratory than in the field

° An excessive amount of baghouse fines returned to the mixing process

° Highly absorptive aggregates used. (Field absorption may differ from

laboratory absorption.)

* Poor sampling used to obtain aggregate samples for the laboratory

design

* Nonstandard mix design procedures used to develop the JMF

° Material sources (location and/or strata) changed between the mix

design and field production

° Aggregate degradation and shape changes during plant mixing

* Non-uniform stockpiling of aggregates

The changes that occur in the field-produced mixture are likely due to
a combination of some or all of the listed causes in any particular case.
Attention to detail in material production and mix design development can
reduce the potential effects of many of the factors shown.

Some form of verification that the proposed job-mix formula will produce
materials meeting the project specifications will occur on every project.
Some agencies require that verifications be performed by the agency labora-
tory or by a laboratory different from the one that did the mix design. Other
agencies will utilize test strips to validate the job-mix formula. Additional
discussion of test strips can be found in Section 10.4.4. Some agencies allow
the contractor to make minor changes in the job-mix formula without a new
mix design if the changes are made to improve the volumetric properties
of the mix. Improvements to volumetric properties in such cases generally
mean getting the laboratory air voids closer to the target value.

If there are no specified means to verify the job-mix formula, then it
will be done during the initial paving. This creates a higher risk that non-
complying or marginal HMA will be produced for a longer period of time
before adjustments are made.

10.5.2 Mixture Control

Quality-control test data must be quickly reported and analyzed so that
it can be used to improve the consistency and overall quality of the HMA
product. The goal is to understand the data and to evaluate whether any
adjustments to the plant process or materials processes are warranted. It
is necessary to keep thorough records so the quality-control manager can
understand the overall production and observe trends that may need fur-
ther action. Good mixture control is accomplished by first organizing the
data in a meaningful way. Control charts are often used for this. Then the
data must be analyzed and understood. Finally, appropriate adjustments to
the production operations need to be made.

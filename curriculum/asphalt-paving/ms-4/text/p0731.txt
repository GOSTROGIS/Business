The concept for resonant rubblization was developed by Ray Gurries as
a means to facilitate the removal of reinforced PCC slabs. High-frequency
(42-46 Hz), low-amplitude (less than 1 inch; 25 millimeters) blows are
generated by an oscillating beam and applied through a steel-breaking shoe.
Diagonal cracks are induced through the entire thickness of the slab, while
the PCC near the surface is crushed, resulting in smaller particles near the
surface. The broken concrete is debonded from any reinforcing steel and
remains tightly interlocked.

The RPB rubblizes the PCC pavement in narrow strips as the machine
moves forward adjacent to the edge of a previously fractured pavement.
It continues with successive passes until it has moved transversely across
the width of the pavement. The forward speed of the machine depends
on the size of the machine, slab thickness, stiffness of the PCC, and the
support provided by the subgrade. The machine is operated at a forward
speed of less than 5 miles per hour (8 kilometers per hour). One machine
can be expected to rubblize 6,000-8,000 square yards (5000-7000 square
meters) per workday shift, with thick airfield pavement work being on the
low end of this range. It is not unusual for multiple machines to be employed
to expedite the process on major interstate projects.

As an RPB fractures an existing PCC pavement, a small amount of dila-
tion takes place. For extremely wide pavements, such as airport runways
and taxiways, relief trenches sometimes have to be cut full depth through
the pavement at fixed intervals to allow for horizontal expansion and to
reduce the confining pressure at interior locations on these pavements. This
is usually not necessary for highway pavements. To remain intact, any exist-
ing PCC pavement must be isolated by using a full-depth sawcut to avoid
damage. No damage has been observed from operating a resonant breaker
adjacent to new HMA placed during shoulder or edge-drain installation.

Multiple-Head Pavement Breaker

The multiple-head pavement breaker is a rubber-tire, self-contained, self-
propelled unit (see Figure 14.12) that breaks the PCC using a series of drop
hammers. It consists of six pairs of hammers—each weighing 1,200 pounds
(544 kilograms) measuring 8 inches (20 centimeters) in width—mounted
laterally in pairs, with half the hammers in a forward row and the remainder
diagonally offset in a rear row so the breakage is continuous from side to
side. Wing units, carrying two 1,500-pound (680 kilograms) hammers, can
be added to each side for a total breaking width of up to 13 feet (4 meters).
In the 16-hammer configuration, the MHB weighs 57,000 pounds (25,855
kilograms). Each pair of hammers is attached to a separate hydraulic lift
cylinder with an adjustable drop height from 60 inches (0-1.5 meters). Each
pair operates as an independent unit, and develops 1,000-8,000 foot-pounds
of energy, depending on the drop height selected, and cycles at a rate of
30-35 impacts per minute.

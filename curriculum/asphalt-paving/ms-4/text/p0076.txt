Advantages and Shortcomings of Each of the Systems

While the Viscosity-Graded Specification represented an improvement
over the Penetration-Graded Specification, it also had some of the same
limitations. Viscosity measurements at 275°F (135°C) allowed the user to
determine the stiffness of the asphalt binder at typical mixing temperatures
in an HMA mixing facility. Viscosity measurements at 140°F (60°C) allowed
the user to determine the stiffness of the asphalt binder at typical high
pavement temperatures. Despite the addition of intermediate temperature
measurements (such as penetration) to some specifications, the stiffness
of the asphalt binder at cold temperatures could only be inferred from the
intermediate temperature data. As in Figure 2.14, Figure 2.15 illustrates
that the actual performance could be significantly different for asphalt
binders with the same grade.

As with the Penetration-Graded Specification, short-term aging effects
were captured through the use of the TFOT or RTFOT, but there was no
provision to conduct long-term aging of asphalt binders to simulate what
happens to the asphalt stiffness after being in a pavement for a number
of years.

Finally, although viscosity is a fundamental property, the viscosity test
is only useful in measuring the viscous component of an asphalt binder.
This is not an issue for conventional, refined asphalt binders but becomes
a greater issue when using asphalt binders produced using polymer
modification. For these asphalt binders, the elastic component of the
asphalt binder really is not properly considered when using the viscosity
test. As such, the viscosity of a modified asphalt binder may not provide
sufficient information about the asphalt binder’s physical properties to
properly judge its performance potential.

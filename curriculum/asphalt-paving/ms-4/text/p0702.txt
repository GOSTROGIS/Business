characteristics of the binder are influenced by these factors:

e Temperature of the asphalt binder—typically about 320°F (160°C);

e Amount of water added to the asphalt binder—typically 2 percent

° Pressure used to inject the asphalt binder into the expansion

° Presence of antifoaming agents, such as silicone additives, in the
asphalt binder. These materials should not be added to an asphalt
binder that will be used as a foamed asphalt.

The selection of the asphalt binder for a project is based on the in-
service environmental characteristics of the pavement being built as
recommended in Chapter 2 “Petroleum Asphalt.”

The first step in the mix design process is to optimize the foaming
characteristics of the foamed asphalt. The Iowa DOT has developed an
excellent procedure for designing foamed asphalt mixes. It is described in
a research report, “Development of a Mix Design Process for Cold In-place
Rehabilitation using Foamed Asphalt.” They use a small laboratory asphalt-
foaming plant (see Figure 13.25) to produce the foamed asphalt. This
equipment simulates the foamed asphalt to be used during construction.
The expansion and half-life characteristics of the foamed asphalt binder
are determined for at least three asphalt binder temperatures and a range
of water contents of 1-5 percent by weight of asphalt binder. The com-
bination of the asphalt binder temperature and water content that maxi-
mizes the expansion ratio for the longest half-life is selected for use.

The optimum moisture content for the reclaimed asphalt pavement/base
material is determined using standard procedures (modified Proctor) for
determining the optimum density and moisture content for an unstabilized
material.

To determine the optimum asphalt-binder content, samples of the reclaimed
material are oven dried and cooled to room temperature and then rewetted
to 90 percent of the optimum moisture content. A range of foamed asphalt
contents is added to the reclaimed material, mixed thoroughly at room tem-
perature, and compacted using either 50 or 75 blows with a Marshall hammer.
After compaction, the samples are extruded from the molds. After extrusion,
they are further cured for 3 days in an oven at 100°F (40°C). The density of the
specimens is determined by measuring the bulk specific gravity. The samples
should be handled very carefully after compaction. Because of the fragility
of the samples, the bulk specific gravity should be determined using the
CoreLok procedure discussed in Chapter 4 “Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) Design.”
As described in Chapter 4, the samples are tested to determine the Marshall
stability and the indirect tensile strength of the samples.

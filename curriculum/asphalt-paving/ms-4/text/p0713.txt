14.1 Introduction

Many miles of Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavement are
rehabilitated each year by placing an asphalt overlay on them. According
to the FHWA’s “Highway Statistics 2005,” there were more than 40,000
more miles of composite pavements than rigid (PCC surface) pavements
in the United States. Chapter 6, “Thickness Design” discusses the thickness
design procedures for those overlays. This chapter discusses techniques
used to either manage the reflection cracking in those overlays or elimi-
nate the occurrence of reflection cracking.

14.1.1 Reflection Cracking

Reflection cracking is the result of horizontal and vertical movements
at the joints and cracks in the underlying pavement, resulting in a pattern
of cracking in the surface that reflects the joints or cracks in the underly-
ing PCC pavement. Temperature changes in the pavement cause the slabs
to contract and expand. This movement is most pronounced at joints and
cracks, which open and close in response to the volume changes in the
PCC slabs. Opening of joints and cracks due to cooling causes tensile
stress directly over these locations in the HMA overlay (see Figure 14.1).
Reflection cracking may occur after an extreme cold temperature event or
after repeated opening and closing.

Traffic loading may produce differential vertical movement across the
joint or crack, particularly when load transfer is inefficient. This causes
shear and bending stress in the overlay. As the wheel (see Figure 14.2)
approaches, the shear stress will reach a maximum at point A, and as it
passes over the joint, it will reverse at point C. As the wheel load passes
over the joint at point B, the bending stress will reach a maximum.

The key to delaying or avoiding reflection cracking is to reduce or
eliminate the stresses and deformations produced in the overlay. Some
treatments include the placement of an interlayer to reduce or dampen
the movement at the joint or crack caused by thermal contraction of the
PCC. Some of the techniques are discussed in Chapter 13, “Pavement
Rehabilitation Methods for HMA Pavements,” including the use of geo-
textiles, asphalt-rubber “stress-absorbing membrane interlayers” (SAMIs),
etc. Single- and multiple-surface treatments have also been used as an
interlayer for rehabilitation of HMA- and PCC-surfaced pavements, as have
specialty asphalt paving mixtures that are extremely ductile and resistant
to cracking. Another widely used approach is to control reflection crack-
ing by sawing and sealing the overlay directly over the joints. This will be
discussed in more detail later in this chapter.

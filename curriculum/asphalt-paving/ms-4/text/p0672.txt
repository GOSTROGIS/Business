Milling is conducted using a cutting drum, which is available in multiple
widths. The cutting drum uses replaceable tungsten carbide teeth to cut
the pavement surface (see Figure 13.9). These drums generally operate in
the “upcutting” direction to facilitate breaking and removal of the existing
material. The teeth are oriented in a helical or a spiral pattern to move the
milled material to the center of the cutting drum, where it is forced onto a
loadout conveyor for removal. The service life of the tungsten teeth depends
on the nature and the depth of the material being milled. A small amount
of water is used to control the amount of dust created by the milling process
and to cool the cutting teeth to extend their life. The size of the reclaimed
material can be adjusted by changing the rotational speed and/or reducing
the forward speed of the milling machine.

Micromilling (also called fine milling) is a variation of the standard
milling procedure. The micromilling process uses more cutting teeth to pro-
duce a much finer textured surface (see Figure 13.10). A conventional milling
machine has teeth spaced % inch (15 millimeters) - 3% inch (20 millimeters)
apart. The teeth on a micromilling drum are typically spaced about % inch
(8 millimeters) apart. This results in almost double the number of teeth for
micromilling versus conventional milling. It is also possible to have the
teeth spaced even closer at about 4 inch for micromilling, but this is not
very common. Micromilling can provide a very fine, uniform texture that
can serve as the final riding surface with or without an overlay. It can
also be used to correct some minor pavement distress or to remove pave-
ment markings because of changing traffic patterns. With micromilling,

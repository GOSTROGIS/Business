The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) developed its “Distress
Identification Manual for the Long-Term Pavement Performance Pro-
gram” to support the data collection efforts associated with Long-Term
Pavement Performance (LTPP) studies initiated in 1987. This manual has
been through several iterations, with the current version issued in 2003
(FHWA 2003). Designed as a project-level inspection procedure, the man-
ual identifies distress types and severity levels for three pavement surfaces:
asphalt concrete surfaces, Portland cement concrete surfaces, and con-
tinuously reinforced concrete surfaces. The manual serves as an excellent
resource for project-level and research surveys; however, an approach for
calculating a distress index from the distress data has never been devel-
oped. Without an index calculation, the procedure has limited application
for network-level pavement condition surveys.

The Asphalt Institute’s Manual, MS-17, includes condition evaluation
forms for flexible and rigid pavements used by the Ontario Ministry of
Transportation (after some editing for consistency with Asphalt Institute
terminology). These forms illustrate the level of detail often associated
with a pavement distress survey.

Characteristics of an Effective Survey
An effective pavement distress survey should have the following
characteristics:
° There should be little interpretation expected of the raters in the field.
° The methodology should be objective and repeatable.
° It should minimize incursions with traffic.
° It should provide a sufficient level of detail so the agency can estimate
maintenance and rehabilitation needs accurately.
It should include a method of estimating distress quantities.
It should differentiate distress severity levels in situations where distress
severity changes dramatically over a relatively short period of time.
° The importance of the distress in determining the appropriate repair
option should be factored into the calculation of the pavement condition
index.

11.3 Pavement Distress Surveys

Whether part of a formal pavement management system or a project-level
pavement evaluation to select an appropriate maintenance or rehabilitation
treatment, pavement distress surveys provide information on the type and the
extent of deterioration present and the primary mechanisms causing the dis-
tress. These considerations are both very important in selecting and designing
an improvement. Pavement distress-surveys may be conducted in conjunction
with other testing, such as destructive or nondestructive testing.

11.3.1 Distress Survey Approaches

The previous section introduced methods of conducting both functional
and structural pavement evaluations. Pavement distress surveys are the
most common type of functional pavement evaluation used by highway
and public works agencies. As part of a network-level evaluation, a dis-
tress survey may be conducted on selected samples to provide an overall
condition for each pavement section in the network. As part of a project-
level investigation in which a rehabilitation treatment is being designed,
more detailed distress surveys are typically conducted. At the project level,
distress maps showing distress location, severity, and extent are often
created. Distress maps are time-consuming to prepare but provide an
excellent way of estimating repair quantities or marking the location of
drainage or geometric features. An example of a distress map is shown in
Figure 11.8.

Depending on the types of distress survey being conducted, the following
types of equipment may be required:

° A distress identification manual

° A map or utility layout sheet showing section limits

¢ A measuring wheel

¢ Survey forms and/or mapping sheets

° A clipboard

° Pencils

° A straightedge or string line

° Spray paint (to mark limits and section identifiers)

° A hand calculator

¢ A camera or other recording device

Pavement distress surveys are typically conducted using crews of at
least two people so that while one individual measures distress quantities,
the other serves as a recorder. Having two crew members is also important
for safety reasons because survey crews often have to mingle with traffic.

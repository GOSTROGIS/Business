gradations for quality-control purposes. Consider, for example, gradation
testing performed for quality-control after crushing. If the materials are
crushed in a dry state, and they are separated into stockpiles containing
certain gravel sizes, it may be appropriate to test these gravels without
washing. A visual observation can verify that little dust remains adhered
to the aggregate particles, that the gradation will not likely be affected by
more than one percent, and that the quality-control manager can estimate
that the percentage passing the No. 200 screen would be slightly higher.
Quality-control testing used to evaluate a washing operation’s effective-
ness at removing fines, on the other hand, may require washed gradations.
It is important to remember that this is not the acceptance point. The
aggregates still have to be blended and further processed before being
incorporated into the HMA mixture, and the quality-control manager must
also estimate aggregate degradation during this processing. One can see
that the added accuracy gained by washing gravel materials may not bene-
fit controlling the crushing operation. Using non-washed gradations could
actually provide more benefit because it gives more timely results and
might allow the technician to provide a higher test frequency.

Test procedures for aggregate gradation can be found in AASHTO T 27
and T 11 or ASTM C 117 and C 136.

10.3.2 Fractured Faces and Aggregate
Angularity

Aggregate angularity has long been known to affect the void proper-
ties and compactability of HMA mixtures. Traditionally, aggregate angu-
larity is tested by physically separating the aggregate particles in a given
test sample containing fractured surfaces from those containing no frac-
tured surfaces. Then the two masses are weighed and the percentage of
the sample containing fractures is determined as a percentage by weight.
Typical ranges of fractured-faces specifications are between 75 percent
and 100 percent. Some specifications require a certain percentage of
particles with two or more fractures. The fracture faces test can be per-
formed on coarse and fine aggregate, but the fine aggregate testing can
be very tedious and is prone to technician error. There is also a practical
limit to the smallest particle sizes that can be evaluated.

The difficulty of testing the fractured faces of fine aggregates, combined
with the knowledge that the fine-aggregate texture affects the volumetrics
of mixtures more than the coarse aggregate texture, led to the develop-
ment of the uncompacted voids test. In this test, the fine aggregate is
poured through a standard funnel into a standard cup. The cup is gently
struck off and the weight recorded. One can imagine that a highly angular
aggregate would bulk up in the cup and render a lighter weight, whereas
a rounded aggregate would pack into the cup and render a heavier weight.

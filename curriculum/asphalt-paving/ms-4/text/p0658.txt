Performance
A number of factors can lead to diminished asphalt overlay performance,
including the following:
¢ Insufficient overlay thickness
° The use of the wrong type of HMA surface for the overlay
° Insufficient preparation of the existing pavement before the overlay
° Insufficient consideration of the potential for reflection cracking

Overlay Thickness

The overlay thickness must be designed for the anticipated traffic
over the design period. It should be designed using the principles and
procedures discussed in Chapter 6, “Thickness Design.” The selection of
the proper material for the overlay is discussed in Chapter 4, “Hot Mix
Asphalt CHMA) Design.”

Pre-overlay Preparation

Overlay performance is influenced by the amount, type, and severity of
distress in the existing pavement. Extensive repair of the existing pavement
will maximize the performance of the overlay. For example, replacing all
areas that have medium-to-severe alligator cracking and improving all
areas with poor drainage on a project will significantly increase the life
of a pavement rehabilitation project. This is one approach to pre-overlay
preparation. The other approach is to place an overlay of sufficient thick-
ness to protect all the weak areas in the existing pavement structure. If
an existing base or subbase layer has deteriorated, the designer increases
the thickness of the overlay to account for the decreased strength of the
underlying layers, instead of removing that layer. As the overlay thickness
is increased, the applied loads are distributed over a larger area in the
lower layers, thereby decreasing the stresses and deflections on the weak
layer. The thickness required to adequately protect weak layers usually
makes this approach economically unfeasible. Figure 13.1 shows the total
repair cost and the total overlay cost plotted against the percentage of
area repaired. When the percentage of the total area repaired increases,
the cost of the overlay will decrease. But when these two costs are added
together, the total cost of the project reaches a point at which it is mini-
mized. When the total cost begins to increase relative to the percentage
of the area repaired, the designer should consider reconstructing or using
full-depth reclamation as more cost-effective design alternatives.

4 The Drum-Mix
Production Process

7.4.1 The Development of the
Drum-Mix Process

Drum-mixers started appearing during the early 1970s. The first drum-
mixers used elongated drying drums not only to dry and heat the aggregate
but also to mix the liquid asphalt with the heated aggregate to produce the
final hot mix all in the same drying cylinder.

These first drums were parallel-flow dryers, meaning that the aggre-
gate and the process gas flowed in the same direction. The wet, ambient
aggregates were introduced to the end of the dryer where the burner was
located, and the aggregate was dried as it moved away from the burner.
In this fashion, the liquid-asphalt binder could be introduced into the exit
end/cooler part of the drum.

This process required less capital expenditure for initial equipment,
greater mobility for relocation to remote projects, less installation time,
less maintenance, and higher RAP capability.

This process could not have been developed without the following:

> The availability of uniformly produced, consistently sized aggregate

° The development of large, inexpensive variable-speed motors that

can be fitted to the cold-feed bins to easily proportion aggregates to
the desired final mix gradations

e The development of belt scales and flow meters for measuring the

flow of materials continuously

° The development of finished mix storage silos to elevate the material

so it can be dispensed into trucks

Each equipment development occurred independently and it became
obvious that the need for sizing and fractioning material in a batch plant
was no longer necessary.

Drum-mix plants were lower to the ground, easier to move, and less
expensive to buy and maintain than batch facilities. As a result, they
became the process of choice for hot mix asphalt producers starting in the
late 1970s, and today over 80 percent of the mix produced in the United
States is with drum-mix plants.

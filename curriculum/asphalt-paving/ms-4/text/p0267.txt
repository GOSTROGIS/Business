At any time during the test, the applied stress can be divided by the
observed strain to arrive at a creep modulus or stiffness. Because the
strain changes with time, so does the creep modulus. In creep testing,
many engineers prefer to compute creep compliance rather than creep
stiffness. Creep compliance can be portrayed as the inverse of creep stiff-
ness (although it is not strictly correct, theoretically). In other words, creep
compliance is the strain at any time during the test per unit of stress. At
the conclusion of a creep test, the test specimen will partially rebound to
its original shape. For many materials, however, some permanent deforma-
tion remains in the test specimen.

The rate of change of creep compliance versus loading time is then
calculated and plotted as shown in Figure 4.31. The minimum point of the
curve occurs where the rate of creep compliance changes as a function of
time is zero. This point is identified as the flow time and is the time when
shear deformation occurs under constant volume. Higher values of flow
time (FT) from the axial static creep test are thought to be related to better
rutting resistance in the HMA mixture.

Dynamic Creep Test

loading cycles and measuring the resulting axial strain. The concepts of
repeated load axial (dynamic creep) testing are similar to the repeated shear
test at constant height. A specified axial stress (usually 69-207 kilopascal) is
applied to a test specimen for 0.1 seconds and released to a rest period of

15.1 Cold Mix Asphalt

15.1.1 Introduction

Cold mix asphalt preparation is not as complicated as that described
for hot mixes in Chapter 7. Generally, placing and compacting does not
usually call for equipment as sophisticated as that required for hot mix
asphalt. However, this type of pavement construction is no less important,
because a large percentage of the nation’s roadways have cold mix asphalt
bases and even surfaces.

A less-controlled, simplified process is an advantage during in situ cold mix
preparation with a distributor and a motor-grader operation. But the same
quality control as that found in hot mix asphalt preparation is possible when
a cold mix is prepared in a plant-mix operation. However, many plants used
for producing cold mixes do not use, or have, all the means for maintaining
close uniformity of control. Wherever suitable materials are available, such
plant-mixing procedures are employed for high production and low cost.

Cold mix recycling or reworking of existing asphalt pavement or aggre-
gate structures is another important pavement construction alternative.
Cold mix recycling is the process by which reclaimed asphalt pavement
(RAP), reclaimed aggregate materials (RAM), or both are combined with
new asphalt in place or at a central mixing facility to produce cold mix
base mixtures. For more complete information on cold mix recycling, see
Chapter 13, “Rehabilitation Methods for Asphalt Pavements.”

15.1.2 Description

Cold-placed asphalt pavement mixtures (cold mixes) are generally made
with emulsified or cutback asphalt. Emulsified asphalt may be anionic
or cationic MS and SS grades. Cutback asphalt may be MC or SC grades.
Special additives to the emulsions add a High Float (HF) designation to
the grading system and will slow the breaking of the emulsion when
the reclaimed material has a high percentage of fine material or minus
No. 200 (75 microns) sieve. The Asphalt Institute’s MS-19, Asphalt Emulsion
Manual should be consulted for more information.

The aggregate material may be anything from a dense-graded crushed
aggregate to a granular soil having a relatively high percentage of dust.
The aggregate at the time of mixing might be damp, air-dry, or artificially
heated and dried.

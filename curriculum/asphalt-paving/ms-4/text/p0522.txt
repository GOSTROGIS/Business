percent compaction. Next, the roller operator makes another coverage, this
time consisting of a static pass and a vibratory pass. After that coverage, the
technician places the gauge back in the exact location and takes a second
reading. This time the technician records a compaction value of 91.5 percent.
At this point, the paving superintendent must decide whether to go to the
next phase of compaction or to attempt to gain additional density with the
breakdown roller. Assume the superintendent decides to make another pass
with the breakdown roller, and the next density result is 92.0 percent.

Now, the breakdown roller moves on and the intermediate roller continues
the compaction process. During intermediate rolling, it may not be necessary
to test after each coverage, but a couple of readings to show progression
of density should be made. In this example, assume that after three passes
with the intermediate roller, the compaction reading is 93.0 percent and the
superintendent decides to move on to the finish roller.

The finish roller will work to smooth the surface of the pavement and the
joints but will not necessarily gain additional compaction. This depends on
a number of factors such as the temperature of the mat and the compaction
achieved before finish rolling. See Chapter 9, “Hot Mix Asphalt Compaction”
for more information on compaction methods. For this example, assume that
after two coverages with the static roller, the nuclear density gauge reads
93.5 percent, then after another coverage, there is no gain in density. At this
point, depending on variables such as temperature of the mat, additional
compaction from the finish roller is not likely, and the finish roller moves on
to the next portion of pavement. There is now a rolling pattern and measured
compaction results for that pattern. The same rolling pattern is expected to
give similar density results if used again under the same conditions.

The paving superintendent now has one rolling pattern and a result of
93.5 percent on a project that specifies a minimum of 93 percent. A rolling
pattern achieving only 0.5 percent above the minimum is probably not very
comforting to a superintendent who will have many daily variations because
of weather, mix temperature, mix properties, subbase preparation, and so
on. Perhaps the superintendent will try another rolling pattern on a new
portion of the pavement and use different sequencing of compaction. A new
pattern may include a different number of passes, a different amplitude on
vibratory compactors, a different tire pressure, a ballast weight change on
pneumatic rollers, heavier compactors, insertion of another vibratory roller
into the intermediate compaction process, and so on. Numerous options are
available for compaction rolling patterns.

that nuclear density gauges can have a large bias and wide variation from the
actual density of the pavement. Good quality-control plans improve the accu-
racy of these instruments by calibrating the gauge with cores from the project.

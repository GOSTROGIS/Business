igneous Rock

Igneous rocks are formed by the cooling and solidification of hot molten
materials (magma) that erupt from or are trapped beneath the earth’s crust.
These rocks are further classified as extrusive or intrusive rocks. Extrusive
rocks are formed by material that has poured out on the earth’s surface,
such as that produced by volcanic action, and are distinguished in general
by their glass-like structure (rhyolite, andesite, basalt, etc.). Intrusive rocks
result from cooling and solidification at great depths within the earth’s crust.
They are wholly crystalline in texture (granite, diorite, gabbro, etc.). Although
originally formed beneath the earth’s surface, they are often exposed today
as a result of earth movements and erosion processes.

Metamorphic Rocks

Metamorphic rocks are generally formed by the modification of sedimentary
or igneous rocks because of intense pressures caused by movement within
the earth’s crust and by excessive heat and solutions. Factors causing such
modifications are complex, and the original form of the altered rock is often
difficult to determine. One distinguishing feature of metamorphic rocks is the
parallel planes in which the minerals that form the rocks are arranged. This
arrangement is called foliation. These planes are zones of weakness along
which the rocks may be split more easily than in other directions. Examples
of foliated rocks are gneisses and schists (altered from igneous material),
and slate (altered from shale). Marble (altered from limestone) and quartzite
(altered from sandstone) are common types of metamorphic rocks having a
massive texture, usually without foliation.

Pit or Bank-Run Materials

Gravel and sand are natural aggregates existing in loose or unconsoli-
dated alluvial deposits and are typically pit or bank-run material extracted
without drilling and blasting. Gravel is usually defined as aggregate par-
ticles ranging in size from 3 inches (75 millimeters) to No. 4 (4.75 millime-
ters). Sand consists of particles ranging in size from No. 4 (4.75 millimeters)
to No. 200 (75 microns). Sizes No. 200 and smaller include the silt fraction.
Many pit or bank-run alluvial or glacial sources also contain cobbles with
aggregate particles as large as 10 inches (254 millimeters) or more.

Exposed rocks are eroded and degraded by many processes of nature,
both physical and chemical. The products of the degradation processes
are usually moved by wind, water, or moving ice, and deposited as a soil
mineral in various landforms.

presents the following equation to calculate the pay factor:

Pay factor (%) = 55 + 0.5 X PWL

This pay factor would then be multiplied times the contractor’s payment for
that particular bid item. Assume that the project specifications establishes that
payment for HMA is by the ton completed and in place. For all of the tonnage
represented by that lot, the price per ton would be paid at the bid price times
the pay factor. This may cause the payment to increase or decrease depend-
ing on the contractor’s accomplishment of the quality characteristics that were
defined. Some agencies pay bonuses if the pay factor exceeds 100 percent.
Other agencies restrict the total payment to 100 percent of the bid price.
When such restrictions are in place, specifications can be written to allow
positive pay factors to offset negative pay factors from previous paving lots.

In the pay factor equation above, the number “55” could be changed
by the specifying agency. A closer look at the equation reveals that, if
55 were used, the highest pay factor that could be achieved would be
105 percent. This means a contractor could receive a 5 percent bonus if every
lot achieved 100 percent within limits (PWL). Of course, if a PWL less than
90 percent were achieved on any given lot, the pay factor would be less than
100 percent, which would reduce the contractor’s payment for that lot.

One can see that the agency can control the possible bonus by changing
this pay factor variable. If the variable were set at 60, for example, the pos-
sible bonus would be 10 percent. A variable of 50 would mean that nothing
greater than 100 percent pay could be achieved, but payment reductions
would be assessed for any PWL less than 100 percent. Slight changes to this
pay factor variable have severe financial impacts on the incentive or disin-
centive being offered to prospective bidders. The agency goal is to select a
pay factor variable that will motivate contractors to achieve higher quality
standards represented by high PWLs. In the end, this will cause the agency
to pay a little more for a roadway that will last a lot longer.

Many specifications consider the fact that when mixtures are out of speci-
fication tolerances, they usually incur penalties based on more than one of
the test requirements. Multiple non-compliances in one lot might unrea-
sonably compound the penalties, or equal monetary consideration might
be given to test parameters that do not hold equal value in performance.

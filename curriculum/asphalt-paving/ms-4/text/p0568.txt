11.2.0 Structural Characteristics and
Evaluation

A pavement structural evaluation is used to estimate the structural
adequacy of an existing pavement to determine whether the pave-
ment can sufficiently meet projected traffic loadings. In situations when
the pavement is not adequate to meet projected loadings, the results of
the structural evaluation can provide the information needed to design the
appropriate treatment.

Because of the level of detail normally associated with this type of eval-
uation, structural evaluations are typically conducted as part of a project-
level investigation. This may include more extensive pavement condition
surveys conducted in conjunction with destructive testing (such as coring
and boring) and with nondestructive testing using pavement deflection
devices. Pavement deflection testing does not disturb the underlying pave-
ment structure and does not require pavement materials to be removed for
laboratory testing. However, it does require accurate pavement thickness
information at each test location so coring is often conducted to verify
pavement layer thicknesses.

Pavement Deflection Testing

Pavement deflection testing measures the response of the pavement to
a load in terms of the resultant pavement deflection. The deflection data
are used to backcalculate in situ material properties, to calculate pavement
responses to load, and as inputs to other pavement analyses. By plot-
ting maximum deflection measurements with road stations, areas of high
deflections can be easily delineated to define project or subproject limits.
The deflection information can also be used in deflection-based overlay
design procedures.

Types of Equipment

Several types of pavement deflection devices are available, including
impulse deflection devices (such as the falling weight deflectometer),
steady-state dynamic deflection devices (such as the Dynaflect), and static
deflection devices (such as the Benkelman Beam). The falling weight
deflectometer (FWD) is the most commonly used deflection device for
assessing pavement responses.

The FWD is capable of simulating in magnitude and duration the
dynamic loads applied to a pavement by wheel loads. A dynamic load is
applied by dropping a set of weights onto a circular load plate. A range of
load magnitudes can be achieved by varying the number of weights used
and the heights from which they are dropped. The actual load magnitude

It is also important to get a sufficient quantity of material when
sampling. The sample size is important not only to obtain enough mate-
rial to complete the tests but also to achieve a representative sample. As
an exaggerated example, consider a graded gravel material that contains
15 percent of rock that is retained on the #4-inch (19 millimeters) screen
and passes the l-inch (25 millimeters) screen. Each of these +%-inch
(19 millimeters) rocks weigh about 250 grams. Assume that a sample size
of only 500 grams is obtained. If the sample taken happens to contain
one large rock, then the gradation test will yield 50 percent retained on
the 34-inch (19 millimeters) sieve. If the 500-gram sample scoop does
not happen to capture a large rock, then the gradation test would yield
0 percent of that size. In reality, the gradation is somewhere in between
0 percent and 50 percent. As the sample size increases, the test result
is no longer impacted by the size of the sample—it is only impacted by
the true gradation of the materials being tested. This is the point that
establishes minimum sample sizes. Typical minimum sample sizes for
aggregates and for HMA are presented in Table 10.2.

Production Lots

The quantity of HMA produced on a given project is usually divided
into defined portions called “lots.” Quality assurance and quality-control
plans usually require samples to be taken at defined intervals within
these lots. The intervals are generally measured in any of the following
two ways.

1. Time intervals. Typical lot sizes when sampling by time interval
would include either a half day’s production, or a full day’s
production.

2. Quantity intervals. Typical lot sizes when sampling by quan- |
tity interval would include 5,000 or 10,000 tons (4,536 to 9,071
tonnes), depending on anticipated production rates. Lots based on
linear feet (meters) of paving have also been used.

5.2 Methods for Evaluating

Subgrade Strength
and Stiffness

5.2.1 Introduction

Thickness design of asphalt pavements depends to a large extent on
the strength and/or stiffness of the finished subgrade. Common empirical
methods of thickness design depend on a subgrade strength index such
as the CBR or R-value. Mechanistic-empirical methods use the subgrade
resilient modulus, a measure of material stiffness, to establish pavement
thickness. In either method, characterization of the subgrade strength or
stiffness is a fundamental part of thickness design (for further information,
see Chapter 6, “Thickness Design”).

The concept of testing soils at their anticipated moisture and density
should be the governing criterion for thickness design.

Subgrade strength or stiffness of a soil or aggregate base or subbase
depends on soil type and is closely related to density and moisture con-
tent. Variable strength or stiffness can be expected, depending on the
degree of compaction and moisture content after the pavement has been
completed. If experience has indicated that a certain soil cannot be com-
pacted economically to high density, it would be unwise to perform design
tests with the soil at a high-density value.

Design strength or stiffness should be based on values obtained from
soil samples in their worst anticipated condition. To obtain such values
generally involves testing a sample that has been soaked in water.

Design methods used by different agencies vary widely, and their
approach to evaluating the strength or modulus of the unbound materials
for design purposes also varies. Evaluation methods discussed here are
probahly the most widely used methods for asphalt pavement design for
airfields and highways.

Both field and laboratory test methods have been established by vari-
ous agencies over the years. Each has advantages and disadvantages that
must be considered. Field-test methods have the advantage of testing the
unbound layers (base and subgrade) in situ at the confining pressure and
moisture content that exist at the site. This may also be a disadvantage
in that the existing conditions may not represent the intended conditions
after construction. For example, if a pavement is to be built over a satu-
rated soil, a field test would provide a strength index for the soil in a
saturated condition; however, drainage improvements to the site will likely
change the subgrade moisture content after construction.

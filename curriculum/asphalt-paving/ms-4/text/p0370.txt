When sampling hot bins, it is important to take a full cross section of the
flow from the gate because most hot-bin walls are sloped and materials
can segregate slightly. Also, the flow of material across the screen lends
itself to segregation. This does not cause any problems with the final mix
because the materials are being mixed in the pugmill, but it is important
to obtain an accurate gradation of each hot bin. Therefore, it is important
to sample each bin across the full width.

After the sampling is done and the gradations from each hot bin are
established, the ratio of each hot bin can be calculated to arrive at the
job-mix formula.

Since material from each hot bin is weighed, the percentages of the hot
bins need to be converted into weight values. Most batch computers make
this job easy by allowing you to enter the formula in weights that total
1000 pounds (453 kilograms). For example, if hot bin No. 2 is 33.5 percent
of the total mix, then the weight target in the batch formula will be 335
pounds (152 kilograms). The operator then selects the batch size—with
the batch computer doing the calculations for the operator—to establish
the weights for the entire batch. This procedure also allows the operator
to change the batch sizes without changing the formula. It is common for
batch plants to change batch size. This usually is close to the size typically
run. Most computers make this adjustment. For example, if a plant is a 4-
ton (3.6 metric tonnes) plant, but the desired load is 14 tons (12.7 metric
tonnes), the batch computer will produce four drops at 3.5 tons (3.2 met-
ric tonnes) to arrive at the target quantity.

The gradations in the final mix will only be accurate if the operator
or the computer batches the proper quantity of material from each hot
bin. The accuracy of the scale is critically important. Frequent calibration
checks are advisable. ;

Each activity is required for gradation accuracy in the final mix produced
with a batch plant. More aggregates in the job-mix formula and tighter mix
tolerances require stringent management of the various factors that can
affect final gradations.

7.3.2 Asphalt Content Control with a
Batch Plant

Asphalt content control in a batch is simpler than gradation control.
Asphalt is simply weighed to the target weight in each batch in a separate
container called the asphalt weigh bucket. A fill valve opens, the computer
watches the weight accumulate in the asphalt scale, and the computer stops
the scale just before the target weight is reached, allowing some material to
still drain from the valve and pipe until the target weight is reached. This
early shutoff function is referred to as a free-fall compensation value, and

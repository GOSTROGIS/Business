15.6.1 Introduction

Research in Europe and in the United States has shown that it is pos-
sible to build asphalt pavement surfaces that will provide reduced noise
levels. Throughout the world, sound caused by transportation systems is
the number one noise complaint. Noise generated by traffic is one of the
prime offenders. Engine (power train), exhaust, aerodynamic, and tire/
pavement noise all contribute to traffic noise. Modification of pavement
surface type and/or texture can result in significant tire/pavement noise
reductions. A quiet road surface can be built while at the same time con-
sidering safety, durability, and cost using one or more of the following
approaches:

¢ A smooth surface texture using small top-size aggregate

* An open-graded friction course (OGFC) with a high air-voids content

15.6.2 Nature of Noise

Noise, like all other sounds, is a form of acoustic energy. It differs from
pleasant sounds only in that it often disturbs us and has the characteristics
of an uninvited guest. Understanding noise requires a basic knowledge of
sound physics and how humans respond to it.

Sound is fluctuation of air in a wavelike motion. Associated with this
motion is acoustic or sound pressure that is measured in decibels. The
decibel combines the magnitude of sound with how humans hear. Because
human hearing covers such a wide range of sounds, it does not lend
itself to measurement with a linear scale. If a linear scale were used, most
sounds occurring in daily life on a scale of 0 to 1 would be recorded
between 0.00 and 0.01. Thus, it would be difficult to discriminate between
sound levels in our daily lives.

Instead of a linear scale, a logarithmic scale is used to represent sound
levels and the unit is called a decibel or dB. The term dB(A) is used when
referring to the sound levels that have been A-weighted. The ear has its
own filtering mechanisms, and the inclusion of the A after dB indicates
that the scale has been normalized or “fine tuned” to hear like a human.
The decibel scale ranges from 0 dB(A), the threshold of human hearing, to
140 dB(A) where serious hearing damage can occur. Table 15.9 represents
this scale and some of the levels associated with various daily activities.

Alongside a freeway, the sound level (i.e., noise) might be in the range
of 70-80 dB(A). As a rule, an individual can differentiate between two
levels of similar sound that are at least 3 dB(A) different in level.

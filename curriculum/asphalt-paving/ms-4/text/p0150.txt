Slag is a by-product of the iron- and steel-making processes. Slag that
is coproduced with iron in blast furnaces is known as Blast Furnace Slag
(BFS). Slag that is generated during the production of steel in basic oxy-
gen furnaces or electric arc furnaces is called steel furnace slag or steel
slag. For aggregates used in asphalt paving applications, both types of slag
are processed by the air-cooled method in which the molten slag is placed
into beds and cooled slowly under atmospheric conditions. The hardened,
crystalline material is subsequently crushed and screened.

Expanded Shale, Clay, and Slate (ESCS) are often called structural light-
weight aggregates. These aggregates are derived from the intense heat
treatment of the naturally occurring materials of shale, clay, and slate.
The raw materials are subjected to temperatures that exceed 1800-2100°F
(980-1150°C) in a rotary kiln, which causes them to expand. These aggre-
gates have a rough texture and are angular. They have a strong affinity for
asphalt cement and provide good skid resistance in asphalt paving surfaces.
Because of their insulating qualities, underlying subgrades of pavements
that contain lightweight aggregates show some evidence of reduced frost
depths. Because these aggregates may have densities as low as one-half
of conventional aggregates, adjustments are needed in mix-design calcula-
tions to accommodate for differences in specific gravity when these aggre-
gates are used in mixes in combination with conventional aggregates.

Many synthetic aggregates are made from reclaimed materials. Some
have been used for some time but on a limited basis, while others are in
developmental stages. Reclaimed materials used in these synthetic aggre-
gates include recycled plastic and glass, processed sludge and sediment,
and by-products from coal combustion.

Recycled glass has been used as an aggregate substitute in HMA paving.
The recycled glass is crushed and screened, yielding fine aggregate—typically
minus No. 4 sieve (4.75 millimeters) aggregate particles. Coarse glass particles
typically contain flat and elongated particles, which need to be avoided. The
quantity of flat and elongated particles depends on the crushing equipment
and the amount of crushing to which the glass is subjected. Specific grav-
ity of the material is 2.0-2.5, and absorption is obviously insignificant. When
recycled glass is used in HMA, glass processing must include the removal of
ferrous and nonferrous metal, plastic, and paper. The term glasphalt has at
times been used to describe pavements containing recycled glass.

There has been evidence in the past of poor bonding with asphalt binder
that has resulted in stripping and raveling.

Two other waste materials that have been used as aggregate substitutes
in HMA mixtures are ground rubber and roofing shingles. These materials
are discussed in Section 3.4, Aggregate Additives.

5.2.3 Field Test Procedures

Dynamic Cone Penetrometer

Field Test Methods

The Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP) is a field-testing device to
quickly estimate the in situ strength and deformation characteristics of
pavement subgrades. The test can be performed on native soils or on com-
pacted materials. The DCP Index that results from completing the test can
be readily correlated to a CBR for use in the empirical CBR design method.
Developed in South Africa in 1975, the DCP has since found its application
in many parts of the world because of its portability, speed, and ease of
use in flexible pavement design.

For field evaluation of subgrade stiffness, the Falling Weight Deflectometer
(FWD) is commonly used by highway agencies and airports throughout the
United States. The test is conducted by raising and dropping a weighted
package on the pavement surface and measuring vertical deflections on
the pavement surface. Using principles of mechanics, the stiffness of the
HMA layers, aggregate base, and subgrade layers can be back-calculated
at the testing location. Due to the speed and nondestructive nature of
the test, numerous tests can be conducted on a pavement feature in a
relatively short amount of time.

General

The DCP is a testing device used to estimate the in situ strength and
deformation characteristics of native soils or fills placed as part of a pave-
ment subgrade; it generally is not useful to evaluate the properties of
bases and subbases. If the test is performed on existing pavements, the
asphalt concrete surface layer must be removed, typically by coring an
appropriate-size hole in the asphalt concrete. It relies on correlations with
various soil types and the relative compaction or density of soils. Though
commonly used to estimate the structural capacity of soils, this test is now
being considered as a quality-control tool for pavement subgrades during
construction, in lieu of more common in situ density testing.

The test involves driving a cone-shaped probe (see Figure 5.7) into a
soil using a dynamic load and measuring the advancement of the probe
for each applied load. The strength of the soil being penetrated has a
direct impact on the penetration observed. Thus, the strength of the soil is
related to the depth of penetration achieved with each blow used to drive
the DCP. As previously discussed, the strength of the soil depends on den-
sity, moisture content, and soil type or classification.

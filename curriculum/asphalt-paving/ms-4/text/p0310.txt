Subsurface water can collect at the toe of cuts if appropriate drainage
is not provided. If the material encountered at grade is stratified, it will be
necessary to construct the drain to intercept the permeable layers and to
remove seepage water. A weakness of this type of construction, however, is
that water frequently bypasses the drain and rises to the surface beneath the
pavement. This is caused by variations in the local permeability of the soils.

If an appreciable gradient is involved, a cross-drain should be placed at
the downhill end of the cut. This feature will intercept any water flowing
longitudinally that, if not drained, could saturate the fills and cause slump-
ing of the fill slopes.

A properly designed, compacted, and adequately maintained dense-
graded HMA pavement will be impermeable to water. However, in some
localized conditions, subdrainage may still be required.

Drainage Related to Frost Action

When the frost depth exceeds the thickness of the pavement structure, a
subsurface drainage system may be required to lower the water table to pre-
vent the formation of ice lenses and the damaging effects of frost heaves.

Frost heaves are a serious problem. It should be noted that all saturated
or partially saturated soils will expand in volume (heave) when the water
in the pores expands by 9 percent on freezing. However, because the
water content of most subgrade soils is less than 15 percent, the overall
expansion is less than 2 percent. This likely applies to most subgrade
soils in North America. True frost heave, from a geotechnical perspective,
only occurs in a particular type of soil where the D,, of the soil is in the
range of 30-60 microns and the uniformity coefficient (D,,/D,,) is less than
about 6. This is a medium-size uniform silt. This type of soil has sufficient
permeability to attract water in the capillary zone to the freezing front and
is also fine enough to have a high capillary rise. This results in ice lenses
forming in the soil. The ice lenses grow in a downward direction (as water
in a pond freezes, being fed by water from below), as seen on Figure 5.10.
In order for ice lenses to grow in the soil, there must be freezing soil tem-
peratures, a water table close to the frost line, a soil favorable to the rapid
movement of capillary water, and a soil capable of retaining water. When
freezing temperatures are prolonged and the frost line penetrates suscep-
tible soil, the ice lenses that form cause a heave at the surface.

To avoid damage to drainage structures, determining the depth of frost
penetration is necessary for design purposes. The most reliable informa-
tion is obtained from local records of depth of frost penetration in the area
and for the soils concerned. When such local information is not available,
frost penetration can be estimated from charts obtained from the National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration National Climatic Data Center,
(www.ncdce.noaa.goy).

Drainage problems are caused by either surface flow or subsurface flow.
Each requires separate analysis and treatment.

Surface drainage includes the disposal of all water present on the pave-
ment surface and nearby ground. If possible, surface water should be
prevented from entering the subdrainage system. Inevitably, some water
enters the pavement structure, but may be kept to a minimum by surface
maintenance procedures (e.g., crack sealing, etc.).

Subsurface drainage deals with water in the granular layers and in the
subgrade soils beneath the pavement. Such water may enter the pavement
through open joints, cracks, and other openings in the surface, through
adjacent porous material, or from the ground below.

Subsurface water may be present in several forms, including these:

° Water that is free to flow under the force of gravity, referred to as “free

water”

° Water that moves by capillary forces, referred to as “capillary water”

Important differences exist between surface and subsurface drainage.
Surface runoff is usually a direct result of rainfall or melted snow. Water
flow to a subdrain depends entirely on the permeability of the soil and
the pavement courses through which the water must pass. The maximum
flow of surface water into a subdrain usually occurs within hours of a
rainfall event. In contrast, the maximum flow into a subdrain from ground-
water sources may occur several hours or even days after rainfall events.
Design requirements in the first case are based on the amount of rain or
melted snow and, in the second case, on the permeability of the soil or
aggregate.

5.3.2 Surface Drainage

Design for Roadways

Protecting pavement from the action of surface water is most easily
accomplished with full-width asphalt paving. This means that the traffic
lanes and the shoulders are paved with HMA to provide an impervious
cover that prevents surface water from directly entering the pavement struc-
ture during rainfall. The pavement surface should be free from holes and
cracks, have a permanently tight joint with the shoulder or gutter, and be
shaped and sloped (crowned) for adequate surface runoff. Surface water
usually is collected at the edge of a paved surface in ditches or gutters,
which discharge the runoff by gravity flow to a location remote from the
pavement. To prevent erosion on embankments, water should be directed
to spillways by way of curbs or dikes constructed on the extreme edge of
the shoulder. In addition, if either the volume or the velocity of the flowing
water is expected to be excessive, these features can be constructed using
asphalt lining, or other impermeable materials, to reduce the potential for
erosion.

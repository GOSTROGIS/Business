Aggregate cleanliness is one way of describing the level of surface coatings
on an aggregate. Aggregate coatings can affect the bond or adhesion of binder
to the aggregate. Aggregate cleanliness of coarse aggregate is often determined
by visual inspection, but a washed sieve analysis generally provides positive
proof. The sand-equivalent test, developed by the California Division of High-
ways and described in AASHTO T 176 (ASTM D 2419), is the most common
method of assessing the level of cleanliness of fine aggregates by determining
the relative proportion of detrimental fine dust or claylike materials. The test
method summary describes the procedure: “A measured volume of soil or
fine aggregate and a small quantity of flocculating solution are poured into a
graduated plastic cylinder and are agitated to loosen the claylike coatings from
the sand particles in the test specimen. The specimen is then ‘irrigated’ using
additional flocculating solution forcing the claylike material into suspension
above the sand. After a prescribed sedimentation period, the height of floc-
culated clay is read and the height of sand in the cylinder is determined. The
sand equivalent is the ratio of the height of sand to the height of clay times
100.” Cleaner aggregates will have higher sand equivalent values. Common
specification minimum limits for this property range from 40 to 50.

Determination of liquid limit and plasticity index properties is another
method sometimes required by specifications for assessing the clay content in
fine aggregate. Procedures for determining these properties are contained in
AASHTO T 89 and T 90 (ASTM D 4318). These procedures define liquid limit
as “the water content, in percent, of a soil at the arbitrarily defined boundary
between the semi-liquid and plastic states.” Plasticity index is defined as “the
range of water content over which a soil behaves plastically.”

Samples for both the liquid limit and the plastic limit tests use the portion
of the material that passes the No. 40 (0.425 millimeter) sieve mixed with
varying amounts of water. In the liquid limit procedure, a prepared sample is
spread in a brass cup and cut with a grooving tool, forming a notch through
the sample. The brass cup is then repetitively raised and dropped until the
divided sample sections flow together. The liquid limit is the corresponding
water content at which the sample flowed together at 25 drops of the cup.

In order to determine the plasticity index, the plastic limit of the material
must be determined. According to the test method summary of D 4318, “the
plastic limit is determined by alternately pressing together and rolling into a
¥%-inch (3.2 millimeters) diameter thread a small portion of plastic soil until
its water content is reduced to a point at which the thread crumbles and can
no longer be pressed together and re-rolled. The water content of the soil at
this point is reported as the plastic limit. . .. The plasticity index is calculated
as the difference between the liquid limit and the plastic limit.”

Use this volume

This volume provides complete classroom readings, worked examples, named practice tasks and answer guidance for people, tools and safe work. Follow the learning route in each chapter, study the labelled figures and explain your reasoning. The separate answer book contains the worked keys and observation records.

Care for people, place and the next person using the work runs through the cases. Local First Nations, Métis and Inuit learners and partners bring distinct knowledge and priorities. Participation does not require sharing personal stories or cultural teachings, and no diagram claims to represent a Nation’s protocol.

Classroom authoring is complete for the listed competencies. Qualified technical review, actual fleet/site matching, a local teaching pilot and observed practical qualification are separate requirements. The practical records are ready for an instructor to configure and use; they do not certify unobserved work.

Study chapter · Outline B2

Working safely around haul trucks

A haul truck brings material into a place where people and machines already have work to do. The transfer becomes a coordinated operation: truck, paver, ground worker and traffic control each need a shared understanding. Blind area means a space the operator cannot see from the operating position. Pinch point means a place where a person can be caught between moving or closing surfaces. Neither disappears because a worker is wearing high-visibility clothing.

At Cedar Learning Access, a delivery may arrive while someone needs the community entrance. The crew must protect both the delivery path and the public route. The task is to keep people out of the interaction between truck and paver while the approved delivery arrangement is followed.

Consequences of lapses in communication
Equipment specific hazards

Learning step 1

Consequences of lapses in communication

Communication fails when a message is unheard, misunderstood or directed at the wrong person. Agree the signalling arrangement before the approach: the designated person, the signals or radio channel, the acknowledged stop instruction and the response to lost contact. Use the established site procedure, precise messages and confirmation. A driver looking toward a worker is not proof that the worker’s intention was understood. Eye contact supports communication, but it does not replace a confirmed message or a safe position.

The ground worker identifies themselves and remains in the planned visible position with an escape route, away from the truck’s swept path and reversing space. If visual or radio contact is lost, the movement must stop under the agreed procedure; the worker does not approach a moving vehicle to restore contact. Restart only after the people involved have re-established the arrangement. Record a recurring communication failure so the system can be improved.

Learning step 2

Equipment specific hazards

Study the whole movement envelope, not only the tyre tracks. A turning truck has a swept path; the body, rear overhang and attachments can move through different areas. A reversing truck creates a changing space between itself and the paver. That space stays clear of people and loose equipment. A raised body changes height and stability; overhead utilities, structures and ground condition must be addressed before the delivery operation.

Clean mirrors, windows, cameras and lights help the operator see, but visibility aids have limits and require inspection. Spilled material, a dirty access step or a contaminated walking surface adds slip and housekeeping risks. A person should never infer the safe location from an illustration’s scale. The actual truck configuration, manufacturer instructions, assessment and traffic arrangement determine the controlled area. A pre-delivery check connects these physical conditions to the agreed communication.

Worked example · A missed acknowledgement

Fictional sequence: 08:10 the ground worker calls the truck identifier; 08:11 an unrelated driver replies; 08:12 the assigned driver begins backing. The assigned driver has not acknowledged the direction. A pedestrian also approaches the facility entrance.

Think it through.

The sequence contains no confirmed instruction between the assigned people. Stop the movement through the agreed procedure, protect the pedestrian route and re-establish the identified driver/signaller exchange. The three timestamps show order, not proof of a safe movement. The handover should record the wrong-driver reply and the access conflict; simply repeating the original call leaves the cause unresolved.

Study chapter · Outline B3

Safety working around and operating power brooms

A power broom prepares a surface by moving unwanted loose material. Its brush also moves air, dust and debris. A clean-looking surface may still have an uncontrolled airborne exposure, and a clear brush path may cross a pedestrian route. Read the machine as a combination of travel, brush movement and dust control.

The Rosco RB50 example in this chapter is the manufacturer’s Operations, Service and Parts Manual 1017258-03, Rev. B, for serial 255027 and above. Its component and warning pages are a real model study. Match a different training machine to its own manual before practical instruction.

Effects of noise and dust
Dangers associated with vehicle movement
Dangers associated with moving parts

Learning step 1

Effects of noise and dust

Noise can damage hearing and mask a spoken warning. Dust can irritate or contain hazardous constituents, including respirable silica where the material produces it. Identify the material and exposure conditions; visible dust is not a measurement of the respirable fraction. Controls may include the machine’s appropriate dust-suppression system, a suitable method and access separation, with exposure assessment and required respiratory/hearing programs.

PPE is chosen after the hazard assessment. High visibility addresses conspicuity; it does not control inhalation. Respiratory protection must match the contaminant, exposure and required fit/program conditions. Inspect the actual water system or other fitted dust-control equipment under the manual, and respond if its performance changes. Do not assume every RB50 has every optional system shown in a product picture.

Learning step 2

Dangers associated with vehicle movement

Travel and steering can create a collision even when the brush is disengaged. Establish the working route, speed/movement controls, pedestrian separation, traffic coordination and operator visibility before sweeping. Broom swing and attachments can extend beyond the tyre path. A person at the edge of the travel lane may still be within the moving assembly’s envelope.

The operator checks access and the surroundings from the intended operating position and follows the machine’s travel and sweeping instructions. The ground crew remains in the planned safe area. A new parked vehicle, visitor or traffic queue changes the route; the operator stops and the arrangement is reassessed. A warning horn tells people something is happening but does not give the machine a clear route.

Learning step 3

Dangers associated with moving parts

The rotating brush, drive mechanism, hydraulic connections and moving linkages can entangle, strike or pinch. Guarding and exclusion protect people during use; inspection, cleaning and adjustment require the applicable shutdown and hazardous-energy control. A stopped brush may still be supported by hydraulics or connected to stored energy.

Trace the component picture from brush core and bristles to support/linkage and machine. Identify which parts rotate, swing or raise. Loose clothing, hands or tools must not enter the moving mechanism. A jam or wound material is reported and dealt with under the actual isolation procedure. Reaching in for a quick pull while someone holds a control is not isolation.

Worked example · The clean strip and the dusty doorway

Fictional observation: a broom produces a visibly clean 80 m strip, but dust travels toward an occupied doorway and its fitted water system has stopped spraying. The operator proposes finishing the last 20 m first.

Think it through.

The achieved 80 m does not validate the exposure control. The planned run is 100 m; 20% remains, but that arithmetic is irrelevant to accepting an uncontrolled hazard. Stop the affected work under the procedure, protect the doorway and have the dust-control fault assessed. Resume when the task controls are established, not when the inconvenience feels small.

Safety working around and operating air hammers

An air hammer converts compressed-air energy into repeated impacts at its working tool. Air hose, coupling, retainer, handle, trigger/control and tool steel are parts to identify before learning the task. The energy that makes breaking possible can also create noise, vibration, flying material, hose movement and physical strain. Preparation therefore includes the air supply and connections as well as the hammer itself.

Noise and vibration hazards
Hazards of flying debris
Ergonomics and safe equipment handling

Learning step 1

Noise and vibration hazards

Repeated impact produces substantial noise and hand-arm vibration. Exposure depends on the actual tool, maintenance, material, use time and working conditions. A rating in a brochure is not an individual’s measured daily exposure. Use the assessed controls: appropriate equipment, maintenance, exposure organization and correctly selected hearing/PPE arrangements. Report unusual vibration, looseness, numbness or other concerns through the workplace process.

The CP1210/1230/1240 family manual provides actual pneumatic-tool safety and component information; the applicable model and configuration still matter. A lower-vibration model may improve control but does not eliminate the need to assess the job. For a training record, distinguish trigger time from total time spent in the work area.

Learning step 2

Hazards of flying debris

Impact breaks material into fragments that can strike the operator or others. Assess the material, access and nearby work; establish the required controlled area and suitable protection. Eye/face protection must match the hazard, while dust controls address the airborne material. Positioning a bystander just outside a sketch’s boundary is insufficient without the actual assessment.

Inspect the tool retention and hose/coupling arrangement under the manual. A loose connection can become an uncontrolled hose, and an unsecured tool can be ejected. Before disconnecting or changing a tool, isolate the supply and release stored pressure using the specified procedure. Never test for a leak with a hand or direct compressed air at a person.

Learning step 3

Ergonomics and safe equipment handling

A heavy tool can strain the body while carrying, starting, working or stowing. Plan access and the working position before lifting it into place. Choose the appropriate tool/support and approved handling method; avoid twisting with the load or using the body to force an unsuitable tool. Hoses should be routed to reduce tripping, dragging and damage while allowing the intended controlled movement.

Maintenance affects effort: damaged or unsuitable tool steel can increase the force and time a person applies. The operator recognizes a change and reports it instead of compensating with more force. Stowing means controlling the energy, placing the tool securely, organizing the hose and protecting others from a falling tool or protruding steel.

Worked example · Count exposure honestly

Fictional log: 09:00–09:20 setup; 09:20–09:35 tool in use; 09:35–09:45 pause; 09:45–10:05 tool in use. The worker reports “65 minutes of hammer operation.”

Think it through.

Elapsed time is 65 minutes, while recorded tool-use intervals total 15 + 20 = 35 minutes. The remaining 30 minutes are setup/pause. Keep both values because different exposures may continue during non-trigger time. The log does not calculate a permissible vibration dose; actual assessment needs the relevant exposure data and method.

Safety working around and operating propane tanks

Propane equipment is an assembly: cylinder, valve, connector, regulator where required, hose and appliance must be compatible. A familiar-looking fitting is not enough to establish compatibility. Identify each part and the pressure/function it serves before preparing the task. The acquired Fairview Tiger Torch instructions and the actual appliance markings are useful examples; they do not authorize mixing arbitrary components.

Potential fire hazards while working around propane tanks
Proper storage and handling
Proper connection procedures and check for leaks

Learning step 1

Potential fire hazards while working around propane tanks

Propane is stored under pressure and can form a flammable mixture with air when released. Escaping liquid can cause cold injury; a leak can also create an oxygen-displacement concern in an unsuitable space. Ignition sources include flames, hot surfaces, sparks and some equipment operations. Adequate ventilation, separation and the correct assembly therefore belong in the task plan.

A cylinder must be suitable, identified and within the applicable inspection/requalification requirements. Inspect its condition and the connected equipment through the prescribed pre-use checks. Odour may indicate a release, but smell is not a complete leak or concentration test. An unexpected leak, damaged connection or uncertain assembly is a reason to stop and use the emergency/isolation procedure, not to experiment with ignition.

Learning step 2

Proper storage and handling

Storage and transport are different contexts. Secure the cylinder against unintended movement in the required orientation, protect the valve and keep it in the permitted ventilated location away from prohibited heat/ignition exposures. Follow the supplier, workplace and transport requirements for the actual quantity and vehicle. The stowed torch and hose should be protected from damage; closing a valve alone does not make loose equipment adequately secured.

Identify whether the task involves use, temporary placement, storage or transport. Each can have different requirements. Keep the product SDS and relevant instruction accessible, and know the site’s spill/fire/emergency arrangements. Do not place a cylinder in a vehicle space merely because it fits there.

Learning step 3

Proper connection procedures and check for leaks

Connection is a supervised procedural skill. Confirm the cylinder/appliance identity and the specified connector type, inspect sealing surfaces and the complete hose/regulator assembly, and use the manufacturer’s connection method. Some fittings have different thread directions or sealing arrangements; an improvised adapter, force or an unspecified sealant can create a hazard. The actual instructions determine the connection and tightening method.

Use the manufacturer-approved leak-test solution and test procedure; a flame is never a leak detector. Observe the specified connection locations and response to bubbles or another leak indication. If the assembly leaks, take it out of operation and follow the prescribed isolation/repair route. Rechecking after a repair is part of the applicable procedure. An instructor assesses the actual connection and leak check with approved equipment, not a learner’s confidence alone.

Worked example · The assembly register

Fictional teaching sample: the cylinder and torch are identified, but the hose rating is unreadable and the regulator model cannot be matched to the appliance. A learner proposes connecting them because the threads fit.

Think it through.

The assembly has two unresolved compatibility/condition questions. Matching threads does not establish pressure suitability or the correct sealing design. Keep the assembly out of operation; obtain the appropriate component identification and manufacturer instructions, then have the supervised setup and leak test completed. Record the unknown components rather than replacing unknown values with guesses.

Weather effects and precautions

Weather affects the worker as well as the material. Hot mix and hot pavement add radiant heat to the work environment; sun, humidity, air movement, workload and clothing affect how the body loses heat. A forecast temperature alone cannot describe the exposure. Crew members also differ in acclimatization and health needs, which should be supported without demanding personal medical details in front of the group.

The effects of temperature on the body
The importance of proper hydration
Define effects of exposure to direct sun

Learning step 1

The effects of temperature on the body

The body produces heat during work and loses heat through exchanges with the surroundings, including evaporation of sweat. Heavy work, radiant heat and clothing can make cooling more difficult. Heat strain can develop even when the air temperature seems familiar. The work plan assesses conditions, provides appropriate controls and adjusts work/rest and supervision arrangements as conditions change.

Recognize changes in behaviour, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea or collapse and obtain help. Cold, rain and wind can also affect dexterity, judgement, traction and health. A weather response includes clothing, shelter, suitable work organization and an emergency plan. A new worker needs an acclimatization arrangement rather than an assumption that determination will make them tolerate the exposure.

Learning step 2

The importance of proper hydration

Workers need ready access to appropriate drinking water and the opportunity to drink regularly under the workplace heat plan. Hydration is one part of control; it does not replace reducing exposure, rest, cooling or recognizing illness. Do not make a class-wide medical fluid prescription or assume a person with symptoms only needs another drink.

Heat exhaustion and heat stroke require prompt response. The WorkSafeBC heat-stress guide treats heat stroke as a medical emergency. Altered mental state, collapse or other severe signs require emergency assistance: call the local emergency number, get the first-aid attendant and begin the plan’s safe cooling response while waiting for help. Do not leave the person alone or give fluids to someone unable to swallow safely. The trained response follows the workplace emergency and first-aid arrangements.

Learning step 3

Define effects of exposure to direct sun

Ultraviolet exposure can damage skin and eyes, including on days that do not feel extremely hot. The task plan combines suitable clothing/head protection, shade, scheduling and appropriate sunscreen or other protection under the guidance used by the workplace. Select protective items so they remain compatible with required PPE and the task.

A reflective or hot work surface adds to the need to assess exposure. Check breaks, water, shelter and communication before starting; recheck when work intensity, weather or worker condition changes. A short team check-in should invite early reporting without blame. Care for the crew includes making it possible to use the controls, not only telling people they exist.

Worked example · A break plan that cannot be used

Fictional schedule: a crew is assigned continuous handwork from 10:00 to 12:00. The written heat plan requires planned cool-rest opportunities, but the relief person has been reassigned. A new worker begins making confused replies.

Think it through.

The plan is not being implemented, and confusion is an emergency warning sign in this context. Stop the affected work, summon emergency/first-aid help and follow the cooling response. Arrange coverage and reassess the work before resuming. The two-hour scheduled block does not establish a safe exposure interval; it reveals a mismatch between the plan and staffing.

Safety and traffic control procedures

Traffic control is a planned system for road users, workers and equipment. Signs, devices, traffic control persons (TCPs), traffic assistants and communication have defined roles. A paving learner must understand that system even when they are not assigned to control traffic. At a community entrance, include pedestrians, cyclists, mobility needs and emergency access in the planning questions.

Traffic control procedures (TCP) Certification
Review TCP training content
Review TCP regulations

Learning step 1

Traffic control procedures (TCP) Certification

WorkSafeBC Part 18 defines traffic-control training and responsibilities. Section 18.6.2 addresses a traffic-control-person training program, including knowledge and practical competency assessment; the employer must meet the applicable qualification requirements. A classroom paving chapter does not issue that qualification. Confirm the accepted training program and the actual person’s current evidence before assigning TCP duties.

A traffic assistant has a different role and workplace training requirements under 18.6.3. Do not relabel an unqualified person a TCP because the crew needs help. Course completion records, employer orientation and site-specific instruction serve different purposes; all applicable requirements must be met.

Learning step 2

Review TCP training content

Traffic-control training develops recognition of hazards, the required plan, setup and removal of devices, safe positioning and escape routes, signs/signals, communication and responses to changing conditions. The B.C. Traffic Management Manual for Work on Roadways provides the roadwork framework, used with current regulation and road-authority requirements.

The learner reads the approved plan’s location, revision, work activity and traffic arrangement before interpreting an individual sign. A diagram from a manual is a typical arrangement with conditions, not a ready-made plan for every site. Conditions such as sight distance, road speed, intersections, queues, pedestrians and lighting affect the actual plan.

Learning step 3

Review TCP regulations

The number and positioning of people and devices follow the risk assessment, applicable requirements and approved traffic-control arrangement. There is no universal answer of one or two TCPs for all paving work. Under 18.8, the position must be identified as safe in the risk assessment; escape and approach visibility matter. Equipment is kept clean and serviceable. Precise, deliberate signals and effective communication between multiple TCPs are required by the relevant provisions.

The paver crew must not change the traffic arrangement informally when production slows or a truck arrives. Communicate the changing need to the responsible person, maintain the controls and wait for an authorized adjustment. A queue extending beyond the planned warning arrangement is a changed condition requiring action, not a reason to hurry signals.

Fictional documents: T-02 revision A places the facility entrance outside the work area. Revision B extends the work beyond that entrance. The crew has B, but a traffic briefing still uses A.

Think it through.

The drawings describe different access conditions. Study chapter · Outline B8

Plant site and gravel pit safety

A plant or gravel pit is an interconnected worksite. Material moves through feeders, crushers, screens, conveyors, stockpiles and vehicles; people may not be able to see the next process from where they stand. A stoppage at one machine can leave others running or material stored above it. Begin with the site induction, responsible supervisor and applicable jurisdiction. A mine/pit operation may be governed by B.C.'s mining safety framework; other workplaces fall under the applicable WorkSafeBC requirements.

Define noise hazards and precautions
Site specific hazards

Learning step 1

Define noise hazards and precautions

Crushing, screening, engines and material impacts can create sustained or changing noise. Hearing protection is selected within the site’s assessed program. It must remain compatible with communication and other PPE; a person who cannot hear a warning needs a safe communication arrangement rather than removing protection in the exposure area.

Review the site’s noise areas, permitted access, signs and protection requirements before entering. Equipment condition and engineering controls influence noise as well as reliability. An unusual sound can be a defect indicator, but approaching the source through a restricted area to investigate is not an appropriate response for an unassigned learner.

Learning step 2

Site specific hazards

Crushing and entanglement hazards occur at feed openings, belt/pulley nip points, rotating shafts and moving mechanisms. Guards do not authorize reaching through or around them. Isolation must account for connected equipment and stored material/energy when cleaning or maintenance is assigned. A bridge of material can collapse; an idle belt may still be connected to a starting sequence.

Vehicle/loader routes, stockpile faces and reversing areas require the site’s controls. Flying debris and dust can extend beyond a machine footprint; assess the material, collection/suppression systems and exposure. Stockpile instability, water edges and restricted plant structures also matter. Follow approved pedestrian routes and do not climb a pile or conveyor to take a better look. Report missing guards, damaged dust controls or access conflicts to the responsible person without entering the hazard.

Worked example · One stopped belt is not the whole system

Fictional process: feeder → crusher → conveyor → screen → stockpile. The conveyor stops and material begins accumulating upstream. A visitor proposes stepping over it while an operator investigates.

Think it through.

A stopped component does not establish isolation or a safe crossing. Accumulation adds a stored-material hazard and connected equipment may restart. Keep the visitor on the authorized route, report the condition and use the site’s controlled shutdown/isolation response. The process map helps identify connected sources; it does not replace the isolation plan.

Study chapter · Outline B9

Light vehicle transporting procedures

Transport preparation begins with a complete description: vehicle, trailer, load, mass, dimensions, attachments, route and driver. Gross vehicle weight rating, actual loaded mass, axle rating, tow rating and cargo-securement rating answer different questions. A small-looking load can exceed one component’s limit even when the whole combination appears capable.

The 5,000 kg categories used in the learning tasks are a comparison exercise, not a single legal switch between company and federal rules. Applicable B.C. vehicle, driver, inspection, cargo-securement and permit requirements depend on the actual combination and activity. Company rules apply alongside the law and cannot reduce its requirements.

Procedures for transportation of vehicles under 5000 kg
Procedures for transportation of vehicles over 5000 kg

Learning step 1

Procedures for transportation of vehicles under 5000 kg

For a light vehicle or combination, identify the manufacturer’s ratings, registration/licensing requirements, trailer/hitch/brake/tyre/axle suitability and the actual loaded condition. Include people, fuel, tools and attachments in the appropriate mass assessment. A nominal machine mass may exclude options or carried material. Verify the route and permitted load dimensions before departure.

Cargo must be restrained by an arrangement meeting applicable requirements and the actual anchors and tiedowns. Keep loose tools, ramps and attachments secure too. A company checklist helps organize the checks, but a tick beside 'under 5,000 kg' cannot establish that the combination is legal or safe. The driver and responsible loading personnel need a common load record.

Learning step 2

Procedures for transportation of vehicles over 5000 kg

Heavier transport adds capacity and planning questions but does not erase the same fundamentals. Determine the relevant vehicle and cargo-securement rules, driver qualifications, required inspections and any oversize/overweight permits or route restrictions. The B.C. cargo-securement information and National Safety Code Standard 10 provide the applicable framework; special commodity/equipment provisions may matter.

The weakest relevant component or a prohibited configuration can control the outcome. A tiedown’s labelled capacity is not automatically the allowable restraint of the whole system: angle, method, anchor and applicable rules matter. Loading/unloading is also a separate controlled operation, with suitable ground, ramps, alignment, separation and assigned communication. The trip record must reflect the actual loaded machine, not a previous similar delivery.

Worked example · The trailer rating check

Fictional ratings-only exercise: trailer permitted gross mass 3,500 kg; measured empty trailer 900 kg; proposed machine 2,300 kg; tools/ramps 420 kg. Ignore no other real requirement; this is one arithmetic screening check.

Think it through.

Proposed gross mass = 900 + 2,300 + 420 = 3,620 kg. It exceeds the stated trailer figure by 120 kg, so the proposed loading fails this check. The unused-looking deck space does not change the result. Even a revised load below 3,500 kg would still require axle, tow, hitch, restraint, route and driver checks.

Study chapter · Outline B10

Complete fatigue awareness training

Fatigue is reduced physical or mental capacity associated with inadequate sleep, prolonged wakefulness, demanding work or other factors. It can affect attention, reaction, memory and judgement before a person recognizes the full change. A crew depends on accurate handovers and timely responses; fatigue therefore belongs in work planning, not in a judgement about someone’s character.

Define mental fatigue
Define a work schedule that helps reduce fatigue
Discuss available training

Learning step 1

Define mental fatigue

Sleep debt accumulates when sleep is less than a person’s need. Circadian rhythms influence alertness across the day and night. A worker may have a scheduled rest interval but little opportunity to sleep after commuting, meals and personal responsibilities. Extended wakefulness and successive demanding shifts can compound the problem.

Signs may include slowed responses, difficulty concentrating, errors, irritability or difficulty staying awake. These observations do not diagnose the cause. Respond through the workplace fitness-for-task and support arrangements, with privacy and respect. Caffeine or willpower cannot be treated as a complete replacement for sleep. A fatigued person may also be unsafe to drive home; plan a safe response rather than simply sending them onto the road.

Learning step 2

Define a work schedule that helps reduce fatigue

A fatigue-aware schedule considers shift length, timing, consecutive duties, recovery opportunity, travel and workload. The employer’s planning and response arrangements should make reporting possible and provide a practical way to change the assignment when needed. A schedule can look adequate on paper while overtime, an early call-out or a long commute removes the intended recovery.

Distinguish time off duty from sleep opportunity and actual sleep. A calculation can expose an obvious planning conflict, but it cannot certify that an individual is fit. Ask whether the schedule remains workable after a delay and whether the highest-consequence tasks have appropriate staffing and supervision. Record the changed assignment and handover so the response does not create another gap.

Learning step 3

Discuss available training

Useful fatigue-awareness training explains causes, signs, work and driving effects, reporting, prevention and the workplace response. CCOHS provides a retained evidence source for this study. Evaluate an external course by its content, current evidence, instructor/provider suitability and relevance to the actual work; a website name or completion badge alone does not establish effective controls.

The learner practises recognizing a condition, communicating it without blame and using the agreed support route. The supervisor practises responding to the work risk rather than demanding private information in front of colleagues. A training record identifies the content completed, the learner’s demonstrated understanding and the employer-specific arrangements still required.

Worked example · Rest interval versus sleep opportunity

Fictional shift finishes at 20:00; the next begins at 06:00. Total travel uses 2 hours, meals/personal preparation 1.5 hours and a required call 0.5 hour. Calculate the maximum remaining interval before other interruptions.

Think it through.

There are 10 hours between shifts. Subtracting 2 + 1.5 + 0.5 leaves at most 6 hours available. That is an opportunity estimate, not proof of six hours of sleep or a universal fitness threshold. The schedule needs review through the workplace fatigue process, especially before safety-critical work or driving.

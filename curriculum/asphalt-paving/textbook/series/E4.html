Determine compaction levels

Density relates mass to volume. Compaction changes the arrangement of particles and air voids, but the measured density must be compared with the correct reference and method. Two instruments can report numbers that look similar while representing different locations or measurement principles. Keep the instrument, sample, units and denominator visible.

Learning step 1

Two methods to determine compaction

A nuclear density gauge uses a radioactive source and detector arrangement to infer a material property through its calibrated method. The actual instrument, mode, calibration/standardization, surface preparation and test method affect the result. In Canada, possession/use and transport require the applicable CNSC licensing, training, security and radiation-safety arrangements. Reading the Troxler 3430 manual is useful instrument study; it does not authorize source handling or operation.

A core is a physical specimen removed at an identified location. The approved coring and laboratory method establish sampling, handling, dimensions and density determination. Coring also requires utility/location checks, traffic/access controls and safe equipment/water management, followed by the specified repair of the hole. A damaged, contaminated or mislabelled core may not support the intended result.

Gauge and core results are compared only through the specified procedure and any required correlation. A percentage density must state its reference: maximum theoretical mixture density, laboratory compacted reference or another defined basis. Do not compare percentages that use different denominators. The result belongs to the sampled location and acceptance unit defined by the contract; one point does not automatically represent an entire project.

Report original readings, checks, location and any limitation. A low result prompts the approved investigation/disposition process. Additional rolling is not automatically appropriate after the material’s workable window or when the cause is unknown.

Worked example · The denominator changes the meaning

Fictional in-place density is 2.34 t/m³. Reference A, maximum theoretical density, is 2.50 t/m³. Reference B, a compacted laboratory reference in a different teaching system, is 2.40 t/m³.

Think it through.

Against A: 2.34/2.50 × 100 = 93.6%. Against B: 2.34/2.40 × 100 = 97.5%. Both calculations use the same numerator; they answer different defined questions. Neither establishes acceptance without the applicable method and criterion. Never relabel 97.5% as percent of maximum theoretical density.

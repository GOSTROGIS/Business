Study chapter · Outline H1

Milling and reclaiming equipment

Milling removes pavement to a controlled profile. Reclaiming processes existing pavement and underlying material to form a designed layer. Understanding the material route helps a learner recognize the machine’s working parts and the people who must stay clear of them.

Milling equipment
The function of milling equipment
Major mechanical components
Potential failures, symptoms and indicators of failure

Learning step 1

Milling equipment

Identify manufacturer, model, serial number, rotor configuration and fitted options before selecting information. The CAT cold-planer parts guide illustrates assemblies, while the performance handbook explains equipment families. Neither replaces the matching operation and maintenance manual (OMM). A brochure can show a machine’s purpose without describing its safe operating sequence. Record which document supports identification, which supports operation and which is specific to the training machine.

Learning step 2

The function of milling equipment

A cold planer uses a cutting rotor to remove a controlled depth, producing a milled profile and material for collection. A reclaimer mixes a designed depth of existing layers; specified water, binder or stabilizing agents may be introduced through a controlled system. Foamed asphalt is a particular binder-treatment process, not simply any hot asphalt mixed with water. Reclaiming, grading, compaction and paving form a coordinated sequence with design and testing requirements. Reuse can reduce virgin-material demand, but mixture suitability and performance still need verification.

Learning step 3

Major mechanical components

Trace power from engine through the drive systems to the rotor and travel system. Identify cutting tools and holders, rotor drum, cutting chamber, side plates, collecting and discharge conveyors, tracks or wheels, lifting columns, water/dust-control system, operator station, grade sensors and guards. The cutting tools fracture the surface; conveyors move the recovered material; grade controls influence the cut relative to a reference. Call a component by its actual manual name and point to it from a safe position. A guard is part of the machine’s protection, not an optional cover.

Learning step 4

Potential failures, symptoms and indicators of failure

Unusual noise, vibration, smell, leaks, damaged tools, cracks or warning messages are observations requiring action. State where and when they occur and whether conditions are worsening. Stop and secure the machine when a hazard or serious fault is suspected, keep others clear and notify the responsible person. Only authorized personnel diagnose or repair within their competence. Never search for a high-pressure leak with a hand or reach into a stopped-looking conveyor. Record the defect and authorized return-to-service decision.

Worked example · A noise with a location

A new rhythmic noise and vibration begin at the discharge conveyor after a load change. A learner suggests increasing speed to clear it.

Think it through.

Report the change and location, stop safely under the machine procedure and protect the area. Have the responsible person arrange inspection under the required isolation. Increasing speed is an unsupported intervention and may worsen the fault.

Study chapter · Outline H2

Milling and reclaiming safety procedures

A milling plan must account for the cutting system, material discharge, truck movement, traffic and people on the ground. Agree on boundaries and communication before the rotor or travel system moves.

Interpret and follow job plan
Awareness of machine-specific safety
Traffic Control requirements
Proper PPE
Lockout procedures
Secure equipment for safety

Learning step 1

Interpret and follow job plan

Read the path, pattern, depth, pass sequence, utilities, obstacles, discharge arrangement and planned stopping points. Confirm the current revision and who can authorize a change. Walk the area only when access is controlled. Resolve utility information and unidentified obstructions before cutting. Brief the plan with ground crew, truck drivers and traffic-control personnel, using a drawing and read-back.

Learning step 2

Awareness of machine-specific safety

Recognize rotor and conveyor entanglement, ejected material, silica-containing dust, exhaust, noise, hot surfaces, pressurized hydraulics, blind areas and raised components. Dust control is an engineered system requiring inspection and effective operation; a visible spray does not prove adequate exposure control. Block or support raised equipment using specified devices before access. Guard removal, clearing jams and service require the applicable isolation procedure.

Learning step 3

Traffic Control requirements

The traffic-control plan and site assessment determine TCP positions, numbers, signs, devices and communication. Milling traffic, public traffic and pedestrians can change relative positions as work advances. Keep a defined route and reassess driveways, intersections and service access. Only trained and authorized people perform assigned traffic-control roles. Stop if control or communication is lost.

Learning step 4

Proper PPE

Select PPE through the hazard assessment and current requirements, including suitable footwear, head, eye, hearing and high-visibility protection as applicable. Respiratory protection requires the employer’s program, correct selection, training and fit testing where required. WorkSafeBC Part 8 assigns workers specified basic clothing, general-purpose gloves, footwear and safety headgear, while employers provide other required PPE; agreements may provide more. The employer must ensure required PPE is used and maintained.

Learning step 5

Lockout procedures

Identify every hazardous energy source, including engine/fuel, electrical, hydraulic, gravity, rotating parts and stored pressure. Use the machine-specific written lockout procedure: stop, isolate, secure, dissipate or restrain stored energy and verify the safe state before work. A key removed or battery switch off does not alone establish full isolation. Each worker’s protection follows the applicable lockout system.

Learning step 6

Secure equipment for safety

Park in the designated stable location, lower or support components as instructed, apply parking controls and required chocks, and secure keys and access. Protect the public from the machine and residual hazards. Remove or secure detachable items without leaving guards missing. Record defects and communicate the machine’s status to the next shift.

Worked example · A changed discharge route

The planned truck position is blocked by a delivery vehicle. Someone proposes swinging the conveyor over a pedestrian route.

Think it through.

Stop the conflicting movements, preserve the pedestrian boundary and ask the lead to revise the arrangement with traffic control. Check overhead and swept-path hazards. Resume only after the new plan is communicated and controls are in place.

Study chapter · Outline H3

Milling and reclaiming operator roles and responsibilities

Milling is team work with distinct control responsibilities. Clear roles prevent two people giving incompatible instructions and ensure the operator receives reliable information about the cut and surrounding movement.

The role of Milling Machine Operator
The role of ground men
Hand signals communication

Learning step 1

The role of Milling Machine Operator

The machine operator controls assigned travel and working functions from the operator station, monitors warnings and machine response, and maintains awareness of the work area. “Top end” describes a crew role, not permission to ignore ground observations. The operator must know who gives movement instructions, what the stop signal means and when to stop for uncertainty. A ground guide cannot remove the operator’s duty to operate safely.

Learning step 2

The role of ground men

Ground crew members may check cut depth/profile, manage assigned grade controls, observe the material route and coordinate truck positioning from approved locations. Duties depend on the machine and work plan. No person should measure beside moving cutting equipment or stand in a truck’s pinch zone to improve visibility. Agree who reports depth, who communicates with trucks and who records quality. Rotate or adjust work when fatigue, noise or visibility makes the assigned role ineffective.

Learning step 3

Hand signals communication

Before work, rehearse a small agreed set of movement and stop signals, radio wording and acknowledgement. Use one designated movement signaller at a time, while anyone can give an emergency stop. If a signal is unclear, conflicting or lost, stop and re-establish communication. Crane/hoisting work has applicable standardized signal requirements; a crew-created milling signal must not replace them. Use plain words with machine ID, direction and limits; avoid “over there” or an unexplained horn pattern.

Worked example · Two people signal

One ground worker signals a truck forward while another signals stop. The operator sees both.

Think it through.

Stop the affected movement. Clarify the hazard and designated signaller, confirm the driver and operator understand the revised instruction, and resume only when the area is clear and the communication arrangement is restored.

Milling and reclaiming equipment maintenance

Daily maintenance protects the next operator as well as the machine. Use the actual model’s maintenance schedule and safe work instructions. Record what was checked, what was serviced and what remains out of service.

Service lubrication system (greasing)
Service air intake system as required (air filters / pre cleaners)
Drain fuel tank sump, water separator fuel filters if equipped
Add fluids as required
Inspect and clean components as required
Housekeeping tasks

Learning step 1

Service lubrication system (greasing)

Identify grease points from the machine chart, lubricant specification, interval and required machine position. Clean fittings before connection and load the grease gun according to its instructions. Use the specified quantity or completion criterion; generic numbers of gun strokes and visible overflow are not universal rules. Some bearings and centralized systems have different requirements. Do not reach past guards or into a hazard to access a fitting. Collect excess lubricant and report damaged fittings or blocked lines.

Learning step 2

Service air intake system as required (air filters / pre cleaners)

Check the intake restriction indicator and service the pre-cleaner as instructed. Keep dirt out of the clean side of the system. Inspect seals and housings; a damaged seal can defeat a clean filter. Clean an element only where the manufacturer permits it and with its method and limits; otherwise replace it. Reduced-pressure air is not automatic permission to blow out every filter. Do not run without the required element.

Learning step 3

Drain fuel tank sump, water separator fuel filters if equipped

Where fitted, service the fuel sump/water separator using the specified safe conditions and collection method. Identify water, debris or unusual contamination and report it. Keep ignition sources controlled and avoid skin contact. Dispose of collected fluid through the approved route. Opening a high-pressure fuel line is not a routine water-drain task.

Learning step 4

Add fluids as required

Identify each reservoir and correct fluid before adding anything. Check on the specified ground condition, temperature and engine state. A two-sided dipstick may have different stopped/running scales; read the marking and manual. Clean the fill area and tools, avoid mixing incompatible fluids and do not overfill. Unexpected consumption or a repeated low level requires investigation, not endless topping up.

Learning step 5

Inspect and clean components as required

Inspect radiator and oil-cooler surfaces, plugs, dipsticks, battery condition, hoses and accessible guards. Cooling surfaces can be damaged by inappropriate pressure or tools. Hot coolant and batteries have their own hazards; use the safe procedure and authorized access. Record leak location and appearance without touching a pressurized leak. Electrical and mechanical repairs belong to authorized personnel.

Learning step 6

Housekeeping tasks

Remove rubbish and loose items from safe access areas. Clean excess grease, steps and handholds, and keep waste out of drains. Account for tools and restore guards before release. Enter meter reading, date, tasks, products, defects and next action in the maintenance record. A blank box is ambiguous: mark completed, not applicable with reason, or held for correction.

Worked example · The wrong grease assumption

A chart specifies a particular grease and service interval. A worker brings an unlabelled cartridge and proposes ten strokes at every fitting.

Think it through.

Do not use the unlabelled product or generic stroke count. Confirm lubricant, points, quantities and safe positions from the matching chart. Obtain the correct product and record any blocked or damaged fitting for action.

Study chapter · Outline H5

Markers, grades and stakes

A stake is a compact instruction that depends on its location, legend and reference. Read the whole mark before acting. Protect survey control so the next crew inherits reliable information.

Abbreviations used on stakes
Interpret stakes

Learning step 1

Abbreviations used on stakes

Common abbreviations include BM or B/M—benchmark; C—cut; CB or C/B—catch basin; SMH—sanitary manhole; EL/Elv—elevation; FG—finished grade; PL—property line; RW—right of way; SAN or S—sanitary; Mon—monument; G—gas; PVC—polyvinyl chloride. Project legends can differ, so confirm the meaning and units. A gas mark identifies a potential utility concern; it does not give complete location, depth or clearance information. An elevation is a height relative to a stated datum, not a depth below the current surface.

Learning step 2

Interpret stakes

Read station, side, offset, target/reference mark, cut/fill, grade and slope together. Station 0+125 means 125 m along the defined alignment in this metric example. An offset stake sits away from the point it describes; preserve both the offset and direction. Property pins and survey monuments are protected control, not disposable construction stakes. A temporary benchmark may use an assumed datum, while other control is tied to a geodetic datum; do not combine them without an authorized relationship.

Cross-heads and reference marks can carry a level used to transfer information. Confirm which physical mark is the reference before measuring. Locate work such as catch basins, manholes and headwalls from the approved layout. Compare the milling limit and required profile with the marks before setting controls. If a stake is disturbed, illegible or conflicts with the plan, protect the evidence and request verification. An apparently small guess can propagate along an entire pass.

Worked example · Read an offset

A fictional stake says STA 0+125, 2.0 m RT OFFSET, FG EL 102.400, C 0.060. The legend defines cut from the marked reference to the required grade.

Think it through.

Locate station 125 m on the alignment and interpret the right-side offset using the project convention. The stated cut is 60 mm from the defined reference, not automatically from the top of the stake. FG is 102.400 m in the stated datum. Confirm the target location and mark before using the value.

Study chapter · Outline H6

Operate milling and reclaiming equipment

Machine operation develops through supervised practice on the selected equipment. The sequence below explains the decisions and observations required; the instructor uses the matching OMM for control positions, settings and exact operating steps.

Plan job
Position the machine
Engage teeth
Move forward
Make adjustments
Control depth
Set Controls

Learning step 1

Plan job

Review the training or job plan, permitted surface, depth, line, material discharge, weather, utilities and exclusion areas. Complete pre-start checks and daily maintenance, identify emergency stopping controls, and agree on communications. The instructor defines the permitted operating envelope and observes the learner. A changed condition requires a new assessment before continuing.

Learning step 2

Position the machine

Position the machine on the approved approach with adequate support and clearance. Align the truck or approved side-discharge arrangement without placing a person between moving equipment. Check the full conveyor movement envelope, including overhead electrical hazards. Use the designated guide and stop if visibility or acknowledgement is lost.

Learning step 3

Engage teeth

Engage and lower the cutting system only through the selected machine’s sequence, with guards and dust controls effective and everyone clear. Rotor speed, depth and engagement conditions are model- and task-specific. A rotating drum is not approached for a visual check or manual clearing. Unexpected impact or vibration requires an appropriate stop and report.

Learning step 4

Move forward

Move along the verified line with smooth control and continuous observation. Painted lines, string references or the previous cut may guide alignment when authorized. Keep the cutting and discharge processes coordinated; do not focus so closely on a line that people, trucks or obstacles disappear from attention.

Learning step 5

Make adjustments

Make deliberate, controlled adjustments after identifying the reason. Large repeated changes can create an uneven profile and obscure the cause. Communicate changes to ground crew and allow the machine’s response to be evaluated. If the machine behaves unexpectedly, stop for investigation instead of chasing the error with more controls.

Learning step 6

Control depth

Set and verify cut depth against the approved reference. Check at safe, authorized locations and record station, side and measured depth. Existing surface variation, sensor reference changes and machine movement can influence the result. Average depth alone does not establish an acceptable profile everywhere.

Learning step 7

Set Controls

Study chapter · Outline H7

Follow shut down procedures

Shutdown leaves the machine in a known safe state for people, maintenance and the next shift. A parked machine can still contain heat, pressure and gravity hazards.

Shut down procedures

Learning step 1

Shut down procedures

Choose the designated stable parking location with service access and adequate separation from traffic, pedestrians, edges and drainage routes. Plan access for a possible failure to restart. Use level ground where the machine procedure requires it, including accurate fluid checks. Complete the machine’s stopping, cooling and shutdown sequence; do not invent a universal idle time.

Place the rotor, conveyor and raised components in their specified safe positions. Apply parking controls and required chocks or blocking. Shutdown and maintenance lockout serve different purposes: if someone will enter a hazardous area or service the machine, apply the full required isolation and verification. A parked machine with the key removed is not automatically ready for service.

After the required safe state is established, walk around from safe access areas. Look for leaks, damage, missing guards, debris and anything that changed during the shift. Do not open a hot pressurized system to complete a routine check. Record defects, meter reading, remaining materials and unresolved work. Mark a machine out of service when required and ensure the status reaches the next operator.

Secure keys, controls, access panels and detachable items according to the site plan. Consider public access after the crew leaves and protect residual hazards. Confirm the final state with the instructor or responsible person. A completed checklist identifies the actual machine, manual procedure and observer; a ticked generic form alone is not evidence of a safe shutdown.

Worked example · Service access tomorrow

The nearest parking place is level but blocks an emergency access route. Another approved area provides stable ground and service access.

Think it through.

Use the approved location that preserves emergency access. Follow the machine shutdown procedure, secure components and parking controls, inspect safely and hand over the final state. Convenience does not justify blocking the route.

Study chapter · Outline H8

Transport milling and reclaiming equipment

Transport is a planned operation involving machine configuration, carrier capacity, route and load securement. The person driving the carrier and the person moving the machine need a shared plan and clearly assigned responsibilities.

Roles and responsibilities
Prepare machine for transport
Loading and unloading procedures

Learning step 1

Roles and responsibilities

The carrier/driver checks legal loading, axle and vehicle ratings, route/permit requirements and cargo securement within their role. The designated machine operator follows the OMM loading procedure. A trained ground guide controls the agreed communication from a safe position. Do not assume a job title automatically authorizes loading. Confirm who checks ramp suitability, machine mass/configuration, attachments and final securement before movement.

Learning step 2

Prepare machine for transport

Prepare the machine in its specified transport configuration: conveyor, guards, attachments, locks and loose items. Remove attachments only with the approved handling method and rated lifting/rigging equipment where required. Know the actual mass, dimensions and centre-of-gravity implications of fitted equipment. Use designated tie-down points; a convenient handrail or hydraulic line is not one. Secure detached items as separate cargo. Retain guards needed for safe loading.

Learning step 3

Loading and unloading procedures

Inspect the carrier, ramps, approach and ground for capacity, support, alignment and traction. Establish a controlled area and agreed stop signal. Follow the machine’s direction, speed and configuration requirements when crossing changes in slope. Stop if a guide is lost or alignment becomes uncertain. After positioning, apply parking/support requirements and the compliant securement system. National Safety Code Standard 10 and applicable BC rules govern the arrangement; chain count alone does not prove adequate restraint. At unloading, control the machine before releasing restraints in the authorized sequence.

Worked example · A mass record that excludes attachments

The transport plan lists a base machine mass of 19,800 kg. A fitted conveyor option and attachments add 1,250 kg.

Think it through.

Planned machine load is at least 21,050 kg before any other cargo. Confirm actual configuration, carrier/ramp/axle ratings and route requirements. The base-machine figure is insufficient for release; obtain the transport lead’s verified plan.

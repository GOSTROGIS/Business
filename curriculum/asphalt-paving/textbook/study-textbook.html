Asphalt paving · Foundations and safe work

A road is a connection between people

A delivery gets through. A family reaches a service. A worker returns home. Good paving depends on material knowledge, careful preparation and people who understand one another. This volume teaches you to read the work, name what you see, check a quantity and explain a safe next step.

The learning follows A1–A6 and B1 of the SkilledTradesBC Asphalt Paving/Laydown Technician program outline. Seven chapters contain the readings, definitions, drawings, worked examples and practice information needed for this stage. Later volumes will develop B2–L6. This is the 30 October 2026 classroom development edition; qualified technical review and classroom pilot are pending. Practical competence requires supervised instruction, observation and the applicable workplace authorization.

Start with the purpose of the road

An asphalt paving/laydown technician helps turn a prepared site and a specified mixture into a pavement that serves its intended use. The work connects materials, machines, people and records. Infrastructure means the systems that support daily activity: roads are part of that network, alongside services such as drainage and utilities. A road can carry a delivery, support trade, provide access to school or health care, or serve recreation. The same route may serve several purposes.

The person using the finished road sees the surface. The crew also needs to understand what supports it. A suitable foundation, drainage, appropriate materials and verified construction all contribute to performance. A dark, smooth surface alone cannot tell you whether those conditions were met.

Cedar Learning Access, fictional case. A learning centre needs an access route that will serve people arriving on foot and in vehicles. The owner has appointed a designer and contractor. Before work starts, the crew needs current drawings, an agreed scope, a traffic and public-access arrangement, suitable equipment and a clear safety orientation. A neighbour's request about an entrance matters, but the crew must refer a proposed design change through the authorized project process. Listening and recording the concern helps the right person respond.

Follow the material and the information

Aggregate is mineral material such as sand, gravel or crushed stone. A pit is a source associated with excavating deposits such as sand and gravel; a quarry typically supplies rock that is extracted and processed. Crushing reduces particle size; screening separates material by size. Suitable material comes from an approved supply and must meet the project requirements. Finding nearby stone does not establish its suitability, extraction permission or environmental approval.

B.C. has varied landscapes and resource locations. Transport distance, the approved grading and properties of the aggregate, access and supply consistency affect a project. The crew uses the specified source and delivery records. A local material can be useful, but “local” is not a quality test. Equally, a long haul can change scheduling and logistics without changing the required product.

Asphalt binder, also called asphalt cement in the handbook, holds mineral particles together in an asphalt mixture. It is different from Portland cement, which is used in concrete. A plant proportions and processes the required materials. A haul vehicle brings the mixture to the paving operation. The paver receives and moves the mixture; its screed contributes to shaping the mat. Rollers compact the placed material, and the work is checked through the required inspection and testing process.

The information travels with the material. A delivery ticket identifies a load; a drawing identifies where the work belongs; a specification defines requirements; a test record reports an observation under a stated method. These records answer different questions. A ticket's mass cannot, by itself, prove the thickness or density achieved in the road.

Build the material story

Draw five connected stages: source, production, delivery, placement and compaction/checking. At each stage, add one material or machine term, one purpose and one record or question. Roads have different surfaces and purposes

An earth road uses the ground surface, which may be shaped or improved. A gravel road has an unbound mineral surface. Asphalt pavement uses an asphalt-bound surface; concrete pavement uses a cement-based material. Supporting layers and drainage vary with the design. An ice road is a seasonal route whose opening, loading and use require the relevant authority's controls. A photograph does not establish safe ice capacity or permission to travel.

A resource road describes an access context, often connected with resource activity; it does not name one surface. A logging road describes a use. A corduroy road describes a construction form using logs laid across the direction of travel. Those three terms are not interchangeable. Chapter 2 develops the history and the evidence for different methods.

A forecast of openings over ten years is different from the number employed in one year. Construction includes building work and civil engineering as well as paving. Compare environmental claims within a boundary

Asphalt and concrete are pavement materials, not complete environmental outcomes. Compare a defined service, time period and system boundary. A material-production figure alone leaves transport, construction, maintenance, use and end-of-life outside the answer. A reusable material is not automatically the option with the lowest total impact. The amount recovered, replacement of new material, processing energy, transport and required performance all matter.

Evidence card I5 · Material recovery, a bounded comparison. The U.S. EPA's March 2004 Hot Mix Asphalt Plants chapter describes asphalt mixtures that may include reclaimed asphalt pavement, or RAP, alongside aggregate and asphalt cement. FHWA's undated historical Recycled Concrete Aggregate review describes concrete being recycled as aggregate, principally for base material in the review's U.S. context. These are two evidenced recovery pathways. Neither card provides a matched B.C. life-cycle emissions comparison, equal pavement designs, haul distances or a common service life. They support “both materials can enter recovery pathways,” not “either material is always greener.” The references describe mechanisms rather than current local market shares.

Ask what a road was built to do

Road history is a history of materials, movement, power and relationships with place. A route can serve travel, trade, access to resources, settlement or military movement. Its purpose can change. A road that enables one activity may also affect land, water, access and existing travel routes. A timeline should identify whose account is being used and avoid treating one engineering tradition as the beginning of all movement.

In B.C., a classroom discussion of road history does not give permission to map or publish community-held routes or knowledge. Use the public evidence cards below. Invite local knowledge only through the appropriate relationships and agreed purpose. The lesson asks you to explain change, not to speak for a community.

Evidence cards for a timeline

H1 · Via Appia. UNESCO describes the Appian Way as a major Roman road constructed and developed from 312 BCE to the fourth century CE. Its initial strategic purpose included military conquest. It later supported connected settlements, agriculture and trade. Engineering works included the road and associated structures. One road can have several purposes over time; “Roman” does not mean “the world's first road.”

H2 · Alaska Highway. Public Services and Procurement Canada's history, updated 16 December 2025, explains that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers built the road during the Second World War to provide land access to Alaska. It identifies local First Nations' vital role as guides. The pioneer road was completed in 1942, and the permanent location in 1943. Responsibility for the Canadian portion transferred to National Defence in 1946. Official civilian non-military use began in 1948. Further responsibility changes occurred in 1961, 1964 and 1992. These are different events, not competing dates for the same event.

H3 · Post-war highways. FHWA identifies the U.S. Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 as a major step in the Interstate system. Its historical discussion connects the system with national planning and everyday movement of goods and people. The 1950s were within the Cold War period. A date and a national-security context do not prove that every new road had one motive; use evidence for the particular project.

H4 · Present resource roads. B.C.'s resource-road information describes a network used for resource industries and access. A resource road is not necessarily paved, and a logging road is not necessarily a corduroy road. Its owner, administrator, condition and access rules need to be established from the relevant record. A present-day category does not describe every historical method used to build it.

Materials and methods solve different problems

An earth route uses the ground surface, sometimes shaped or improved. Stone and gravel add mineral material. Cut or shaped stone can form a durable surface where the design, foundation and maintenance suit the use. Wood has also been used: a corduroy road is a historical construction form using closely laid logs across the travel direction, often associated with soft or wet ground. The log pattern explains the name. It is a method, while logging road describes a road's use; the categories overlap in some histories but are not synonyms.

Asphalt pavement combines aggregate with a bituminous binder. Concrete pavement uses a cement-based binding system. These materials have different production and construction processes. Do not confuse asphalt cement—the binder in an asphalt mixture—with Portland cement used in concrete. A road may have several layers and may be repaired with a different material from its original surface. Appearance alone is not enough to identify its entire construction.

Modern asphalt placement commonly involves delivery of mixture, controlled placement through a paver and screed, and compaction using rollers, with associated preparation, checks and finishing. Mechanization changes how material is moved and shaped; it does not remove the need for a suitable foundation, drainage, specifications or skilled people. This unit describes the change in method. Operating settings belong to the relevant supervised training and approved documents.

Worked example · Correct a category mistake. A museum draft says, “All logging roads were corduroy roads, and modern roads replaced wood with asphalt.” The first clause confuses purpose with method. The second implies one universal sequence. A better label is: “Corduroy construction used logs across a route. It was one historical way of addressing difficult ground. Logging roads have used varied surfaces and construction methods; modern asphalt paving is one distinct technology.”

Follow the material through the system

Paving technology connects material sources, manufacturing, transport and placement. Begin with the function of each stage. A pit or quarry supplies mineral material; processing prepares aggregate to specified characteristics; a plant combines selected materials; haul vehicles move the mixture; paving equipment places and shapes it; rollers compact the placed layer. Preparation, testing, traffic arrangements and finishing also matter. A flow map is a way to understand relationships, not permission to operate equipment.

Know what the ingredients do

Aggregate is mineral material such as processed sand, gravel or crushed stone. A pit generally obtains unconsolidated deposits; a quarry extracts rock that may need crushing and screening. Material availability does not establish suitability: the required properties come from the approved design and specification. A short haul distance alone cannot answer whether a source meets the project requirements.

Asphalt binder, also called asphalt cement in many documents, binds the aggregate in asphalt mixtures. It is distinct from the Portland cement used in concrete. Additives and modified binders can change mixture characteristics for a defined purpose, but their selection and proportions require the applicable approved information. Fuel used to heat a production process is not automatically an ingredient in the pavement mixture. A delivery label must identify the actual product rather than relying on the loose word oil.

Distinguish batch and drum production

A batch plant prepares identifiable batches: aggregate is proportioned and combined with binder in a mixer. A drum-mix plant uses a continuous process in which material flows through the plant. Drum designs differ; do not treat every drum layout as identical. The beginner distinction is batch quantities versus a continuing material stream, not “one uses a drum and the other has no drum.” A batch plant can have a drying drum before its mixer.

Separate the recycling routes

Reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) is asphalt pavement material recovered for possible reuse. A plant-recycling route takes recovered material through suitable processing and an approved production process. Reuse is conditional on the design and quality requirements. A claim that all recovered material can be added without testing is unsupported.

Hot-in-place recycling treats an existing asphalt surface on the road using a coordinated equipment train. Its broad purpose is to reuse existing material in place through a specialist process. It is different from hauling recovered material to a plant. A milling machine's removal of material is also not proof that the removed material has been successfully recycled. Track the actual route and evidence for reuse.

An asphalt train is a coordinated sequence of equipment and roles. In ordinary placement, haul, paver/screed and compaction functions connect. A specialist hot-in-place train has its own process and equipment. The word train does not mean every paving job uses the same machinery, controls or sequence.

Begin with the work and the people affected

A hazard is a source or situation with the potential to cause harm. Risk considers the likelihood and consequences of that harm under the actual conditions. A control changes those conditions to prevent or reduce exposure. A control needs an owner, a way to check it and a response if it fails. “Be careful” does not tell a new worker which action is required.

The paving crew works around moving equipment, hot materials, changing surfaces and other people. A task that was controlled yesterday may change with wind, visibility, traffic, equipment condition or a different worker. Before work begins, the crew needs a shared understanding of the task and the current controls. During the work, a changed condition needs attention rather than silent improvisation.

The examples in this chapter teach recognition and decision making. The workplace must provide its own orientation, procedures, training, qualified supervision and emergency arrangements. A classroom exercise does not qualify a person to rig a load, operate machinery, direct traffic or act as the designated first-aid attendant.

7.1 · PPE is selected for a hazard and a person

Personal protective equipment (PPE) is equipment worn to protect a person against specified hazards. Selection starts with an assessment of the exposure and the work. A product that protects against one hazard may not protect against another. A general-purpose glove is different from a glove selected for a particular chemical; eye protection and face protection have different roles; hearing protection must suit the assessed noise exposure and the required program.

Fit and compatibility matter. A respirator must be appropriate for the contaminant and conditions and used within the required respiratory-protection program. A generic “dust mask” description does not establish adequate protection against a gas, oxygen deficiency or a particular dust. A facepiece, eyewear, headgear and other items must work together without making one another ineffective. Training includes the limits of the equipment, not just how to put it on.

Know the provision responsibilities

WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation 8.2 makes the worker responsible for clothing needed for the natural elements, general-purpose work gloves, appropriate footwear including safety footwear, and safety headgear. The employer provides all other PPE required by the regulation at no cost to the worker. Agreements may provide for the employer to supply some or all of the worker-responsibility items. If employer-provided PPE causes allergenic or other adverse health effects, the employer must provide appropriate alternatives or safe measures. Read the actual agreement and the complete regulation before deciding how an item is supplied.

Responsibility to provide an item does not remove the employer's duties concerning suitable protection, instruction and the workplace. Under 8.3, PPE must provide effective protection, be compatible, avoid creating a hazard and be maintained in good working order and sanitary condition. Where workplace evaluation is required, 8.4 addresses consultation. 8.5 specifies program elements for chemical exposure or oxygen-deficient atmospheres, including selection, use, inspection, cleaning, maintenance, storage and training.

7.2 · Recognize how exposure happens

A hazard assessment identifies the job steps, people exposed, hazards, existing controls and further controls needed. It must consider routine work and foreseeable changes. Cleaning, clearing a blockage, maintenance, refuelling and a public-access change can introduce hazards that are absent from normal production.

Inhalation brings an airborne contaminant into the respiratory system. Carbon monoxide can occur with combustion equipment; hydrogen sulphide can be a concern in particular petroleum-related or confined-space conditions; welding produces fumes specific to the process and materials. Dust-generating work can create respirable crystalline silica exposure. Disturbing suspect asbestos-containing material requires identification and the applicable controls. These examples identify possible hazards to assess, not a claim that every paving site contains every substance. Smell, appearance and a photograph cannot establish a safe airborne concentration.

Ingestion can occur when contamination reaches the mouth through hands, food, drink or other objects. Absorption occurs when a substance enters through skin; its significance depends on the substance. Injection can occur when high-pressure fluid penetrates skin. A small injection wound can conceal serious injury: report the event and obtain emergency medical care promptly under the emergency procedure. Never use a hand to search for a pressurized leak.

Hazards also act through noise, vibration, heat, cold and physical contact. Noise exposure needs assessment and controls; hearing protection is one part of the required response. Vibration can affect the hands and arms or the whole body, depending on the equipment and exposure. Heat risk depends on workload, weather, clothing and other conditions; a plan may include controls, acclimatization, suitable breaks, hydration and emergency response. Cold and wet conditions can affect health, dexterity and traction. A calendar month alone does not establish a safe exposure.

Biological hazards may include bacteria, other microorganisms or viruses encountered in the actual work environment. Contaminated water, waste or sharps require the relevant assessment and procedure. Workers should report an unexpected condition and keep others from exposure while the responsible person arranges a response. Do not handle an unknown sharp or contaminated material as an improvised classroom demonstration.

Musculoskeletal injury (MSI) involves muscles, tendons, joints or related tissues. Force, repetition, posture, duration and work organization can contribute. A shovel's load, a rake's reach and a wheelbarrow route affect the demand on a person. A change in tool, load handling, layout or work organization can reduce that demand. An awkward movement may cause a strain or sprain; a crush, impact or fall can cause trauma. Reporting early discomfort or fatigue helps the crew address a developing problem.

Put the control before the exposure

WorkSafeBC 5.55 requires hazardous-substance exposure to be eliminated or controlled below harmful levels and applicable exposure limits using the specified control approaches. A substitute must have known hazards and reduce the risk. PPE as the primary control is permitted only under the conditions in the clause; it is not an automatic first choice. Effective controls often work together.

Worked example. A fictional crew proposes cutting near an occupied entrance. The observation “dust reaches the doorway” identifies a possible exposure route and affected people. It does not measure the substance or concentration. The next step is to prevent the uncontrolled exposure and have the responsible people assess the material, cutting method, controls and access arrangement. A useful record identifies the task and location, what was observed, who may be exposed, the interim response, the person responsible and what must be verified before work resumes. Simply handing out unspecified masks leaves critical questions unanswered.

7.4 · WHMIS and first aid are working systems

WHMIS connects hazard classification, labels, safety data sheets (SDSs), education and workplace training. Some classified hazards do not require a pictogram. The environmental symbol may appear on an SDS or label but is not mandatory under WHMIS. 7.5 · Rigging starts with qualified people and known information

Rigging connects and controls a load for lifting or handling using suitable equipment and arrangements. A sling connects the load to the lifting system. A shackle, hook or other fitting has an identified function, compatibility and working load limit. WorkSafeBC Part 15 is the starting regulatory reading for this task. Under 15.2, rigging and slinging must be done by or under the direct supervision of qualified workers familiar with the rigging and code of signals. The complete provisions address identification, design factors, WLL, operation, slings, components and inspection. Manufacturer instructions and the actual lift planning are also needed. A learner must be able to recognize when the information or qualification is missing.

Read the word, find the evidence, explain the limit

To practise a term, write the trade word, point to its feature or source statement, and explain the function in one plain sentence. Then say what the picture or statement cannot establish. Keep technical words intact while making the explanation easier to follow. For example: “The asphalt course is the surface layer shown here; this picture does not specify its required thickness.”

Record evidence without overstating it

For each selected activity, record: activity ID; date; task and document version; what you did; support used; result; check; feedback; revision; and next practice. Mark the observed skill. A group product needs an individual contribution record. An accurate calculation needs units and assumptions. A digital claim needs actual tool use. A future application is a plan until it has been observed.

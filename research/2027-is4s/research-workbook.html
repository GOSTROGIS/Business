Proposed teaching framework · 22 October 2026

The current nine names below are also explicitly recorded in the Numeracy Hub's brand guidance. The descriptions here are working teaching prompts, not quotations from or a validated assessment instrument issued by ESDC. Verification against the current official framework is queued in the source register.

Skill Ask the learner to… Look for this evidence
Reading Find and use an instruction, requirement or relevant detail. The detail is identified and correctly applied; the learner can point to its source.
Writing Make a record another person can use. A clear, accurate note, form or explanation with the necessary units, dates or context.
Numeracy Estimate, select a method, calculate or measure, and check. Working, labelled units, a result and a reasonableness check.
Digital Use a digital tool to find, organize, create or share task information. An accurately created/retrieved file, table or record; the learner explains a relevant tool choice.
Problem solving Identify a discrepancy, consider options and justify a response. A reasoned decision supported by task evidence rather than a guessed answer.
Communication Explain, listen, clarify and check shared understanding. A useful exchange and a check-back that reveals whether the message was understood.
Collaboration Agree roles, coordinate contributions and give useful feedback. Shared decisions and an identifiable contribution from each learner.
Adaptability Respond to a changed condition and revise the approach. A revised plan and an explanation of what changed and why.
Creativity and innovation Generate, try and improve a useful idea within the constraints. A proposed improvement, a small test or comparison, and reflection on the result.

Document literacy remains central: learners read drawings, interpret tables, complete forms and navigate records. Integrate these tasks across Reading, Writing, Numeracy and Digital. Continuous learning runs through practice, reflection and transfer; it is not simply a new name for Adaptability. See the full historical crosswalk.

A repeatable lesson pattern

Use the photographed lesson's instructor/learner/resource/time structure and the Hub's visible worked steps. A program can demonstrate all nine across a sequence; select two or three focus skills for detailed feedback in an individual short lesson.

Stage Instructor action Learner action Evidence
Connect Introduce a believable task and ask about relevant experience. Describe a familiar strategy or ask a question; personal disclosure is optional. Starting strategy and support preferences.
Read the task Supply a short document or clearly labelled diagram. Identify givens, constraints, units and the required output. Annotated task or explained interpretation.
Estimate and model Demonstrate one example, with method and tool steps visible. Predict an answer or range, then follow and question the method. Estimate and method choice.
Practise Offer a new example and purposeful roles. Solve, measure, record and check; explain to a partner. Task record, practical result and check-back.
Change one condition Introduce a stated variation. Revise the plan, compare options and test an improvement. Revised work and justification.
Reflect and transfer Ask what helped and where the approach applies next. Name the skill used and point to evidence; choose a next step. A short reflection tied to actual work.

Learner-sheet template: task → what you need → givens/diagram → estimate → method → worked example → your turn → check → changed condition → explain → next task.

Instructor-sheet template: purpose → provider outcome/source (pending until verified) → focus skills → materials → timed actions → anticipated misconceptions → worked answers → observation prompts → supports used → follow-up.

Feedback and access

Use simple task-specific observations: not observed yet, completed with support, completed independently, and explained or transferred to a new case. These are local feedback descriptors, not official Skills for Success levels, admission decisions or trade certification.

Record what support was used. Demonstration, a glossary, enlarged diagrams, assistive technology, extra practice and calculator access can make a task accessible. If Writing is the assessment target, an oral response can show understanding but does not by itself demonstrate Writing. Likewise, paper-based work can preserve the activity when a device is unavailable, but Digital remains unobserved until the learner uses a digital tool.

Ask learners how they prefer to participate. Use relevant community and workplace contexts with ACCESS/community input; do not invent shared Indigenous traditions or require disclosure of family, cultural or traumatic experiences. Offer collaborative improvement goals and private feedback. Use fictional records for practice involving students, clients, patients or incidents.

What the Numeracy Hub contributes

  • Keep dimensions, units, drawings and calculator steps close to the question.
  • Provide a worked example and a chance to try again with changed values.
  • Show why an answer is reasonable, including common unit or decimal errors.
  • Offer print-friendly work as well as digital practice.
  • Start with approachable tasks and increase the reasoning demand deliberately. Use “Start”, “Practise” and “Extend” for this draft; formal complexity labels need a validated mapping.
  • For later visual materials, carry forward warm cream, white task surfaces, red headings/actions and gold rules/highlights. Use the existing IS4S assets appropriately; do not add invented cultural motifs.

Technical and professional criteria come from the actual provider's current approved materials. Classroom arithmetic, communication practice and scenario work do not independently establish safe performance on equipment, permission to practise or qualification credit.

Teaching structure and interactions worth carrying forward

  • Start with On the job: why this action matters in this occupation.
  • Show a labelled diagram, form, table, schedule, or model, then number the steps.
  • Include a worked example with answer and units. Calculator key sequences are visible and can run on the selected emulator. A global control hides operations so learners can attempt the task first (header and controls).
  • Move into practice with new values. Guided practice gives the first steps and fades them across questions (exam settings).
  • Give a reasoned correction: wrong unit, decimal position, sign, or premature rounding (feedback implementation).
  • Revisit topics across days. The review exam uses missed topics and time since last practice; progress is local to that browser (topic record).
  • Provide printable blank and answer versions: the hub offers Word and PDF exports (exports).
  • Preserve accessibility choices: visible focus, reduced-motion handling, keyboard tabs, text-size controls and readable body text. Source presence is not a full accessibility audit.

The hub tags numeracy tasks Level 1–3 and separately offers Easy, Medium, Hard, Brutal and Nightmare settings (difficulty implementation). Do not present these local settings as validated proficiency levels for all nine skills. For the new program pack, a proposed progression of Supported practice → Independent practice → Transfer to a new situation is clearer and avoids importing the intimidating labels. Formal proficiency alignment requires a separate framework check.

Concrete examples that show the approach

  1. Ordering brackets: 3 units × 14 brackets + 6 spares = 48. Boxes hold 10, so 48 ÷ 10 = 4.8 and the order is 5 boxes. The explanation distinguishes rounding purchases up from rounding possible cut pieces down. This creates a natural nine-skills extension: read the job, calculate, record the order, enter a spreadsheet, explain the rounding, peer-check, handle a quantity change and compare an alternative. Existing example.
  2. Timesheets: 45 min ÷ 60 = 0.75 h. The lesson first explains clocks/elapsed time, then decimal hours and pay. It does not assume that a learner who reads decimals already understands that time is base 60. Useful for all 11 programs, particularly security, care and classroom schedules. Existing sequence.
  3. Proportion with a reasonableness check: 18 m of pipe takes 4 h, so a same-rate classroom model gives 45 m in 10 h. The text says to keep units aligned and check that more work takes more time. Do not treat this simplified model as a production or staffing promise. Existing practice.
  4. Documents as tools: the piping code-book section asks learners to locate a Division, read a reference number, find a table value, then do the math. This already combines reading, digital navigation and numeracy. It is an excellent pattern for security procedures, classroom plans or a simulated service manual, with authoritative content supplied for each occupation.

Proposed common program research structure

Use the same sequence for every program: Workplace purpose → Cohort facts and open questions → Authentic document/task → Worked example → Supported practice → Independent attempt → Change in the situation → Reflection and transfer → Nine-skill evidence → Sources and instructor checks.

Keep learner pages short and practical. Put admissions, credentials, purchasing questions and source-status notes in a staff research page. For each skill, name what the learner actually does and what the instructor can see or collect. A tag alone is not integration. Use two or three focus skills in a short lesson; make all nine observable over the module or sequence rather than forcing nine separate exercises into every activity.

Retain ACCESS/IS4S brand assets according to the brand-use notes. No repository-wide LICENSE or AGENTS.md was found in this checkout. Reusing the user's design direction does not establish redistribution rights for external code books, textbook passages, fonts or manufacturer images. Link or paraphrase with source attribution until the particular item's reuse terms are checked; original diagrams and activity wording can follow the existing style.

All quantities, schedules and records below are invented for learning. These are proposed preparation activities, not trade specifications, clinical guidance or credential assessments. Calculations use only the givens; real technical parameters and procedures come from the program's instructor. Each activity can be discussed aloud, drawn or completed with accessible calculation tools.

1. Welding — plan repeated blank lengths

  • Givens: A paper planning task needs four blanks. Each requires 125 mm of usable length plus a fictional 3 mm planning allowance per blank. Available stock length: 600 mm. This allowance is a supplied exercise value, not a cutting standard.
  • Learner prompt: How much total length should the plan allocate, and how much remains?
  • Worked answer: One blank: 125 mm + 3 mm = 128 mm. Four blanks: 4 × 128 mm = 512 mm. Remaining: 600 mm − 512 mm = 88 mm.
  • Estimate/check: Four usable lengths need 500 mm; four allowances add 12 mm, giving 512 mm. Check: 512 mm + 88 mm = 600 mm.
  • Common misconception: Adding the 3 mm allowance only once gives 503 mm; “per blank” requires four allowances.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Draw four labelled length bars. Stretch: six blanks require 768 mm, so the stated 600 mm is 168 mm short.
  • Next skill — Reading: Underline the words that tell you how often to apply the allowance, then explain your interpretation to a partner.

2. Asphalt Paving — convert depth before finding volume

  • Givens: A fictional rectangular classroom model represents an area 8 m long and 3 m wide, with a uniform depth of 50 mm. Ignore compaction, waste and all material properties for this exercise; 50 mm is not a recommended paving depth.
  • Learner prompt: What geometric volume does the model represent, in cubic metres?
  • Worked answer: Depth: 50 mm ÷ 1,000 = 0.05 m. Area: 8 m × 3 m = 24 m². Volume: 24 m² × 0.05 m = 1.2 m³.
  • Estimate/check: A depth of 0.05 m is one twentieth of a metre; 24 ÷ 20 = 1.2. Reverse check: 1.2 m³ ÷ 24 m² = 0.05 m.
  • Common misconception: Multiplying by 50 without converting mm to m mixes units and makes the answer 1,000 times too large.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Use a conversion strip showing 1,000 mm = 1 m. Stretch: at the same width and fictional depth, a 10 m length represents 1.5 m³.
  • Next skill — Digital: Build a three-input spreadsheet with visible units and explain how you would detect an incorrectly entered depth.

3. Ironworker — reconcile an inventory record

  • Givens: A fictional stores record lists 12 identical components, each with a supplied mass of 7.5 kg. These values are for inventory arithmetic only and provide no lifting or handling limit.
  • Learner prompt: What total component mass belongs in the inventory record?
  • Worked answer: 12 × 7.5 kg = (10 × 7.5 kg) + (2 × 7.5 kg) = 75 kg + 15 kg = 90 kg.
  • Estimate/check: Using 8 kg each gives 96 kg, so 90 kg is reasonable. Reverse check: 90 kg ÷ 12 = 7.5 kg each.
  • Common misconception: Treating 7.5 kg as 7 kg and 5 g; the 0.5 kg is 500 g.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Group the components into pairs, each totalling 15 kg. Stretch: if the count is corrected to 10, the total becomes 75 kg, a decrease of 15 kg.
  • Next skill — Collaboration: One partner checks the count and another checks the supplied unit mass; agree on the corrected inventory entry and record who checked each input.

4. Carpentry — find a rectangle's diagonal

  • Givens: A drawing shows a true rectangle with adjacent sides of 3 m and 4 m. The task is geometry practice; it does not establish construction tolerances or approval criteria.
  • Learner prompt: What is the distance between opposite corners?
  • Worked answer: The diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. d² = (3 m)² + (4 m)² = 9 m² + 16 m² = 25 m². Therefore d = √25 m² = 5 m.
  • Estimate/check: The diagonal must be longer than the 4 m side and shorter than the 7 m route along two sides. Also, 5² = 3² + 4².
  • Common misconception: Adding 3 m + 4 m finds the two-side route, not the direct diagonal.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Draw and label the right triangle inside the rectangle. Stretch: doubling both sides to 6 m and 8 m doubles the diagonal to 10 m.
  • Next skill — Communication: Explain why a 7 m answer represents a different path, using the drawing rather than only naming a formula.

5. Piping — combine straight segment lengths

6. Tower Crane — standardize a fictional load record

7. Marine Mechanical Technician — calculate elapsed workshop time

8. Security — read a shift schedule

9. Metal Fabrication — calculate material utilization on paper

  • Givens: A paper model of a rectangular sheet measures 120 cm × 80 cm. Six rectangles measuring 40 cm × 20 cm are placed on it in a three-column, two-row arrangement. Assume zero cutting loss for this paper exercise; these are not workshop cutting instructions.
  • Learner prompt: What area do the six rectangles occupy, and what percentage of the sheet is that?
  • Worked answer: Sheet area: 120 cm × 80 cm = 9,600 cm². One rectangle: 40 cm × 20 cm = 800 cm². Six rectangles: 6 × 800 cm² = 4,800 cm². Utilization: 4,800 ÷ 9,600 × 100% = 50%. Unused area: 4,800 cm².
  • Estimate/check: The layout occupies the full 120 cm width and 40 cm of the 80 cm height, exactly half the sheet. Used plus unused area: 4,800 + 4,800 = 9,600 cm².
  • Common misconception: Adding length and width finds neither area nor utilization; area requires multiplication and square units.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Draw the sheet as a 3 × 4 grid of equal rectangles and shade six cells. Stretch: nine cells use 7,200 cm², or 75%.
  • Next skill — Creativity and innovation: Draw another arrangement of the six rectangles, then explain how you know it fits and has the same occupied area.

10. Educational Assistant — interpret a visual activity schedule

  • Givens: An invented group activity schedule starts at 09:00: welcome 10 minutes, reading 15 minutes, making 20 minutes, break 15 minutes, in that order. It contains no real learner information and makes no recommendation about an individual's support needs.
  • Learner prompt: What fraction of the one-hour schedule is reading, and when does making begin?
  • Worked answer: Total: 10 + 15 + 20 + 15 = 60 minutes. Reading fraction: 15/60 = 1/4, or 25%. Welcome ends 09:10; reading ends 09:25, so making begins at 09:25. The full schedule ends at 10:00.
  • Estimate/check: Four 15-minute blocks fill an hour. Check the timeline: 09:25 + 20 minutes = 09:45, then + 15 minutes = 10:00.
  • Common misconception: Confusing an activity's duration with its clock start time, or using 15/100 because an hour is mistaken for 100 minutes.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Show four quarter-hour blocks beside the activity timeline. Stretch: transfer five minutes from making to reading; reading becomes 20/60 = 1/3 of the unchanged hour.
  • Next skill — Communication: Explain the schedule using both words and a visual sequence, then invite a partner to show when the next activity begins.

11. Nursing — total a synthetic intake practice record

  • Givens: A fictional arithmetic record lists 250 mL, 0.15 L and 100 mL, all marked as measured intake volumes in the exercise. There is no real patient, fluid target, medication or clinical decision. One litre equals 1,000 mL.
  • Learner prompt: Rewrite all entries in millilitres and find the total in millilitres and litres.
  • Worked answer: 0.15 L × 1,000 mL/L = 150 mL. Total: 250 mL + 150 mL + 100 mL = 500 mL. In litres: 500 mL ÷ 1,000 mL/L = 0.5 L.
  • Estimate/check: 250 mL is one quarter of a litre; 150 mL + 100 mL adds another quarter, making half a litre. Independent unit check: 0.25 L + 0.15 L + 0.10 L = 0.50 L.
  • Common misconception: Treating 0.15 L as 15 mL; it equals 150 mL. Do not infer clinical appropriateness from the total.
  • Scaffold / stretch: Use a 1 L diagram divided into ten 100 mL sections. Stretch: an additional fictional 0.20 L entry brings the total to 700 mL or 0.7 L.
  • Next skill — Digital: Enter the synthetic values into a practice table with separate value and unit fields; compare the result with the hand calculation before marking it checked.

Make, measure, improve

Paper-glider lab: an introduction to the nine skills

Back to the research pack

Proposed 60-minute pilot · Adult foundational learning · Instructor review before delivery

Purpose: Follow a visual instruction, collect useful measurements, compare results and explain an improvement. Connect the work to reading a job sheet, recording a measurement and sharing a change with a team.

Source connection: The three supplied ACCESS Essential Skills lesson images use a delta-wing paper-plane task, instructor/learner activity columns, pre-assessment, hands-on work and reflection. This is a new adaptation. The fourth page and the original folding handout were not supplied. The glider below is a simple classroom model, not a reconstruction of that missing handout or an aerodynamics lesson.

Focus for feedback: Reading, Numeracy and Communication. Collect brief evidence for the other six skills across the sequence. The learner's method and improvement matter more than distance travelled.

What you need

  • Two sheets of ordinary paper per pair, a ruler or tape, pencils and the record sheet below.
  • A clear, instructor-controlled testing lane with a marked start line and soft target zone. Use paper only; keep people out of the lane. Launch only on the facilitator's signal and retrieve together.
  • One accessible device per pair with a spreadsheet or simple table tool. A calculator is welcome.
  • A demonstrated model and enlarged numbered instructions. Offer a partner-assisted or facilitator-launched version so learners can direct, observe and measure without throwing.

If a device is unavailable, complete the record on paper and schedule the digital entry later. Mark Digital not observed yet, rather than treating a paper table as digital evidence. For a larger class, use several supervised lanes or extend the testing time.

Make the simple model

The facilitator demonstrates each step, then checks that learners can identify the next step in the numbered instructions.

  1. Place a rectangular sheet with a short edge at the top. Fold the left long edge to meet the right long edge. Crease, then unfold to show the centre line.
  2. Fold the two top corners toward the centre line so their top edges meet at the line, making a pointed top.
  3. Fold each new sloping outer edge toward the centre line to make a narrower point.
  4. Fold the model in half along the centre line, keeping the folded layers on the outside.
  5. Make one wing by folding a top sloping edge down toward the long bottom edge. Turn over and repeat on the other side.
  6. Open the wings gently so the body can be held underneath. Compare the two wings. Point out any difference before testing.

Use the first model to practise following and clarifying instructions. If a fold is difficult, demonstrate again or use a prepared model while the learner directs the steps. Test the activity locally before teaching; the outcomes are following, measuring and improving, not a promised flight performance.

Worked example — invented data

The practice target is 170–210 cm inclusive along the lane. Baseline trials are 120, 150 and 180 cm. Their total is 120 + 150 + 180 = 450 cm. The mean is 450 ÷ 3 = 150 cm, or 1.50 m. One of three trials is in the target zone.

After one change, fictional trials are 150, 180 and 210 cm. Their total is 540 cm; the mean is 540 ÷ 3 = 180 cm, or 1.80 m. Two of three trials are in the target zone. The mean increased by 30 cm.
